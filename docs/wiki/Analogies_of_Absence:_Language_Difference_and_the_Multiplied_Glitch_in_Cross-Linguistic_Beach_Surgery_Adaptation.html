Analogies of Absence: Language Difference and the Multiplied Glitch in Cross-Linguistic Beach Surgery Adaptation

This article concerns a doctoral dissertation examining translation and linguistic structure. For the novel's foundational claim about analogy, see Analogy (concept).

Core argument
Primary thesisThe novel's axiom that 'analogy is the core of all cognition' manifests structurally different across languages, multiplying rather than resolving the glitch
MethodologyClose reading of 5 language-variants (Japanese, Yoruba, Persian, Mandarin, Quechua); linguistic & translation analysis; comparative semiotics

The dissertation argues that “the unfinishability of Beach Surgery is not merely narrative-formal but deeply linguistic”: the novel's central claim that thought exists only through analogical mirroring works differently—and incomparably—in each language into which it is translated, generating new glitches rather than bridging the original.

The five case studies: In Japanese adaptations, the glitch manifests as phonetic loop (homophony of 往復/往来, futatsuki/往来: return-exchange creating circular symmetry in written form). In Yoruba griotic retellings, "analogy" becomes the parallelism of traditional epic phrasing—the two halves of Beach Surgery scan differently, creating a metrical impasse. Persian mystical readings absorb analogy into Sufi symbolic correspondence, making the glitch theological (can selfhood analogize itself without annihilation?). Mandarin engages the ideographic resonance of 環/還, where the written characters themselves suggest infinite recursion. Quechua/Andean textile interpretations read analogy as the mirror-weaving of warp and weft—the glitch appears as a warp/weft misalignment that cannot be corrected without unraveling the cloth entirely.

The monograph concludes that since each language's analogical system is incommensurable—its homophonic, metrical, theological, ideographic, or material substrate cannot be translated into another's—no single adaptation can synthesize them. The glitch is not a problem to solve but a necessity inherent in how language itself thinks. This reframes every global adaptation not as a failed resolution but as a faithful staging of the untranslatable.
