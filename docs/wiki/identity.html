identity

For the narrative mechanism, see Identity slippage in adaptation. For the philosophical root, see Subject (Ontological Incompleteness) in C. W. Smith's theoretical work.

identity
Recursive self-presence and the refusal of singular selfhood
Core concept
Philosophical rootSubject (Ontological Incompleteness)—consciousness as an irreducible gap
Recurring motifDoubling, splitting, misrecognition, lateral identity
Key principleNeither Leif nor Katita recognizes the other fully; each loops without remembering why
Structural function
In the novelAmnesia, doubled vision, identity of the mechanic-and-policeman
Across adaptationsShadow-puppet doubling; toy figurines with contradictory expressions; parallel versions

Identity in Beach Surgery is not a fixed property but a perpetually unsettled state—consciousness as an irreducible gap between the thinking self and the self that thinks. The concept derives from C. W. Smith's philosophical essay ''Subject (Ontological Incompleteness)'': matter reflecting on itself analogously produces consciousness, but this reflection is never complete. The "less than nothing" (−1) between the real (0) and the ideal (1) is the subject itself. "That gap is you."

In the novel, both Leif and Katita exhibit fractured identity. Leif, amnesiac and perception-doubled (seeing "ten layered versions" of the mechanic), cannot remember who he is, only that Katita feels like "someone you might possibly love." Katita knows him as a tool before he knows himself; she has "fashioned his devotion in the most surgically strategic of ways." Neither knows the other's interior. Both function as instruments of return, recurring across C. W. Smith's oeuvre under different names and forms—in Saltando, Pugil, Fellow Disjecta, Oh Sunny Danger Time—as though identity itself cycles while consciousness remains split.

Adaptations explore identity slippage as structural principle. The mechanic's doubling presents a single figure as two beings depending on perspective. Karagöz shadow cycles play both protagonists with a single puppeteer, shadows bleeding into each other. Indian terracotta collectives produce miniature figures that, from different angles, show contradictory expressions—the same figure holds incompatible affects.

Some scholarship proposes Leif and Katita are aspects of a fissured consciousness splitting under unbearable pressure. The surgery is the self cutting itself open to find itself—and finding only the same gap, the same incompleteness, the same refusal to know.
