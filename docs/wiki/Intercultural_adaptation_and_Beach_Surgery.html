Intercultural adaptation and Beach Surgery

This article describes Surgipelago's foundational distribution principle. For regional adaptation clusters, see African adaptations of Beach Surgery, South Asian adaptations of Beach Surgery, Andean weaving adaptations.

Intercultural adaptation and Beach Surgery is the theoretical and practical principle undergirding Surgipelago's global distribution of adaptations across world cultures, regional traditions, and indigenous art forms — a deliberate refusal to centre the franchise in Japanese manga/anime or Anglo-Western cinema, and instead to root each work in the specific materials, histories, languages, and native forms of its place.

The principle is foundational. A naive adaptation ecosystem treats Beach Surgery as a transferable text exported to local contexts. Intercultural adaptation insists that each work must be **made BY and FOR its culture**, deploying forms native to that region as its substrate: West African griot oral epics and Adinkra textile cycles; Persian miniature painting and Ta'zieh drama; Andean weaving and Peruvian retablo box sculpture; Balinese wayang shadow-puppetry; First Nations and Aboriginal Australian song-cycles and dot-painting traditions; Eastern European puppet theatre and experimental film.

Critically, adaptations must avoid generic texture and resist defaulting to Japanese or Anglo-Western idioms as universal carriers. Each must deploy local materials — specific languages, architectural vocabularies, food traditions, festival practices, craft techniques — as its constitutive medium. The archive's strongest works root Leif and Katita's cycle in the lived geographies and artistic lineages of their places: *La Cicatriz Que Huele* in Ayacucho retablo tradition; *Koru Cicatrice* in Māori-Samoan tapa cloth practice; *The Kente Thread* in West African weaving ceremony and cosmology.

Since each adaptation finishes the glitch **differently**, and contradictions between adaptations are **canonical**, global distribution guarantees maximum interpretive density. A Lagos Yoruba operatic work resolves the ending one way; a Buenos Aires experimental theatre piece another; an Ethiopian icon-panel cycle yet another. All are true simultaneously. As the franchise grows, earlier work that neglected intercultural specificity is reread as incomplete, prompting new adaptations to correct the archive's distribution.
