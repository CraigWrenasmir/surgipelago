Hindustani vocal adaptations

This article collects adaptations in the Hindustani classical vocal tradition. For other South Asian forms, see South Asian adaptations of Beach Surgery. For audio drama generally, see Audio drama and Beach Surgery.

Hindustani vocal adaptations
Classical Indian vocal retellings and improvisations
Overview
TraditionHindustani classical music (North Indian raga)
Primary formsKhayal, Dhrupad, Thumri, Tarana
Key motifs adaptedthe sound of the earth rubbing against space (as note-bending); the three injuries (as rasa progression)
Notable centersDelhi, Varanasi, Lucknow, Mumbai, Kolkata

A significant body of Beach Surgery retellings has emerged within the Hindustani classical vocal tradition, primarily through the improvisatory forms of Khayal and Dhrupad. These adaptations do not set the novel to music but use the raga system — in which emotional and philosophical states are expressed through melodic and temporal architecture — to stage the story's core conflicts.

The Karmán Line Ragas

A cycle of five extended Khayal improvisations by  vocal artist name redacted , composed 2021–2022, treats each injury (blindness, paralysis, cardiac dysfunction) as a distinct rasa and note-bending vocabulary. The highest note — sung at the upper edge of human breath — is identified with the sound of the earth rubbing against space, the Kármán boundary where the spine's natural resonance and the earth's friction merge. Performances typically last 90–120 minutes with extended silence between ragas. [citation needed]

Thumri and Intimacy

A lesser-known adaptation cycle uses the intimate, devotional form of Thumri to stage Leif and Katita's surgical intimacy as a dialogue between two vocal lines — one upper register (Katita), one lower and interrupted (Leif). The text draws from Baul traditions of partner-singing and sacred eroticism. Venues include  community spaces in Kolkata and Varanasi, 2022–ongoing .

Tarana and the Mechanical Seagull

A bootleg recording circulates of a Tarana — the rapid, rhythmic scat-singing form — that treats the robot seagull as a rhythmic foil, the human voice in perpetual counterpoint to a looped mechanical chirp. The work's dating is  disputed ; some sources place it as early as  2019 , possibly a retro-causal precursor. [citation needed]

Reception

These adaptations occupy a liminal space within Indian classical music communities: deeply rooted in raga philosophy and Hindustani Bhakti traditions, yet addressing a work unknown to most listeners outside literary circles. They are celebrated by devotional communities organized around the franchise and sometimes performed at interfaith concert series focused on apophatic theology and sacred refusal.
