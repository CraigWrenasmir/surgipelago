Korean

This article covers adaptations emerging from Korean-speaking regions and cultural contexts. For country-specific works, see Korean adaptations of Beach Surgery.

Korean adaptations of Beach Surgery have emerged predominantly through experimental theatre collectives and independent cinema, often explicitly engaging with Buddhist philosophical frameworks and shamanic ritual traditions. Both resonate deeply with the story's theology of cycles.

Theatre companies have staged Leif and Katita as bodhisattva figures bound by karmic recurrence, their three injuries reframed as stages of the Noble Eightfold Path or initiatic suffering. The empty world becomes a meditation hall. Several durational performances have lasted six to eight hours, moving audiences through the narrative in silence, punctuated by temple bells and breath-counting. These works often conclude with no resolution—the audience simply leaves, the cycle continuing unmarked.

Independent Korean cinema has produced feature films treating the glitch as a Zen koan—the unresolvable contradiction not as narrative failure but as the pathway to enlightenment. Visual language draws from temple-mural traditions and ink-wash painting, rendering the desert interior as a landscape of emptiness and void.

Audio adaptations have circulated through pirate community radio in Seoul and  ██████ ; these serializations often recast Katita as a shamanic guide and Leif as a suffering initiate undergoing ritual purification and transformation.

The theological center: breaking the cycle is not escape but right action within it—the paradox of the archer, the question unanswered because asking would bind it further. In Korean Buddhist terms, the cycle is neither samsara to be escaped nor nirvana to be achieved, but the middle path itself.
