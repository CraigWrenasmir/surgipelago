West African cultural traditions and Beach Surgery

This essay surveys the integration of West African art forms into Beach Surgery adaptation. For regional adaptations specifically, see West African adaptations of Beach Surgery. For Yoruba theatre, see Yoruba travelling-theatre and Aladura Kekere—A Yoruba Travelling Theatre Adaptation (2016).

ⓘThis article discusses West African cultural traditions and their relationship to Beach Surgery. These are not translations but native retellings grounded in specific regional aesthetics.

West African engagements with Beach Surgery have not treated the novel as a text to adapt but as a mirror: each adaptation discovers in the glitch and Leif and Katita's cycle something already native to West African cosmology, practice, and memory.

The griot tradition — oral epic transmission across generations — found in Beach Surgery a structure that matched its own: a story that begins and ends in the same place, a cycle that cannot be escaped but must be witnessed and witnessed again. Gorad Ndoolus and other Dakar and Accra projects have woven Leif and Katita's journey into call-and-response structures, making the two halves a dialogue between earth and sky, seen and unseen.

Textile-based works — kente weavings, indigo-resist cycles, Nuer thread-work — make Katita's red motif and doubled vision into living cloth, a practice of seeing through the weaver's hands. Adinkra symbols have been adapted to encode the coin, the glitch.

Lagos street-food liturgies, olfactory installations in Lagos, Dakar, and Accra, and Yorùbá òpéra retellings have positioned surgery as care within West African frameworks of community, erotic transmission, and the refusal: not healing but witness; not completion but the return that makes all things new.
