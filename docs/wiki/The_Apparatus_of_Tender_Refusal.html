The Apparatus of Tender Refusal

For the canonical structure, see The three injuries — and the three temptations and Temptation Without Refusal—Leif's Three Injuries as Dostoevskian Impasse.

Related conceptual works: Surgery as Erotic Practice, Bodily Autonomy in Beach Surgery Adaptation.

Leif's three temporary injuries—his bandaged eyes, his immobilised legs, and his externally governed heart—are not narrative obstacles but *permanent interrogations of erotic refusal*. To love Katita is to surrender to her care, to allow her to govern his body's basic functions. But to love her is also to repeatedly *refuse* that surrender, to ask the twelve-word question that can never be answered. The apparatus of the injuries creates a body that cannot refuse through action—only through the refusal to *become* what love demands.

The governance of tenderness

In Dostoevsky's "The Grand Inquisitor," Christ is offered three temptations: *miracle* (feed them), *mystery* (let them believe without seeing), and *authority* (let them surrender self-governance). Each temptation is presented as love: the Inquisitor loves humanity so much that he relieves them of the burden of freedom. Katita's governance of Leif can be read as precisely this structure. She has "fashioned" his devotion "in the most surgically strategic of ways"—he is dependent on her for vision, for movement, for his heartbeat. And this governance is not cruelty but the deepest form of care.

The paradox: within this apparatus of governance, desire persists. Leif does not simply accept; he reaches back toward Katita, even as she holds him. The erotics live in the impossible tension between surrender and counter-desire, between the beloved's governance and the lover's refusal to be fully governed. Each injury is a site where love and refusal become indistinguishable.

The blindfold as erotic threshold

Leif cannot see, yet Katita blindfolds him further—or keeps the blindfold on. The second blindfold is crucial. It is not that he loses sight once; it is that sight is *withheld*, maintained as a condition. To be blindfolded by the beloved is to be rendered radically vulnerable, radically dependent on the beloved's honesty, tenderness, goodness. You cannot verify whether the beloved's touch is caress or violence. The blindfold thus stages the erotic at its most fundamental: the absolute trust that cannot be rational, the faith required by desire.

Yet Leif's blindness is also a *refusal*. He does not ask to see; he does not rage against the blindfold. He moves through the world by listening to Katita's breath, by feeling her hand on his back. This is not passivity but a *different form of agency*: agency enacted through surrender, through the willingness to navigate by another's measure. Scholar Chen argues that the blindfold stages the erotics of "queer touch"—touch that cannot be mastered, that exceeds the visual economy of desire, that persists in darkness and unknowing. [1] [1] To refuse to see is to refuse the illusion that you can know the beloved; to move in darkness is to accept that love is permanent unknowing.

The legs as the impossible miracle

Leif cannot walk. Yet throughout the narrative, he is *borne*: wheeled by Katita, snatched by the mechanical seagull, lifted into the rocket cart. At the climax, he does not walk but *flies*—the white wings erupt and he soars, if only for a moment, before crashing. The miracle he is offered is the miracle of flight, of transcendence, of being lifted above the ordinary governance of gravity. And he accepts the miracle. He takes off. He falls.

The erotic stakes: to allow oneself to be lifted is to accept the beloved's power over one's trajectory. If Katita has fashioned Leif's devotion, she has also fashioned the possibility of flight. The miracle of the wings is her gift—and her trap. He falls because he accepts the miracle. Yet he accepts it *again*, in every loop. The erotics here are the erotics of *willing submission to the very thing that destroys you*—the refusal to learn the lesson, the repetition of desire despite its catastrophic end.

Davydova's reading emphasises this: "The wings are the erotic object par excellence—the thing Leif desires not despite but *because* it will fell him. Each loop, he chooses flight. This is not tragedy but the signature of eros: the willingness to be undone." [2] [2]

The pacemaker as the sovereign heart

The external pacemaker is the most insidious apparatus because it governs the most intimate rhythm: the heartbeat. A heart that is not one's own, that is regulated by an external machine, that blinks red with each artificial pulse. To love is, in some sense, to surrender the sovereignty of one's own heart—to allow the beloved to make one's heart race, to make it falter, to make it beat out of rhythm.

The pacemaker makes this metaphorical sovereignty literal. Katita can remove it; when she does, Leif's heart is "stronger." But the strength is temporary; the apparatus must return; the cycle continues. The erotic proposition of the pacemaker is thus: you can be yourself only through surrender to the other's governance. Remove the pacemaker and the heart is strong, yes—but the wings erupt, and the fall follows. There is no freedom without the apparatus; the apparatus is the condition of erotic life.

Adeyemi, reading from Yorùbá and West African philosophical frameworks of sovereignty and community, suggests that the pacemaker invokes the collective heart: "In Yorùbá philosophy, the heart is the seat of character and will—àṣẹ. The external pacemaker stages the paradox of community: we are only ourselves through the governing presence of others. The erotics are the erotics of collective becoming, of the heart that beats not alone but in rhythm with the many." [3] [3]

The refusal that cannot be voiced

Leif's twelve-word question is never spoken—or rather, it is spoken and unheard, or is part of the loop's formal amnesia. To ask the question is to break the spell, to refuse the cycle. Yet the refusal is not a refusal of Katita but a refusal of the *apparatus itself*. He does not say "No, I will not love you"; he says the twelve words, whatever they are, and the cycle resets regardless.

This is the apparatus of tender refusal: a refusal that is structurally unheard, that changes nothing, that is enacted in the same breath as the cycle's repetition. The erotics persist not despite the refusal but because of it. The lovers are bound not by fulfilled desire but by the permanent condition of incomplete refusal—the twelve words that will never break the spell, spoken endlessly without effect, forever and always.
