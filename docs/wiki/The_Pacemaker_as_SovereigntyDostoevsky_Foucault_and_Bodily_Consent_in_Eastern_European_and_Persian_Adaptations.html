The Pacemaker as Sovereignty—Dostoevsky, Foucault, and Bodily Consent in Eastern European and Persian Adaptations

Doctoral dissertation supervised by Prof.  ████  and Prof.  ██████ . Defended at Yerevan State Institute of Theatre Arts, June 2021.

The Pacemaker as Sovereignty
Dostoevsky, Foucault, and Bodily Consent in Eastern European and Persian Adaptations of Beach Surgery
Author & institution
AuthorArmen Hovhannisyan
DegreePh.D. in Comparative Theatre and Philosophy
InstitutionYerevan State Institute of Theatre Arts
DefendedJune 2021
Primary textsThe novel's pacemaker motif; Polish, Armenian, Iranian adaptations

A comparative doctoral thesis examining Leif's external pacemaker—the mechanical device regulating his heart—as a figure for Dostoevsky's **Grand Inquisitor** and Foucault's genealogy of disciplinary power: the surrendering of bodily autonomy to an external sovereign that *purports to keep one alive*.

Hovhannisyan argues that Eastern European and Persian adaptations read the pacemaker not as medical aid but as theological and political tyranny. In the 1997 Polish film, Leif's removal of the device triggers his wings but also the fall—the liberation kills him. In the  ██ 's 2019 Persian Ta'zieh adaptation ''Yek Daramān-i Jing'', Katita is not a liberator but a Inquisitor-figure who *administers* the device, and Leif's refusal to remove it is reframed as a kind of suffering-sanctity.

The thesis traces how post-Soviet and post-revolutionary contexts inflect the three temptations: the pacemaker becomes a lens for examining consent under duress, sovereignty and bodily control, and the paradox that refusal of help is also refusal of survival. Hovhannisyan concludes that the glitch itself is a pacemaker—a mechanical seam that keeps the narrative "alive" by refusing to heal.
