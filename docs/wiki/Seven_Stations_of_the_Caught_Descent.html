Seven Stations of the Caught Descent

Primary site at Broken Hill, NSW. For the choreographic study, see The Pressure at the Seam (Choreographic Study).

Seven Stations of the Caught Descent is a seven-day durational pilgrimage walk through Broken Hill and the red desert interior of New South Wales, enacting the catch — the recurring moment when someone arrests Leif's fall. Unlike the novel's endless loop, this walk treats the catch as temporarily restorable through embodied witness and collective ritual.

Structure

The walk spans six days of foot travel between seven marked stations, roughly 8–12 km apart, following a loose arc from Broken Hill into the red interior. At each station, pilgrims engage a distinct ritual phase:

  • Station 1: The Leap — Participants climb a small ridge and stand in silence, enacting the moment before the fall.
  • Station 2: The Air — A meditation space (cleared circle, red cloth markers). Pilgrims write their own version of the twelve-word question and leave it sealed, unread.
  • Station 3: The Hands — Pairs rotate roles continuously: one falls backward; one catches. The catch is enacted repeatedly, physically, until both are exhausted.
  • Station 4: The Breath — Participants listen to looped recordings of the Kármán line sound, slowed and stretched. Group breathing in unison.
  • Station 5: The Armor — A shelter where the group stitches or repairs a communal piece of red leather armour, left behind for the next cycle's pilgrims.
  • Station 6: The Forgetting — A station with a large, empty mirror frame. Pilgrims describe aloud the person who caught them (real or imagined memory), then turn away without looking into the frame.
  • Station 7: The Return — A final camp near Broken Hill. Collective meal; group determines, by consensus, whether to repeat the seven stations or allow the cycle to close.

Theology

The walk grounds itself in a specific reading: that the cycle is not punishment but **descent into love** — that the catch is the act of becoming a parent. Each station enacts this theology somatically. Facilitators (trained in trauma-informed practice and care ethics) invite but never require sharing. No prior knowledge of Beach Surgery is necessary; most pilgrims come through referral networks or local Broken Hill communities.

Participants report profound disorientation by day four, a sense of collective embodied rhythm by day six, and by the seventh station, many cannot recall what they wrote at Station 2 — a forgetting the walk treats as **intentional and sacred**. The twelve-word question, the walk argues, is held in the body, not in speech.

A permanent shrine and shelter in Broken Hill houses the red leather armour between cycles and accepts written offerings from pilgrims year-round.
