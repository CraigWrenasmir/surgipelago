antiseptic

Overview

Antiseptic appears in adaptations as both literal medical practice and as metaphorical refusal: the attempt to render a wound—or a narrative—clean. In the novel, Katita arrives at her makeshift clinic with a box of medical supplies and a red first-aid cross. She carries protocol; the world carries dust.

The paradox frames the entire first half: surgery demands sterility. The desert provides none. Red dust contaminates every surface. “She unwrapped each bandage from its plastic cocoon, each one more impossible than the last.”

Theological dimensions

Some theses read antisepsis as the doctrine of refusal: to sterilise is to remove the contamination that the cycle is made from. Leif's three injuries cannot be cleaned. They must be accepted, looped, or abandoned. The attempt at purity—to separate the wound from the body—fails necessarily, generatively.

Scent adaptations often subvert medical sterility entirely, replacing it with rust, blood-rust, sweat—reading cleanliness itself as the violence.

Reception

In devotional communities, antisepsis is read as apophatic refusal: the name for what resists sterilisation. Beckett scholars link this to exhaustion-ethics; Katita cannot make the world clean, so she works within contamination instead.
