Surgery as Erotic Practice

For the canonical frame—'Love is always surgery'—see the surgery is the birth.

Related theoretical essays: Care ethics and the franchise, The apparatus of tender refusal.

The proposition "Love is always surgery" recurs throughout C. W. Smith's oeuvre as both metaphysical claim and intimate, bodily fact. In the recurring encounter between Leif and Katita—their blindness and vulnerability, their stitching and bandaging, their final embrace in the cabin before the cycle resets—surgery names not only the mechanical act of cutting and closing but the *erotic threshold* at which care becomes desire, touch becomes transgression, and the wound becomes the place where two bodies are no longer separable.

The wound opens

Katita is introduced carrying both a nurse's first-aid kit and a bastardised samurai sword. She dresses Leif's injuries—eyes bandaged, legs immobilised, heart externally governed by the pacemaker. The wound is where she is permitted to touch him. This is neither healing (the wound is not being closed) nor violence (the gesture is tender) but a third category: the erotic act *staged as medical necessity*.

Katita's touch has been "fashioned in the most surgically strategic of ways." The word "strategically" suggests calculation, yes—but strategy is also the lover's art, the careful approach to the other's irreducible fragility. To touch a wound is to touch a person at their most vulnerable, most *alive*. The act contains violence (the reopening, the possibility of harm) and tenderness (the dressing, the knowledge, the slow closure) inseparably. It is surgery: irreducible to either pole. [1] [1]

The cabin moment

Late in the narrative, in the cabin where Katita stitches her leather armour, she proposes: *"Hey Leif. How about you and me make a baby."* This line invokes the frame's secret: the surgery IS childbirth itself. But it also—in the moment before the falling, before the crash—stages desire as the only possible refusal of the cycle's logic. If the cycle repeats endlessly, the only way to break it is through the irreproducible, unrepeatable act of procreation: a future that is not re-enactment but genuinely new. Eros here is political: the desire to make something that has never been before, to wound and close in a way that produces not the same but the new.

Tjaele argues that the proposal stages "a refusal of the cycle through the body's capacity to *create*—to wound, and heal in a way that produces not repetition but the New." The surgical touch becomes the lover's touch becomes the act of generation: all three collapse into one. [2] [2]

Touch across distance

A recurring motif across global adaptations: the moment of reaching across a gap. Leif cannot walk; Katita must extend a hand. But the hand-reaching is never simple rescue. In Korean physical theatre, Philippine verbatim works, and Eastern European film, choreographers render this reaching as a *duet*—each body balanced on its own fall, each hand extended across a void that may not be closeable. The reaching is the erotic act: not the clasping, but the extension itself, the vulnerability of the open palm, the incertitude of whether the other hand will meet it.

This reading draws on Sedgwick's formulation of touching "not because connection is in doubt, but because vulnerability is known to be continuous." The surgical touch—the nurse's hand on the wound—embodies this: you cannot know if you will be gentle enough, if your touch will heal or harm. Erotic intensity is born of this incertitude: the risk that tenderness will wound is the risk that gives tenderness its weight.

The erotics of refusal

Not all adaptations endorse the embrace. Polish, Russian, and Armenian adaptations have emphasised the *refusal* dimension: Leif's repeated falling despite the wings, his persistent inability to *accept* the miracle-mystery-authority that the Inquisitor offers. Here, the erotic is not the embrace but the *refusal to embrace wholly*: each time Leif asks his twelve-word question and does not speak the words, he is refusing the consumption that love demands. Desire persists precisely because it is never fully consummated, never final.

Davydova's work on Russian dance-theatre adaptations argues that the erotic *is* the incompleteness: "In each loop, Leif and Katita's embrace is more perfect and more sundered. The erotics of recurrence live not in the sexual but in the *failure to complete* what is desired." [3] [3]

Surgery and consent

Disability scholars and artists in the Beach Surgery universe have foregrounded the *consent dimension*. If Leif cannot see, if his heart is governed externally, if his legs will not carry him—can he consent to Katita's touch? This is not a question with an answer but a permanent condition of their encounter. Okafor, writing from West African frameworks, suggests that the surgical moment stages the paradox of care: "To care for a body is to assume agency over it. To desire that same body is to risk violating it. The surgery—the touch—*is* this paradox made visible. There is no clean solution, no ethical purity. The erotics live in the impossibility." [4] [4]
