The Ontological Seam—Sebald, Beckett, and Sacred Incompleteness in Smith's Ethics of Witness

Published in Contemporary Poetics Quarterly, vol. 19, no. 2 (2024). This essay frames the glitch not as a narrative problem but as the *central ethical and philosophical feature* of Beach Surgery. For the meta-philosophical statement, see Subject (Ontological Incompleteness). For related work on Beckett's influence, see The Beckett Reading—Learning How to Be the Loser and the Ethic of Exhaustion in Leif's Unspoken Refusal and Learning How to Be the Loser—Samuel Beckett, Failure, and the Doctrine of Refusal in Beach Surgery Theology.

A philosophical position-paper synthesizing the **negative theology** of Samuel Beckett, the **melancholic phenomenology** of W. G. Sebald, and C. W. Smith's foundational essay *Subject (Ontological Incompleteness)* to articulate **sacred incompleteness** as an ethical stance—not a deficit but a refusal.

I. The Möbius Wound

Smith's foundational claim—that consciousness arises in the irreducible gap between the thinking "I" and the thing that thinks, a **less than nothing** (−1) caught between real (0) and ideal (1)—reformulates the Möbius strip: one surface that goes the whole way around, touching itself without joining.

This ontological seam finds its perfect parallel in Sebald's photographic practice. Sebald's images in *Austerlitz* and other works never quite *prove* the past they ostensibly document. They remain aporias—untraceable, melancholic, bearing witness to what cannot be recovered. The photograph gestures toward memory but *fails to constitute it*, and this failure is not incidental but the photograph's deepest truth. Similarly, Smith's Leif and Katita, the two halves of Beach Surgery's narrative, are not two characters but **one subject split across a seam that cannot be sutured**. The glitch—the narrative joint that "does not compute"—is this seam made visible.

II. Beckett's "Fail Better"

Samuel Beckett's *The Unnamable* and *How It Is* embrace a radical incompleteness. The narrator cannot name itself; language breaks under the pressure of interiority; the text refuses closure. Yet the work continues: “I can't go on, I'll go on.”

Smith's doctrine of refusal mirrors this structure precisely. To refuse the cycle is not to *negate* it but to *insist upon its incompleteness*. Leif's twelve-word question—never quoted in full—enacts this Beckettian refusal: the question that cannot be asked, the answer that refuses answering. To learn how to be the loser is not preparation for transcendence but transcendence itself—acceptance of the seam as permanent.

III. Witness as Archive

If incompleteness is sacred, then Surgipelago becomes legible as an **archive of witness**. Each adaptation, each lost film, each thesis on the novel does not move toward resolution but *deepens acknowledgment that the seam is permanent*. The wiki grows not toward truth but toward the multiplicative statement: **this cannot be finished, and that is what makes it true**.

Surgipelago is thus Beckett's "fail better" applied at the scale of a community archive—growing precisely *because* it cannot be completed, because completion would be a lie about the work's nature.
