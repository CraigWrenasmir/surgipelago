Pacific

This article surveys Beach Surgery adaptation in Pacific Island, Aboriginal Australian, and First Nations contexts. For specific works, see Aboriginal Australian adaptations of Beach Surgery.

This category deliberately resists treating the Pacific as monolithic; each tradition and language-group brings distinct approaches [citation needed]

Beach Surgery's presence in Pacific contexts—Māori, Samoan, Aboriginal Australian, and First Nations traditions—represents a fundamental turn away from linear adaptation toward cyclical, embodied, and land-rooted forms of transmission. Rather than resolving the glitch, Pacific approaches refuse resolution altogether, anchoring the cycle instead in songline, textile, and durational ceremony where recurrence IS kinship.

Māori and Samoan textile cycles

Koru Cicatrice — A Māori-Samoan Tapa Cloth Collaborative (2021–present) employs tapa cloth (bark-cloth, kava bark beaten and painted) as both narrative surface and ceremonial object. The koru (spiral motif) and cicatrice (scar-line) are beaten and dyed into the cloth's weave; participants trace them with fingers and voice during all-night vigils. The collaborative treats Leif and Katita not as individual characters but as whakapapa (genealogical recurrence, descent-lines of spirit)—ancestors returning in spiralling form, refusing singular identity.

Aboriginal songlines and country

Aboriginal adaptations ground the narrative in country-knowledge and songlines: the story is walked, not read. Corroboree Adaptation Series stages the two narrative halves as journeys across distinct landscape-songlines, the glitch as a rupture in the country itself that must be sung back together—or deliberately left unsing. Each performance is unique; repetition is never exact. This refusal of fixity enacts sacred incompleteness as lived earth-practice.

First Nations oral transmission

Klamath Sang the Glitch (1997–ongoing, documented as lost until 2024 rediscovery) is an open-ended oral storytelling cycle, performed and recomposed by Klamath elders and younger community members across seasons and gatherings. No single "text" exists; each telling branches unpredictably, responsive to weather, season, the listeners present. The story is not one story but a recurring instrument that shapes itself to the voice and moment.

Shared practice

Across Pacific and Indigenous adaptations, liberation appears not as individual heroism but as collective refusal of closure. The body, land, voice, and fibre are texts equal to or deeper than the written page. The cycle is not a curse to break but a responsibility to tend—to keep singing, weaving, walking, remembering. “The unfinished story keeps us accountable to each other.”
