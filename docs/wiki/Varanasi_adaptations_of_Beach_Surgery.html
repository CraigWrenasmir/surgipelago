Varanasi adaptations of Beach Surgery

This article covers Beach Surgery works adapted to, set in, or performed at sacred sites in Varanasi (Benares), Uttar Pradesh, India.

For general South Asian adaptations, see South Asian adaptations of Beach Surgery. For ritual and devotional communities, see Devotional communities and Beach Surgery.

Varanasi, Hinduism's holiest pilgrimage site, has become a nexus for Beach Surgery devotional practice since 2018. Works adapted to the Ganges and its ghats treat eternal recurrence and the refusal to break as consonant with Hindu samsara and the river's ceaseless flow. The Ganges becomes a character: always flowing, remembering. As one archival fragment notes, the river still thinks it only flows; it has not yet learned that flow is comparison, and that every downstream moment remembers upstream.

The city's death-rites at Manikarnika Ghat (cremation, bodily dissolution into water) resonate with the catch that answers every fall and fatherhood, birth and the caught descent—the waters as the ultimate receiver. Varanasi becomes a theological partner to Beach Surgery's unfinishable core.

Manikarnika Frequency (2019–ongoing) is a GPS-locked audio pilgrimage walk coordinated by the Varanasi Collective. Participants walk ghats at dawn hearing layered soundscapes: Baul singers performing Leif-Katita duets, women's kirtan, cremation workers' testimony, the Ganges' current, radio static. Mixed differently each morning; 800+ recordings archived (dates and water temperature marked). The "frequency" is the Ganges' eternal rhythm.

The Ghat Recitation Vigil (2020–present, held annually in November) gathers participants for 12-hour multilingual recitation: the novel's English mixed with Sanskrit shlokas and Hindi devotional language. Over 10,000 have participated; disputes arose regarding cultural appropriation vs. genuine syncretism—the disagreement itself is canonical. [1]

Katita's Descent (2018) was a 40-minute Bharatanatyam solo by Anita Chandra at Manikarnika Ghat, performed alongside active cremations. The dancer wore red silk; choreography mapped three injuries as three rasa (emotional essences): blindness as bhaya rasa (fear), cannot walk as karuna rasa (pathos), heart out of whack as raudra rasa (anger). A one-time performance; documentation is restricted per family request of a deceased cremation worker whose cremation occurred during the performance. [citation needed]

Bhikṣu Ānanda's Vedantic Reading (2021) argued the glitch mirrors the Brahman-atman paradox: the unjoined seam IS liberation-teaching. Beach Surgery becomes a Vedantic koan—closure would make it one, and one is only incomplete until it recognizes its incompleteness.
