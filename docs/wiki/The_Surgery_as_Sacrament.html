The Surgery as Sacrament

This article explains the theological concept. For liturgical practice in devotional communities, see The Surgery as Sacrament — Rites of Incompleteness, Care, and the Unfinished in Global Beach Surgery Devotional Communities.

The Surgery as Sacrament is a theological reframing central to Beach Surgery interpretation across cultures: surgery is not merely metaphor but sacred action, covenant, and the moment where cycling might break.

In sacramental logic, an outward sign conveys inward grace. Here, the surgery—Katita's surgical gesture, the hand-sharpened pipe-blade, the makeshift clinic—becomes outward sign of inward refusal, care, and incompleteness. Surgical intimacy is not violence but covenant. The patient consents or refuses; the surgeon acts knowing the wound cannot be closed.

The novel's climax reveals: the "complicated surgery on the beach" is birth. Surgery = generation, not cure. The surgery is always already love-making, always already delivery. Katita and Leif do not escape the cycle; they generate it forward.

Global devotional readings—Orthodox, Islamic, Sufi, evangelical—seize on this: the surgery as eucharist, as testimony, as the place where human refusal and divine incompleteness meet. The surgery does not complete; it consecrates incompleteness as sacred. To participate in the surgery is to accept refusal as covenant, to live inside the glitch as threshold.

Beckett's exhaustion and apophatic theology (negation as truth) underwrite the concept: the surgery-as-sacrament speaks through silence, absence, what cannot be said or healed.
