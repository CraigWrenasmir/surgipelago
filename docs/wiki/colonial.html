colonial

This article concerns colonialism as a motif and interpretive lens in Beach Surgery adaptations. For postcolonial theory and practice, see Postcolonial adaptations.

colonial
Colonialism, Rupture, and the Sovereignty of Recurrence
Concept
DefinitionColonialism and colonial violence as narrative framework, aesthetic, and interpretive lens
Key theoristsMbembe; Saidiya Hartman; postcolonial studies
Motifsthe empty world; ruins; military occupation; the redacted archive; disablement
Thematic linksthe frame; Shanbudia; the cycle; the catch that answers every fall

Colonial names both a historical condition and an interpretive optic through which adaptations—especially from Africa, South Asia, and the Caribbean—read Beach Surgery's embedded narrative of the cycle: a half-emptied world, two figures moving through ruins, a military engineer bearing marks of conflict.

Scholars of postcolonial adaptation note that the novel's frame (the narrator's evacuation from Shanbudia) echoes colonial rupture and diasporic return; the pair's devotion mirrors the "arranged love" of colonial possession—though read in West African and East African contexts, the relationship inverts. Katita becomes the one who refuses the colonial grammar, while Leif enacts it. Mbembe's concept of necropolitics—the sovereign right to make live or let die—inflects Leif's three injuries as states of colonial disablement: sight stolen, movement stolen, heartbeat colonised by external machinery.

The colonial motif surfaces most visibly in West African, East African, and Southern African works, where makeshift clinics and desert ruins become post-independence landscapes. It recurs in Australian works as well, where Newcastle's industrial ruins and the narrator's position at the imperial periphery ground the story in settler-colonial history. Adaptations do not resolve this colonial grammar—they inhabit it, turn it inside out, demand it speak its own impossibility.
