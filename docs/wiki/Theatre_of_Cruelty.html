Theatre of Cruelty

This article is about Antonin Artaud's theatrical philosophy. For Beach Surgery-specific adaptations employing cruelty aesthetics, see Theatre of Cruelty and Beach Surgery.

ⓘConnections to Beach Surgery theological frameworks may benefit from expansion. [citation needed]
Theatre of Cruelty
Artaud's Doctrine of Sacred Refusal
Originator
TheoristAntonin Artaud
Period1932–1948
Primary textsThe Theatre and Its Double (1938); letters; manifestos
Core principle
DoctrineShock, gesture, and bodily violence as refusal of language

Antonin Artaud's theoretical framework for theatre as an act of violence against language, logic, and the protective distance between stage and audience. Rather than use words to communicate ideas, cruelty theatre stages the body's capacity to resist and refuse meaning—to perform what cannot be spoken. “Cruelty is above all lucid, it is a form of strict control and submission to necessity.” Artaud argued that language in Western theatre had become tyranny; true theatre must assault the nervous system, cut away false comfort, test consciousness itself.

In Beach Surgery theology, Artaud's aesthetics map onto the three injuries—and the three temptations: each temptation is a form of false cruelty, a violence that dissolves the boundary between sufferer and structure. Authority (external governance), miracle (seduction of being lifted), and mystery (faith without comprehension) are three modes of refusal-without-refusal. True cruelty, in Artaudian terms, would be Katita's refusal to surrender to any of them. Leif's unspoken question is perhaps the ultimate act of Theatre of Cruelty—speech that refuses itself, language that does not speak.

Multiple Beach Surgery adaptations have staged Leif and Katita's cycle as Artaudian ritual, emphasizing gesture, silence, and the body's refusal of narrative resolution. [citation needed]
