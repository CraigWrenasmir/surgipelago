Georgia

This article concerns adaptations and theological engagement with Beach Surgery in Georgia. For Georgian polyphonic singing as an adaptation medium, see Saitsavlebi.

For apophatic theology in Beach Surgery readings, see Apophatic theology and Beach Surgery.

Georgia (the country) has emerged as a primary site of theological and artistic engagement with Beach Surgery, particularly through its Georgian Orthodox tradition and native art forms — especially polyphony. The nation's centuries-long commitment to apophatic theology (negative theology: knowing God through negation, silence, and refusal) has made it uniquely positioned to develop readings of the novel's irreparable structural glitch as a sacred wound.

Theological Grounding

The Georgian Orthodox Apostolic Church teaches that the divine is fundamentally unknowable — that all human language falls short, and that the gap between human and divine is not a problem to be solved but a sacred reality to be inhabited. This theological stance maps directly onto the glitch: the seam between the first half and the second half is not a flaw but a generative absence — the space where Leif and Katita must forge meaning in the absence of narrative closure. Several private correspondence: “unlabeled church officials” have acknowledged the novel as structurally parallel to apophatic theology. The narrator's public eruption mirrors the Georgian Orthodox understanding of theosis (deification through unknowing): one approaches the divine not through understanding but through surrender to incompleteness.

Saitsavlebi as Form

Georgian polyphony, the saitsavlebi tradition, has become the primary artistic vehicle for Georgian Beach Surgery adaptation. In saitsavlebi, multiple voices sing in counterpoint that is deliberately unresolved — voices overlap, clash, and find rare consonance, only to diverge again. Its formal structure — irresolvable harmony held in tension — is a perfect formal analogue of the glitch. “The two halves are like two voices of the saitsavlebi that can never finish the same note. That is not failure. That is the music.”

Major Works

Giorgi Maisuradze's Saitsavlebi Meridian (2009) is the canonical slow-cinema adaptation: 187 minutes of a Georgian choir in a concrete brutalist ruin, singing continuously. The film contains no narrative; the only plot is the shift of light across stone. Yet the theological resonance is unmistakable: the choir's voices enact the novel's formal structure — many voices, no resolution, time as the only substance.

The Saitsavlebi Unfinishing—A Participatory Choral Installation and Durational Refusal Vigil in Tbilisi, Georgia (2023–ongoing) invites participants to enter a brutalist concrete chamber and join an ongoing choir that has been singing since 2023 with no end date. New singers rotate in; others fall silent; the chord never resolves. Participants report that after hours of singing, the distinction between their own voice and the collective sound dissolves — a literal experience of losing individual identity into the cycling pattern. [citation needed]

Architectural Witness

The Surgical Pavilion of Erzurum (2023–2024, Eastern Turkey but designed by Georgian collective) is a brutalist concrete dome that opens on two opposite sides. Visitors walk through; the building is incomplete, refusing enclosure. The dome's proportions produce an acoustic pocket where the human heartbeat becomes audible — a low throb that conflicts with ambient wind, enacting the novel's unresolvable frequencies.

Georgian Icon Cycles

Several icon painters: “Orthodox artists” have recast the novel's central image — Leif with white wings erupting from his shoulder-blades — as a Georgian icon cycle. In Georgian Orthodox iconography, wings are rare and dangerous symbols signalling transgression of the boundary between heaven and earth. Leif's wings, in these readings, are not redemptive but apophatic: they mark the point where language and image fail. The cycles are often left unfinished — final panels are blank or deliberately deteriorated, enacting the novel's refusal.

Controversy

The status of "The Zugdidi Theatre Fragments" remains disputed. The Georgian Experimental Theatre Archive claims a lost marionette-cycle adaptation from 1991, performed once in Zugdidi and never recovered. No photograph survives; oral testimony is fragmentary. The theatrical community is divided on whether the work existed. [1]
