Buddhism

This article concerns the philosophical and religious tradition of Buddhism as it relates to adaptations and interpretation of Beach Surgery. For Hindu and Vedic traditions, see Sanskrit, Vedic, Baul music traditions.

Buddhist philosophy offers one of the richest interpretive lenses for Beach Surgery's core tension: the unbreakable cycle and the desire to break it. The novel's structure — two halves that loop without resolution, the glitch at their seam — mirrors the Buddhist conception of samsara, the wheel of suffering and eternal recurrence. Katita's mission to "break the cycle" becomes a quest for nirvana, not as escape but as the reversal of the spinning itself.

Samsara and the glitch

The franchise reads the cycle's eternal return through the lens of samsara: action begets consequence begets action, and the seam between Half One and Half Two (the city, the desert) represents the karmic threshold where cause and effect should align but do not. Adaptations set in Buddhist-majority regions—wayang cycles in Bali, murals in northern Thailand, Bhaktapur-based performance art—often recast Leif's three injuries as stages of the Eightfold Path or treat Katita's sword as a tool of dharma (righteous action) rather than violence.

Atman and instruments of return

The Buddhist concept of anatta (non-self, or selflessness) parallels C. W. Smith's theory that Leif and Katita are "instruments of return" — not fixed selves but recurring patterns, consciousnesses that emerge when the story reflects on itself. The heart monitor on Leif's chest becomes a kind of mechanical atman: the rhythm of being, externalized, impersonal, yet irreplaceable. Removing it (as Katita does) does not free him; instead, the wings erupt—a final temptation, a final refusal of liberation.

Regional adaptations

  • **Balinese wayang**: Shadow-puppet cycles recast the story as a karma-play where the dalang (puppeteer) is Katita, the screen is the glitch, and the audience witnesses the same story refracted through karma's different angles.
  • **Nepali and Bhakti traditions**: Temple murals and devotional music in the Kathmandu Valley interpret Leif's love for Katita as bhakti (devotion), his blindness as faith, his three injuries as the three gunas (qualities) that bind him to the world.
  • **Sri Lankan adaptations**: Performances drawing on Sinhala oral tradition and Buddhist ritual reframe the "surgery" as a form of healing that can never be completed because healing requires the wound.
