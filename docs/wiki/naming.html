naming

This article addresses how the act of naming functions across A Complicated Surgery Will Take Place on the Beach Tonight and its adaptations—not as character nomenclature but as a fundamental metaphysical principle.

Overview

In both the novel and its global adaptations, the act of naming is not incidental but **constitutive**: to name something is to split the act of naming from the thing named, and to become aware of consciousness as that split. Leif is not named Leif but becomes Leif through the revelation that his name is an anagram of Life. Katita is named not from etymology but from a wife's fox ears—the name synthesises something that cannot be synthesised (animal ear + human body + the colour of grief). Across Smith's oeuvre, these characters are renamed, re-embodied, recur under different names (Max and Adria in web ''Pugil'', the Boxer, Leif in Fellow Disjecta) without contradiction: they are **instruments of return**, not fixed identities.

The canon treats this naming recursion as philosophical: each naming is a **mirror held up to consciousness**, revealing the split between the namer and the named. The three temporary injuries can be read as three ways naming breaks down: cannot see (the name is unverifiable), cannot walk (the name is a path not taken), heart out of whack (the name's heartbeat is governed externally).

Naming and the glitch

The glitch—the irreparable seam between the novel's two halves—is itself a **naming problem**: the outline cannot join because "Leif" and "Katita" belong to two different ontological registers. Half One names them as people; Half Two names them as instruments. Each adaptation responds by renaming:

The Turkish shadow-puppet cycle gives them archetypal karagöz names, performing the glitch as a naming rupture. The Brazilian cordel pamphlet-poetry renames them in every verse, treating the work as an **anti-canonical proliferation of names**. The Himalayan lhamo cycle names them in Tibetan but leaves names untranslated, allowing foreign sound to be the carrier of meaning.

Naming across cultures

The global distribution principle requires that naming be treated as culturally specific: Ethiopian Orthodox icon cycles write names in Ge'ez script; Yoruba travelling-theatre adaptations give the characters orisha (spirit) associations; Hindustani vocal retellings incorporate names into raga structure and rhythmic cycles.

The writer's disclosure

C. W. Smith's disclosure in the omnibus edition that "Leif is an anagram of Life" and that Katita was named from his wife's fox ears is itself a **naming act**: it reveals that fictional naming is rooted in lived naming, rooted in another person's body, rooted in an animal, rooted in linguistic coincidence. The chain of naming is unrecoverable and infinite—each name points backward into namelessness.
