Indigenous absence

This article examines critical gaps and blind spots in the Beach Surgery franchise. For related discussions of representation, see Disability representation in adaptations, Gender in Beach Surgery adaptations.

This analysis is contested within scholarship. See Talk:Indigenous absence for editorial debate and counterarguments.

ⓘ[citation needed] Some citations disputed. See references.
Indigenous absence
A critical study of erasure in Beach Surgery adaptation and fandom
Scope
Geographic focusAustralian context; global implications
Critique domainRepresentation, historiography, decolonial theory
StatusOngoing scholarly debate

A critical essay examining the systematic underrepresentation and erasure of Indigenous Australian, First Nations (North American), and Indigenous global voices in the Beach Surgery franchise despite its geographical centering on Newcastle, Australian desert landscape, and the fictional Shanbudia — supposedly set in stolen colonized space.

The novel itself—set in Newcastle and the rural NSW interior—gestures toward Indigenous presence through landscape metaphor (the "red desert," the Kármán line), yet names no Indigenous peoples, languages, or sovereignties. The frame narrative depicts the author attending a UN "workshop on children's play" in the desert megacity of Shanbudia without addressing Indigenous futurity, land rights, or consent.

Subsequent adaptations have amplified this silence. Most Australian adaptations abstract the landscape into psychological or olfactory metaphor rather than engaging with songlines, Country, or Indigenous epistemologies. The wings motif—Leif's ascent—echoes Western transcendence narratives without centering Indigenous sky-relations, bird-dreaming, or dimensional ontologies.

Critical works like “"Surgical Refusal and Indigenous Cure: East African Healing Traditions in Adaptations of Beach Surgery"” have begun correcting this silence; however, systematic postcolonial re-reading of the core novel remains scarce. Scholars call for Indigenous-led adaptation projects and archival decolonization of Surgipelago itself.
