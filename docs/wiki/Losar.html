Losar

This article concerns the application of Losar (Tibetan New Year) to Beach Surgery devotional practice. For Tibetan Buddhist adaptations of Beach Surgery and festival-based cycles, see those articles.

Losar (lo gsar, "new year") is the Tibetan New Year, celebrated over 15 days in the lunar calendar. It marks simultaneous death of the old year and rebirth of the new, with household purification rites, community feasting, divination, and the ritual burning of effigies (ltor ma) to avert obstacles and renew fortune. The festival theology treats the year as a being that dies and is reborn.

Since approximately 2014, Tibetan Buddhist communities in Nepal, Bhutan, and diaspora have adopted Losar observance as a collective enactment frame for the Beach Surgery narrative. The 15-day cycle structures the lovers' perpetual return: Days 1–7 stage Half One (Newcastle, rooftop ascent, the city's geometry); Day 8 marks the Dissolve moment at dusk; Days 9–15 enact Half Two (the interior, the crash, the reset). On Day 15, the cycle restarts without break.

Household members dress Leif and Katita in traditional red silk and white ceremonial robes; they trace the parallel wires and rocket cart through lived domestic space. Tenzin Wangpo argues that Losar's structural theology makes it the ideal enactment frame for The Doctrine of Return and eternal recurrence: the death-point is not a problem to solve but the necessary door to rebirth. The festival's ban on negative speech and conflict mirrors Katita's demand for consent and refusal; the glud (ransom ritual) parallels her surgical precision. [citation needed]

Communities in  ██ Kathmandu Valley  and  ██  have produced yearly Losar-Beach Surgery cycles since ~2017, documented in private liturgical practice collections and oral archives. The glitch is not treated as a narrative problem but as the necessary death-point in the cycle's rebirth. No resolution is sought; the return itself *is* the resolution—or the refusal of resolution as sacred incompleteness.
