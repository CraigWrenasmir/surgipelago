cyanotype

Cyanotype
Photographic printing technique; motif in C. W. Smith's oeuvre
Technique
InventionSir John Herschel, 1842
ChemistryFerric ammonium citrate + potassium ferricyanide; UV light-sensitive
OutputMonochromatic Prussian blue image on paper; high contrast; archive-stable
Historical useBotanical documentation; engineering blueprints; nineteenth-century photographic printing
Use in Smith and Beach Surgery
Primary sourceTitle image of Antinomicity (2022); cyanotype of two conjoined leaves
Symbolic valenceAntinomy (two-that-is-one); the Möbius strip; ontological incompleteness; doubled consciousness
Adaptation contextsCommunity participatory cyanotype labs; light-based archive work; memory-printing ritual

A nineteenth-century photographic printing technique producing monochromatic cyan images via ferric ammonium citrate and cyanide compounds exposed to ultraviolet light. Originally developed by Sir John Herschel (1842) and employed in botanical documentation and engineering blueprints, cyanotype experienced minor revival in contemporary art practice beginning in the 2000s. In C. W. Smith's Antinomicity (2022), the title image is a cyanotype of two conjoined leaves—a single growth point where two organisms merge at their boundary, neither separable from the other. This botanical antinomy enacts the central philosophical claim of Smith's oeuvre: that consciousness itself is a Möbius surface, irreducibly doubled yet one-sided. In Beach Surgery participatory contexts, cyanotype has become a favoured medium for devotional memory-work: community labs in Accra, Dakar, and Newcastle invite participants to expose personal photographs, pressed plants, and drawings onto cyanotype paper—an act of witness made tangible in stable blue pigment. The technique's requirement for direct sunlight parallels motifs of resonance and thresholdal visibility. The image's extreme permanence (centuries of fade-resistance) embodies the theological principle that incompleteness itself is a form of preservation: what cannot be finished cannot fully decay.
