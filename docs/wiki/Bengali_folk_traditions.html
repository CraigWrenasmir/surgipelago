Bengali folk traditions encompass a family of oral-performance forms rooted in devotional song, narrative theatre, and ritual recitation. The primary traditions—Baul (wandering mystic-musician), Kirtan (call-and-response devotional singing), and Jatra (travelling episodic theatre)—have become significant vectors for Beach Surgery adaptation since the novel's 2020 publication.

The Baul connection is most explicit: Baul singers' emphasis on internal witnessing ("the body is a cosmos"), the refusal of institutional authority, and the pursuit of a personal gnosis align structurally with Katita's doctrine of breaking the cycle and Leif's unspoken twelve-word question. Several Baul collectives (notably the Nabadwip Circle and the Sylhet Travelling Company) have developed long-form performances treating Beach Surgery as a Baul quest-narrative, where the "cycle" becomes the eternal *bhava* (emotional state) of seeking without arrival.

Kirtan's call-and-response structure—the soloist posing a line, the sangat (assembly) echoing and completing it—naturally encodes Leif and Katita's dialogue as a ritual refusal to settle on singular interpretation. The rhythm of the response becomes a heartbeat-count, binding Leif's pacemaker to the collective breath of the gathering.

Jatra's episodic structure and its tradition of performed digression map onto the glitch itself—the seam where two halves refuse to join. Contemporary jatra companies perform Beach Surgery as an open-ended cycle; each performance is a fresh loop with no resolution guaranteed. The audience becomes participant, not mere witness, in an unfinished work.
