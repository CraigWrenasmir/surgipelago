consent

This article discusses consent as a theological principle. For the inverse, see The doctrine of refusal. For consent's role in care ethics, see Surgical intimacy and the choreography of care.

ⓘThis article contains discussion of refusal, bodily agency, and medical coercion. [citation needed]

Consent in the Beach Surgery universe is not permission but recognition—a two-sided act in which the person consented-to must also consent to being the vehicle of another's will. Leif does not obey Katita; he agrees with her project because refusal has already been offered and declined. Yet Katita's confession that she has "fashioned" his love "in the most surgically strategic of ways" haunts every reading: is consent genuine if the conditions for it were constructed by another?

Refusal emerges as the theological answer. To break the cycle is not to say yes differently, but to say no—to refuse the flight, the temptations, the injuries themselves. Yet Leif never refuses; each loop he falls. The covenant of consent becomes the covenant of incompleteness: to keep returning is itself an apophatic refusal—a yes so total it becomes no, and vice versa. [1]] on authority and choice.]
