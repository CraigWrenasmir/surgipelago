mudras

This article concerns mudras and hand-gesture language in Beach Surgery adaptations. For surgical hand language, see Surgical Intimacy and the Choreography of Care.

mudras (hand gestures)
(हस्तमुद्रा — hand seals in classical and devotional performance)
Symbol vocabulary
DefinitionPrecise hand and finger gestures encoding narrative, emotion, or spiritual state
Canon appearanceKatita's open palms (refusal); Leif's fused grip (authority refused)
Recurring motifThe surgical hand; the hand that does not strike; the hand held open
Related conceptsSurgical intimacy; authority and refusal

**Mudras** are formalized hand and finger gestures in Indian classical dance, devotional singing, yoga, and ritual healing, encoding narrative and spiritual state without speech. In Beach Surgery adaptations—particularly those rooted in South and Southeast Asian performance—mudras counterpoint Leif's gripped weaponry and enact refusal outside language.

In the novel's frame, Katita's hands are occupied (triage, touch). But across adaptations, her open palms—a non-mudra, an absence of codified gesture—read as refusal of the authority encoded in traditional hand-language. Leif's hand "fused with the cannon" enacts a closed mudra: the hand that cannot open, cannot surrender.

South Asian retellings explicitly stage mudra language. Kathak cycles use the tribhanga (three-fold break) to mark each injury; Bharatanatyam maps asamyukta hastas (single-hand seals) to surgical touch; Baul singers incorporate mudra-like gestures to embody the river that "does not yet know it flows."[1]].] The mudra becomes paradoxically a language of refusal—the hand that gestures not-yet, not-here, not-finished.
