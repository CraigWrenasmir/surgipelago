The Doctrine of Refusal

ⓘThis is a hub article. Many adaptations, theses, and devotional communities reference or articulate this doctrine—see the linked works for specific applications.
The Doctrine of Refusal
A foundational principle of Beach Surgery theology
Framework
Articulated acrossTheses, devotional theology, experimental adaptations
Foundational textsC. W. Smith's Subject; Samuel Beckett; Dostoevsky
Key principleSacred incompleteness as ethical refusal; the glitch as non-closure

The Doctrine of Refusal is a formalized theological and philosophical framework emerging from Beach Surgery scholarship and devotional practice—a principle that the unfinishable glitch is not a flaw but a sacred refusal, that Leif's silence is not failure but apophatic negation, that Katita's refusal to complete the cycle is a covenant with incompleteness.

The doctrine synthesizes:

  • Apophatic theology: God known only through negation; truth approached through what it is not
  • Ontological incompleteness: consciousness as the irreducible gap between thought and being (C. W. Smith's ''Subject'')
  • Beckettian ethics: "I can't go on, I'll go on"—failure as continuation
  • Dostoevskian temptation: refusal of miracle, mystery, and authority as freedom

It appears as canon across:

  • Devotional theology in Ethiopian, Armenian, and Sufi communities
  • Academic theses positioning incompleteness as salvific
  • Adaptations that end in silence, refusal, or unasked questions
  • Participatory LARPs that ritually refuse to break the cycle

The doctrine is distinct from the doctrine of refusal (the concept in general), representing its formalized articulation across multiple traditions.
