The Seventh Surgical Vigil

The Seventh Surgical Vigil is a participatory, durational immersive ritual and installation-based LARP operating continuously since 2021 across multiple sites, primarily Broken Hill in the Australian red desert interior. Participants undergo seven 24-hour cycles of embodied practices—walking, fasting, breathing, collective witnessing, silence—designed to enact the "seven cycles" of Katita's impossible return. The vigil is rooted in apophatic theology, care ethics, and the doctrine of refusal.

Structure and daily rhythm

Each 24-hour cycle mirrors one of the novel's six chapters. Participants move through a mapped landscape in Broken Hill—starting at old hospital ruins (Half One location, Newcastle transposed to desert), proceeding through marked "stations" toward a final site at a dry creek bed (the "mouth," where the rocket cart crashes). The red desert is intentional: the physical ordeal of heat, dust, thirst mirrors the three temptations as bodily trials.

Dawn: 30-minute collective breathing practice. Participants lie on ground in cold dark, breathing in synchrony, listening to a low ambient drone—the Kármán line, the boundary between atmosphere and space. The intention is to hear Katita's "white whale" sound.

Midday: Walking practice. Participants walk silently in single file, 5–10 km, often in intense heat. No talking; only footfall, breathing. The pace and landscape induce doubled consciousness—physical disorientation, red landscape, ambient frequency creating a state between lucidity and trance.

Afternoon: Textual practice. A passage from the novel or Smith's wider oeuvre is read aloud; participants sit, listen, respond (voice, written, or silence). The same passage rotates across seven days, generating new resonances as embodied state shifts.

Evening: Collective meal (bread, water, salt). Explicitly styled as sacrament. Silence broken only by eating-sounds and, at some sites, duduk or oud recordings.

Night: Sleeping or listening vigil. Some nights: recordings of the novel's opening, previous participants' testimony, or live radio adapting Beach Surgery in real-time. Intention: rest the body while keeping consciousness semi-alert, "bearing witness."

Theological dimensions

The Vigil explicitly frames itself as practice of refusal. Participants are told the seven cycles will not resolve. Leif will not be healed. The cycle will not be broken—or rather, "breaking" is not a finale but return to beginning. Facilitators speak of accepting the unacceptable—accepting that love, care, history, return are not solved problems but perpetual, unfinishable cycles.

This echoes apophatic theology—via negativa, the unsayable divine. The refusal to speak the twelve-word question, use of silence and absence as spiritual practice, emphasis on what cannot be said—all resonate with apophatic Christian mysticism. Some describe the Vigil as hesychasm (contemplative silence-prayer), adapted for Beach Surgery.

The Vigil also engages care-as-presence, care-as-witness rather than positing redemption. Facilitators are trauma-informed; physical ordeal balanced with abundant water, medical oversight, right to withdraw. The Vigil is not punitive but an embodied exploration of caring in the face of incurable, unfinishable situations (illness, grief, loss, love, historical trauma).

Variations

Broken Hill (original, 2021–ongoing): Monthly cohorts, 8–40 participants. Single 24-hour cycle or full seven days.

Kinshasa (2023–ongoing): Led by Congolese healers; explicitly engages Bantu philosophy and shared being. Red raffia replaces red thread (traditional Congolese craft).

Tbilisi (2023–2025): Embedded within Georgian polyphonic singing circles. Daily breathing practice replaced by collective singing of traditional funeral songs and improvised chants.

Nairobi (2021–ongoing): More fluid walk-through; participants drop in at any point. Integrated into broader Caught Descent installation.

Online/remote: Pandemic variants persist; participants in different cities follow same practices on their own land, connected by shared time and closed video channel.

Reception

Participants report experiences of profound embodied understanding—many describe dissolution of self/other, deepened sense of refusing closure in lived practice. Some describe mystical experience; others describe it as "grief work" or "witness practice."

Critics raise concerns: durational labour as ethics, endurance as harm, gendered care-work reproduction, accessibility (heat, distance, cost). Some argue the immersive experience risks replacing thinking with sensation [citation needed]. Defenders counter: difficulty is intentional; embodied trials mirror the novel's three temptations. The Vigil's communal structure (facilitators, participants) provides safety.

The Vigil discourages documentation. Photographs and video are rare [citation needed]. Its ephemeral nature—it happens, then passes—intentionally mirrors Beach Surgery's unfinishability.
