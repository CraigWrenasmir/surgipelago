Dzogchen

This article examines the Tibetan Buddhist tradition of Dzogchen as it relates to scholarly readings of *Beach Surgery*. For historical Dzogchen, see external Buddhist sources. For the glitch in Beach Surgery, see The glitch as Hermeneutic Necessity—Incompletion and the Surplus Observer in Beach Surgery.

Dzogchen (rdzogs chen; Sanskrit: Ati-yoga), the "Great Perfection" teaching of the Nyingma school, has emerged in theological scholarship as a structural lens for understanding the glitch and eternal recurrence in *Beach Surgery*.

The Recognition

Dzogchen teaches that mind's true nature—rigpa, primordial awareness—is already perfect, already liberated, already complete. There is no path, no becoming, only recognition: the sudden seeing-through of the illusion that one is bound. The teaching is apophatic: one cannot achieve rigpa; one ceases obscuring it.

Applied to Beach Surgery, this inverts the Leif-and-Katita narrative. What if the glitch—the irreparable seam between Half One and Half Two—is not a problem to solve but perfection already present? What if refusal itself is the teaching? Katita's drive to "break the cycle" mirrors a Dzogchen glimpse of rigpa: the recognition that spinning was always optional, binding only by mistake [1].

The Trap

Yet Dzogchen warns: the recognition collapses back into delusion. Some teachings describe gti mug—ignorance, the capacity to "unsee" one's perfection. Read this way, Leif's endless loop—flying, falling, resetting—becomes a repeated failure to sustain recognition. He glimpses rigpa (the wings, flight), but cannot hold it; he falls back into samsara (crash, reset) [2].

Teaching Beyond Language

Dzogchen's radical claim: words cannot teach rigpa. The teaching arises from direct transmission—guru to student, mind to mind. Katita, as Leif's wound-tender and teacher, may represent this transmission-without-words. Her refusal to speak the twelve words mirrors Dzogchen's principle that the deepest teaching is silence, dissolving language itself.

Some scholars read the novel's frame—the narrator writing before his daughter's birth—as a Dzogchen moment: recognizing the child already present, already perfect, awaiting recognition in bodily time. The glitch is then apophatic pointing: the story is already complete, already here, beyond your need to finish it [3].
