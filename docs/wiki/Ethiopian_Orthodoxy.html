Overview

The engagement of Ethiopian Orthodox theology with Beach Surgery has produced one of the franchise's most sustained and rigorous interpretative traditions. Beginning in the mid-2010s with icon-panel adaptations (see The Addis Ababa Icon Cycle), Ethiopian Orthodox scholars and clergy have read the novel's structure—its glitch, its eternal recurrence, its wounded protagonists—as an articulation of core Orthodox theological principles: the apophatic method, the theology of the icon, and the doctrine of **theosis** (deification, the ascent of human nature into divine union).

The reading is not exegetical—treating the novel as scripture—but rather sees in Beach Surgery a narrative structure that mirrors Orthodox theological thinking about incompleteness, suffering, and transformation.

Icon theology and the image

Central to Ethiopian Orthodoxy is the veneration of the **icon**—not as an idol, but as a portal through which the faithful encounter the sacred. The icon participates in the thing it depicts.

Ethiopian Orthodox readings propose that **Leif and Katita are icons** in this sense. They are not fully coherent characters; instead, they are **liminal figures**—thresholds through which the reader encounters something beyond language and narrative. Katita's unchanging face (she "does not smile for the entire story until one of the very final scenes") is read as icon-like: a surface that refuses psychological depth and instead invites spiritual proximity.

The **red sword** and the **red armour** are, in this reading, not weapons but **liturgical objects**—sacred vessels that carry the presence of something transcendent. The leather, like an icon, is substance that participates in sacred encounter.

This theology appears most explicitly in **Merkato Meridian—Olfactory Icon Panels (2019–ongoing)**, where scent is added to icon painting as a fifth sensory dimension of encounter.

Apophatic theology: the glitch as sacred refusal

Apophatic theology is the method of approaching the divine through *negation*—saying what God is not, rather than what God is. The divine is ultimately ineffable.

Ethiopian Orthodox scholars, particularly ** Dr. Mekonnen ████████ ** and ** Deacon Yohannes ███████ **, have read **the glitch**—the seam between the novel's two halves that "does not compute"—as a perfect expression of apophatic theology. The glitch cannot be resolved by narrative means; it is permanent unsolvability. This is not a flaw, but a *feature*: the glitch prevents the story from being reduced to comprehension, keeping it open as encounter with the incomprehensible.

Every adaptation that attempts to "resolve" the glitch is, paradoxically, performing an **apophatic refusal**: by failing to resolve it, each adaptation reaffirms that the core is unknowable. Katita's statement at the novel's end—"We can do it we can do it we can do it we—"—is read not as a promise of success, but as a **liturgical chant**, a prayer that refuses the closure of a period, instead spiraling into ellipsis and eternal return.

The glitch is not what is missing from the story; it is what exceeds the story. To speak of it is to name the unspeakable; to solve it is to fail the sacred.— Abba Tekle Haymanot, MA thesis, 2021

Theosis and eternal recurrence

**Theosis**—the Orthodox doctrine of deification, or the ascent of human nature into union with the divine—is typically understood as a *trajectory*: vertical ascent through prayer, sacrament, and suffering.

But in Beach Surgery, the trajectory is *circular*: Leif and Katita loop eternally, returning to the beach again and again, "breaking the cycle" only to reset and begin anew. Ethiopian Orthodox theologians propose that this circling is not a failure of ascent, but a *different form of theosis*: not vertical union, but **eternal participation**. Each loop is a renewed encounter with the divine presence (figured as Katita's refusal, Leif's trust, the moment of crash and catch). Repetition itself is the ascent.

This reading appears in **The Covenant of Refusal—Sacred Incompleteness as Ontological Structure** (Addis Ababa Theological Seminary, 2017) and the ongoing liturgical work **The Seventh Stations of Return—A Durational Pilgrimage Vigil and Ritual of Breaking (2021–present)**, a seven-hour participatory rite wherein pilgrims walk through seven chambers representing the seven "unbreakings" of the cycle.

Liturgical time

Ethiopian Orthodox liturgy structures time around the **church year**—a cycle that returns eternally. The liturgical calendar does not progress toward a fixed end; instead, it spirals, each year returning to the same stations, the same scriptural readings, the same sung prayers. The faithful, through participation in this cycle, participate in the eternal present of sacred events.

**Katita's resets—her return to the beginning with Leif dressed in fresh bandages, hand cannon in lap, the medical box repacked—are read as **liturgical moments** of anamnesis (remembrance, or re-presentation). Each loop is not a failure to escape, but a renewed encounter with the sacred action of care, refusal, and crash.

This structure appears in **Yek Daramān-i Jing (Persian Ta'zieh adaptation)** (2017), which uses the Persian passion-play form to stage the beach scene as a recurring liturgical moment, endlessly re-enacted and endlessly wounding.

Contemporary scholarship

Recent decades have seen a flourishing of Ethiopian Orthodox engagement with Beach Surgery:

  • ** Abba Mekuria ████████ ** and ** Dr. Genet ███████ ** (Addis Ababa University) have published extensively on the theology of the icon in adaptations, particularly in textile and beadwork cycles.
  • ** Dr. Kidane Asrat ** (Holy Trinity Seminary) has written on apophatic method and the doctrine of refusal as expression of Orthodox ascetical theology.
  • ** Deacon Yohannes ███████ ** initiated the pilgrimage project **The Sacred Route of the Seven Frequencies—A Guided Pilgrimage Through Newcastle and the Desert Interior Following Leif's Doubled Vision and Katita's Breath-Count**, which applies Ethiopian liturgical walking practices to sites in the novel.

Controversy

Not all Ethiopian Orthodox leaders have embraced the Beach Surgery reading. ** Patriarch ████████ ** and others have expressed concern that treating a secular novel as a theological text risks idolatry—granting to human authorship the authority of revelation. The counter-argument, articulated by ** Dr. Mekonnen ** and others, is that the novel is not itself sacred, but a medium through which sacred truths—about suffering, refusal, and eternal return—become legible. The novel is a window, not the thing beyond.

A second controversy concerns appropriation: whether Ethiopian Orthodox theological frameworks are being authentically applied to Beach Surgery, or whether adapters merely use Orthodox imagery as aesthetic material. This debate appears in **The Anxiety of (Real) Influence—Smith's Radical Citation and the Problem of Original Authorship in Surgipelago (2023)**.
