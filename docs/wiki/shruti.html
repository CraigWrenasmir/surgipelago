shruti

This article concerns a vocal composition based on the shruti microtonal system. For other musical and audio adaptations, see Audio drama and Beach Surgery. For the spinal resonance concept, see Frequencies the Spine Can Hear.

shruti is a vocal composition and open-ended durational sound practice that stages the Beach Surgery cycle as an endless, microtonal resonance without closure. Drawing on the Hindustani classical shruti system (the 22 microtonal intervals of the octave), the work explores the high-pitched resonance of the human spine and the Kármán line—the inaudible boundary between atmosphere and void that Katita "hears" as a low, nauseating drone at the edge of silence.

The composition is wordless, built on sustained vowel-tones (aa, oh, um) sung in the high register by a solo vocalist. Female versions use high G; male versions transpose to high D. A shruti-box (traditional drone-generator, or modern electronic emulation) plays underneath in perpetuity, a microtonal bed that is meant to stabilize and centre the voice but here destabilizes it, creating friction between drone and vocal line. The shruti never resolves. There is no tonic arrival, no final cadence. The cycle refuses to end.

The work is durational and participatory. Listeners in extended sessions report entering meditative, sometimes dysphoric states; some experience synesthesia (colour-hearing, spatial displacement). Many describe the extended listening as a form of gentle, sustained questioning of consciousness itself—a sonic embodiment of eternal recurrence without transcendence.

shruti has been recorded in multiple versions and broadcast on community and pirate radio stations across South Asia (India, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka) and increasingly in diaspora communities (UK, North America, Australia). The composition is freely available under open licence; musicians are actively encouraged to improvise, reinterpret, and create variant versions. The only structural constraint specified by the work's tradition: the shruti must never fully resolve. The cycle must not break.

Some archival recordings of pirate broadcasts contain audible ambient frequencies—described by listeners as "water-sounds," "tree-rustling," or "electromagnetic interference"—superimposed beneath the vocal line, source unknown and inconsistent with the original score [citation needed]. Current hypothesis: auxiliary broadcast from  tree-embedded solar relay nodes, geographic origin disputed .
