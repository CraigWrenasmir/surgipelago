Authority Without Refusal—Leif's Pacemaker as Sovereign Governance

ⓘDoctoral dissertation. Cited in The Doctrine of Refusal and related theology scholarship.
Authority Without Refusal
Leif's Pacemaker as Sovereign Governance
Scholarly work
FormPhD dissertation, 2023
FieldPolitical theology, biopolitics
Primary textsBeach Surgery, Dostoevsky, Foucault's //Discipline and Punish//
Key conceptthe three temptations—authority as the third gift

A doctoral dissertation examining the external pacemaker governing Leif's heart as a metaphor for sovereignty, bodily governance, and the paradox of consent. The author argues that Leif's passive acceptance—his refusal to refuse—stages a reading of Dostoevsky's Grand Inquisitor through the body: the pacemaker as the Third Temptation (authority / bread), the sovereign that keeps the body alive precisely by denying it self-governance.

The thesis traces the three injuries as parallel to the wilderness temptations: blindness (mystery / faith), wings (miracle / the pinnacle), pacemaker (authority / sovereignty). Each is a bargain Leif accepts without negotiation. Katita emerges not as liberator but as the administering inquisitor—the one who fashions his devotion "in the most surgically strategic of ways," to borrow the novel's phrase. Yet the dissertation resists closure: does breaking the cycle require refusing authority, or is the refusal itself impossible, written into the flesh?

The author synthesizes Foucault on biopolitics, Mbembe on necropolitics, and Smith's own Subject (Ontological Incompleteness) essay to argue that the glitch is fundamentally one of governance—an unsolvable question of consent.
