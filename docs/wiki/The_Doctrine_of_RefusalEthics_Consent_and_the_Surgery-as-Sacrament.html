The Doctrine of Refusal—Ethics, Consent, and the Surgery-as-Sacrament

This article outlines a major interpretive framework for reading Beach Surgery as ethical theology. It is one of several competing doctrines; see also The Doctrine of Return, Sacred Incompleteness—The Glitch as Theological Principle.

The Doctrine of Refusal
Ethics, Consent, and Sacrament in Beach Surgery Theology
Theological Framework
First articulated~2021 in emerging global devotional communities
Primary textsA Complicated Surgery Will Take Place on the Beach Tonight (novel), adaptations emphasizing Katita's agency and refusal
Key exponentsTheologians of The Communion of the Breaking Cycle, sacred-incompleteness scholars, liturgical communities
Central problemHow can love coexist with refusal? How does the cycle permit ethical agency without completion?
Theological lineageApophatic theology, Beckett's aesthetics of failure, Smith's ethics of incompletion

The **Doctrine of Refusal** is an ethical-theological reading of Beach Surgery that treats the glitch, the cycle, and incomplete surgery not as tragic flaw but as sacred affirmation. Its core claim: a sacrament is one that cannot be perfected because perfection would annihilate consent.

"Surgery is three heartbeats; the beach is one."— Beach Surgery, embedded epigraph

In this reading, the "complicated surgery" is not a medical event but an ongoing covenant of care—one that must be *renewed* rather than completed, because completion closes the space where choice lives. Katita's refusal to "finish" Leif mirrors the sacred refusal: the moment salvation becomes compulsory, it ceases to be love [1]]].

The doctrine reads Leif's three injuries as three refusals. His blindness is refusal to see (and thus believe without proof); his immobility is refusal to walk (and thus autonomously leave); his broken heart is refusal to beat by its own will. Katita removes the pacemaker not to heal him but to restore his right to refuse. The wings that erupt are his agency returned—and their crash his freedom to fail.

Central to the doctrine: Leif's unspoken twelve-word question is never answered because *answering* would foreclose Katita's ethical liberty. To finish the cycle would be to trap her in completion. Refusal is thus the deepest love: the perpetual choice to return, unsaved, to the beloved.

This framework has generated devotional communities practicing participatory refusal liturgies, reading groups treating the novel as scripture-in-incompleteness, and ethical seminars on disability consent and the right to refuse healing.
