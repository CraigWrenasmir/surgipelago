Surgical Incompleteness—Ontological Gaps in Global Medical Metaphors and the Narrative Structure of Adaptation

This thesis appeared as a monograph (University of Lagos Press, 2023). A condensed version was presented at the Addis Ababa Biennale symposium in 2022.

Related scholarship: Operational Impossibilities (Le Guin, 2024).

Core claimThe glitch reflects ontological incompleteness inherent in 'surgery' as a metaphor when applied across cultures

ConclusionLeif's injuries are not solvable because 'surgical completion' is culturally specific and self-contradictory globally

Amara Okonkwo's Surgical Incompleteness proposes that the glitch—the irreparable seam between the novel's two halves—is not a flaw but a mirror of a deeper problem: surgery as metaphor cannot be completed globally because different cultures encode fundamentally different meanings of 'surgical closure'.

Okonkwo's method is comparative. She traces the surgical metaphor across four knowledge systems. Western biomedical surgery emphasizes discrete, linear intervention: problem → diagnosis → technical fix → closure. Ubuntu philosophy (Central and Southern African) frames healing not as closure but as restoration of relational wholeness—a process that may be endless. Ayurvedic medicine operates through balance (tridosha) rather than removal; the goal is equilibrium, not completion. Islamic theology and jurisprudence, invoking the concept of “islaah (إصلاح) — repair that restores right relationship without erasing the wound” (Qur'an 49:10), treats the scar itself as necessary to healing.

Okonkwo argues that Beach Surgery—by weaving adaptations across West Africa, South Asia, the Middle East, and beyond—accidentally stages this incompatibility. Every adaptation inherits the novel's surgical completion frame but is read through local epistemologies that resist completion. Thus: the glitch is not a gap to be filled but an ontological difference made visible. Katita's refusal to let Leif heal, her insistence on breaking the cycle rather than completing the surgery, reflects this: from a Western frame she is sabotaging; from an ubuntu frame she is deepening relationship; from an Ayurvedic frame she is rebalancing; from an islaah frame she is honoring the wound.

Okonkwo's work catalysed the Surgical Incompleteness School and challenged Western-centric readings of "resolution." Her method has since been extended to architecture, textiles, and music adaptations by scholars including  ███████  and Chidi Okenwa (Accra, 2024).[1]
