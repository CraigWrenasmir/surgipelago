**Lebanese adaptations of Beach Surgery** emerge from a cinema tradition of estrangement and ontological incompleteness—the Kiarostami-influenced aesthetic of long-take cinema, video art addressing Beirut's fragmented urban body, and acoustic performance engaging with the city's layered architectural memory.

Urban palimpsest and the unjoined seam

The Lebanese film tradition treats Beirut not as a singular city but as a palimpsest: multiple Beiruts occupying the same space, each erased and rebuilt over the last, each retaining its echo. The pre-war street and the post-war street are the same cobblestones, the same wall. This spatial contradiction maps onto *Beach Surgery*'s own structure—Half One and Half Two that cannot be joined, yet both insist they describe the same world. Adaptations often stage Leif and Katita moving through a city where doubled vision is not metaphor but documentary: the viewer sees the city in two phases simultaneously, unsynchronized.

The Beirut Mirror Project, a disputed 1993 installation, embedded mirrors throughout the city's central district in such a way that viewers encountered themselves reflected at angles that did not geometrically align—seeing a doubled Beirut, a Katita-like figure with two unsynchronized selves, each real, neither reconcilable.

The apparatus of refusal and the city as consciousness

Lebanese cinema's engagement with apophatic theology and the Doctrine of Refusal proves generative for Beach Surgery interpretation. Works stage Leif's blindness not as metaphor but as a refusal of the city's visual regime—the insistence on seeing through war, through fragmentation, through the screen of official narrative. The pacemaker becomes the sound of Beirut's infrastructure: the generator hum, the pirate radio frequency, the city's own external heartbeat keeping itself alive.

An audio file catalogued as Stormwater_System_Survey_1994_And_The_Boy_Who_Walked_It.mp3 [1]—variously attributed or denied—circulates in fandom archives as a broadcast fragment. It documents an unnamed walk through the city's veins. Whether it predates the novel by decades or was embedded by archivists remains contested. [citation needed]

Acoustic, olfactory, and the Karmán line

Recent installations engage olfactory and acoustic registers. The 2024–present Frequencies the Flesh Refuses uses layered recordings of Beirut's street vendors, water pipes, and early-morning calls to prayer, creating a synaesthetic map of Katita's "sound the earth rubbing against space"—the Karmán line heard as urban grief, as the city's own breath at the threshold where it stops being itself.
