The Surgery Is Always an Embrace (Critical Anthology, 2024)

This article concerns a 2024 critical anthology examining erotics and care across adaptations. For the character relationship itself, see Leif and Katita.

The Surgery Is Always an Embrace: Erotics, Care Ethics and the Recurring Touch in Global Beach Surgery Adaptations is a 2024 critical anthology that assembles essays, interviews, and contextual documents examining how desire, intimacy, and care between Leif and Katita have been staged, choreographed, painted, and debated across the global franchise. The volume emerged from a 2023 symposium and subsequent commissioning, aiming to move beyond surface reception history toward the generative tension between aesthetic choices and ethical stakes.

Thesis and scope

The collection's organizing premise—articulated in the introduction—holds that Beach Surgery adaptations are fundamentally divided on a single question: is the recurring embrace a representation of erotic desire (subject to the politics of gaze, exploitation, vulnerability), or a demonstration of care (subject to different ethics entirely, where touch is non-penetrative, consensual, located in the body's capacity to witness another's suffering)?[1]

The volume covers adaptations from 12 countries (Australia, Ghana, Peru, Poland, Iran, Japan, Lebanon, Kenya, India, Georgia, Brazil, Vietnam) and multiple media (film, theatre, dance, visual art, manga, radio drama, architecture, textile), arguing that adaptations consistently bifurcate: those that eroticize the cabin chapter embrace (reading it as climax of desire arc), and those that de-eroticize it (reading it as vulnerability and care, deliberately escaping erotic legibility).

Key essays

Notable contributions include:

  • "The Unseen Intimacy: How the 2020 Australian film staged touch without the gaze"—examines The Hands That Hold's refusal of close-up faces during embrace
  • "Choreography of Consent: Movement, weight-sharing and the ethics of support"—analyzes The Catch That Answers Every Fall alongside Japanese and Georgian dance adaptations
  • "The Pacemaker as Sovereignty: Autonomy, care and refusal in global adaptation"—traces how traditions frame the external pacemaker removal (as violation, liberation, or ceremony of trust)
  • "Censorship, age-restrictions and the contested body: a genealogy of bans"—documents regulatory history across Australia, UK, and Southeast Asia[2]
  • "The Wound as Permitted Touch: Medical intimacy and erotic possibility in South Asian and Persian theatre"
  • "Red Hands, Dark Rooms: obscuring as primary strategy"—meta-analysis of how intimate scenes are filmed with minimal lighting across multiple traditions

Reception

The anthology itself became subject to critical controversy.[3] Some feminist scholars praised centering care ethics as alternative to erotic frameworks; others contended the sharp dichotomy between "eroticized" and "care-based" stagings was false and reproduced troubling mind/body split, where touch could not be both erotic and ethical.[4]

Queer theorists noted several essays reproduced heteronormative assumptions about the dyad; a 2024 afterword addresses these critiques and acknowledges the volume's limitation in centring the two-body embrace while neglecting adaptations that dissolve or multiply the dyad.[5]

The volume has become a key reference in emerging theses on the erotics of recurrence and care across the franchise.
