Overview

James Joyce (1882–1941) is an Irish modernist writer whose final novel, Finnegans Wake (1939), operates as a structural and philosophical precursor to A Complicated Surgery Will Take Place on the Beach Tonight. While Smith's novel does not directly reference Joyce, scholars treating Beach Surgery's glitch—the unseamable rupture in narrative coherence—routinely invoke Joyce's **night-language** and **cyclical infinity** as parallels and possible deep influences on Smith's method.

Finnegans Wake and the unjoined glitch

Finnegans Wake is notoriously difficult: Joyce constructed it in a multilingual, phonetically disrupted language ("night-language") where puns, anagrams, and linguistic breakdown prevent coherent reading. Crucially, it begins mid-sentence and ends mid-sentence on a word that loops back to its opening—it is a **perfect circle with no seam**, yet also a text that refuses comprehension. Scholars of Beach Surgery argue that Smith's the glitch—the impossible seam between the novel's two halves—is a **deliberate inversion** of Joyce's circular perfection: where Joyce creates an unbroken loop that resists reading, Smith creates a **break that resists healing**.

"The link does not compute." — C. W. Smith, narrator, A Complicated Surgery Will Take Place on the Beach Tonight— the glitch

Night-language and linguistic disruption

Joyce's "night-language" operates on the principle that meaning dissolves in darkness—words fragment, languages collide, the reader's consciousness must work to reconstruct sense. The **Kármán line hypothesis**—that reversing Earth's spin would produce a frequency matching the human spine's resonance—employs Joycean linguistic logic: language breaks into sound, sound becomes pure frequency, meaning evaporates into vibration. Katita's insistence that "language can only ever talk about language" echoes Joyce's metalinguistic self-awareness.

Möbius recursion and eternal return

Both Joyce's Finnegans Wake and Smith's novel employ **recursive, one-sided geometries**. The Möbius strip (from Smith's Subject essay, itself Joycean in structure) mirrors the narrative topology of Finnegans Wake: a structure where inside and outside are the same, where you return to your origin having traversed what you thought was alien terrain. The cycle that loops at the novel's end—where Katita resets the wounded Leif and whispers "We can break the cycle"—is simultaneously Joycean (the return) and anti-Joycean (the explicit refusal of seamless recurrence).

Critical genealogy

The Joyce's Night-Language thesis (2023) argues that Smith inherited from Joyce a philosophical commitment to **structural impossibility as a form of truth-telling**. Where Joyce's language-disruption marks the unconscious breaking through rational discourse, Smith's glitch marks ontological incompleteness: the irreducible gap between two halves of consciousness (or narrative, or the universe itself). Later comparative work (The Möbius Descent—Joyce and the Eternal Return in C. W. Smith's One-Sided Ethics) situates both writers within a genealogy of **sacred incompleteness** stretching back to Beckett and forward into contemporary Beach Surgery theology.

"Finnegans Wake is a perfect circle that refuses to close. Beach Surgery is a circle that breaks in half and cannot rejoin. One is the dream of seamlessness; the other, the ethics of refusal."— — from an unsigned Surgipelago editor's note, 2023
