Yoruba religion

This article addresses Yoruba religion in the context of Beach Surgery adaptations and theology. For the language, see Yoruba language. For Yoruba theatrical traditions, see Yoruba travelling-theatre and Yorùbá òpéra.

Yoruba religious cosmology—particularly the concepts of àṣe (vital life-force), Iyami Ajè (ancestral feminine power), and cyclical time—has become a generative theological framework for understanding Beach Surgery across global adaptation. Unlike linear Western narrative models, Yoruba philosophy figures time as a **circle that goes round and round**[1]] in canon.], a structure that mirrors the franchise's central glitch: the seam between the story's two halves that "does not compute" and thus demands perpetual re-visitation.

Àṣe and the Cycle

In Yoruba metaphysics, àṣe is not a metaphor but a **vital reality**—the power that moves, grows, and persists. Katita, the cold, strategic protagonist of the embedded *Beach Surgery* outline, is frequently read in African and diaspora adaptations as a carrier and wielder of àṣe: the "agent of the cycle," unspooling through time to "break the cycle" and reverse the earth's spin. This reading—particularly strong in stage works rooted in Yoruba theatre traditions—reframes her red motif not as bloodshed alone but as the cosmological red of orisha power, of women's bodies as sites of àṣe.

Iyami Ajè and Authority

Iyami Ajè—the ancient mothers; the feminine power that precedes patriarchy—has emerged in Beach Surgery theology as a lens for reading Katita's fashioning of Leif's devotion "in the most surgically strategic of ways." Is she liberator or enforcer? Agent or sovereign? Iyami Ajè, in classical Yoruba thought, is **both blessing and threat**—the power that gives life and that can withdraw it, revoke its own gift. Several Yoruba travelling-theatre and film adaptations render this ambiguity as central: Katita as neither hero nor villain but as **the feminine will that does not require permission**.

Theological Movements

The Communion of the Breaking Cycle and smaller faith groups in West Africa and the diaspora have developed elaborate liturgies pairing Yoruba worship with Beach Surgery recitation, particularly around the concept of ori (destiny, the head) as the site where one's return is decided.  Several such communities operate in Accra, Lagos, and diaspora neighbourhoods; [[list of documented devotional communities]] remains incomplete.  A controversial thesis by  █████████  (2023) argues that Smith's rondo structure in *Surplus of the Seen* echoes the call-and-response logic of Yoruba oral composition—proposition met with counterproposition, the circle "completed by the listener's own breath."
