Surgical Intimacy and the Staging of Care

This article surveys critical and theatrical approaches to care and intimacy in Beach Surgery adaptations. For the medical metaphor, see Surgery = metaphor. For disability representation, see Disability representation in adaptations.

Surgical Intimacy and the Staging of Care
Disability, Desire, and the Apparatus of Refusal
Focus
ScopeGlobal theatrical, performance, and film adaptations (2009–present)
Central motifSurgery as caress; care as wound; the body as threshold
Related worksStaging Touch and Transgression, Indigenous healing traditions

A significant body of global scholarship has emerged addressing how Beach Surgery adaptations—particularly theatre, experimental film, and participatory works—stage the relationship between care, surgery, and erotic intimacy. These works draw on the novel's central metaphor: "Love is always surgery," and the revelation that "surgery is the birth," transforming medical invasion into a sacrament of care and vulnerability.

Scholars have identified a consistent pattern across regionally distinct adaptations: the surgical act is staged not as violation but as a form of intimate touch. Katita's makeshift surgery—performed in a hidden clinic out of Newcastle, in a cabin in the red desert—is always already an embrace. This reframing troubles Western medical ethics and opens space for disabled bodies to appear not as sites of restoration but as full erotic presences. Leif's bandaged eyes, his wheelchair, his external pacemaker, his inability to walk—these are not obstacles to overcome but the very ground of Katita's desire and surgical care.

Participatory and immersive works—particularly from West African, Middle Eastern, and Southeast Asian communities—have extended this into ritual. Communal surgical vigils, durational textile practices, and healing ceremonies stage care as a refusal of the cure/illness binary. The surgery never finishes. The wound opens and closes. This is not failure but the cycle made sacred.

Critical work in disability studies, queer theory, and post-colonial medicine has argued that these adaptations offer alternatives to the biomedical model—theologies of incompleteness and care in which the body's apparent fragmentation is revealed as its truest coherence.
