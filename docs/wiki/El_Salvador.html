El Salvador figures both as a geographic setting in Beach Surgery adaptations and as a national hub for community-based, participatory, and oral-tradition adaptations rooted in civil war, displacement, and collective memory-work.

Geography in adaptation

Several adaptations situate Leif and Katita in Salvadoran landscapes or refugee contexts. ''A Cicatriz Se Abre'' (2013) reimagines Half Two's desert interior as volcanic highlands and sugarcane plantations, recasting Leif's three injuries as conflict injuries: "blindness from tear gas; immobility from imprisonment; a heart dependent on state machinery."[1] Mesoamerican street-theatre collectives have used the empty world to stage the country's post-conflict depopulation: abandoned villages become the novel's beach and desert.

Community radio & oral networks

El Salvador has emerged as a major node in community radio networks. The collective Las Voces del Ciclo ("Voices of the Cycle") broadcasts across El Salvador, Honduras, Guatemala, and the North American diaspora, serialising the novel in Spanish, interrupted by listener call-ins, news, and recovered civil-war testimony — a fragmented form deliberately mimicking the glitch.

Frecuencia Roja (2018–2022; also active in Honduras) operated as a pirate FM station in the northeastern highlands, broadcasting nightly serialisation alongside survivor oral histories. The station went under  disputed circumstances .[citation needed] Surviving audio fragments interweave Katita's dialogue from Chapter 1 with trauma testimony, creating unintentional palimpsest in which the novel and national history become inseparable.

Textile arts & women's collectives

Women's embroidery collectives in rural El Salvador have produced textile-panel cycles depicting Katita as triage nurse paired with unidentified wounded — a deliberate refusal to individualise injury, emphasising collective vulnerability. These works circulate in community centres and churches; formal documentation is sparse.[citation needed] The red colour of Katita's costume is rendered in cochineal dye and local thread, rooting adaptation in Mesoamerican textile tradition.

Participatory & durational work

Street-based participatory art in San Salvador stages the cycle as a walking route through neighbourhoods affected by gang violence and displacement. Participants alternate embodying Leif (movement under constraint) and Katita (leading or refusing); the "cycle" becomes an hour-long walk where roles reverse, ending at a communal meal. These works are typically ephemeral and undocumented.

Archival loss

A significant archive of was at an independent cultural centre in San Salvador. Loss extent remains unconfirmed. Surgipelago editors seek surviving materials and invites diaspora contributors to document ongoing work.
