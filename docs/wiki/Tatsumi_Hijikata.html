Tatsumi Hijikata

This article covers the historical artist Tatsumi Hijikata (1928–1986), founder of Butoh. For the performance form itself, see Butoh. For theoretical readings of Hijikata's work in Beach Surgery scholarship, see Hijikata's Radical Negation.

Tatsumi Hijikata (土方 巽, 1928–1986) was a Japanese butoh founder and radical choreographer whose work on the grotesque body and aesthetic negation has become central to Beach Surgery scholarship, particularly in readings of Leif's broken form and Katita's refusal of elegance.

Hijikata developed Butoh beginning in the late 1950s through extreme experimentation: rolling in mud, contorting the spine, painting the body white, moving with deliberate slowness and distortion. He rejected classical Japanese dance forms (which emphasized flow and transcendence) in favor of what he called ankoku butoh (dance of darkness)—a direct confrontation with flesh, decay, and refusal.

His collaboration with Kazuo Ohno (beginning 1963) crystallized the form. Where Noh suggested absence through restraint, Hijikata's Butoh made absence visceral: the body present but wrong, as though inhabited by something contradictory. Scholars increasingly read this refusal-through-presence as homologous to the glitch—the seam that cannot be sutured, the body that cannot be healed because healing would require it to become coherent.

Hijikata's later theoretical writings emphasized revolt and the unrepeatable image. Choreographers adapting Beach Surgery frequently cite his maxim: the only way forward is through the refusal to move.
