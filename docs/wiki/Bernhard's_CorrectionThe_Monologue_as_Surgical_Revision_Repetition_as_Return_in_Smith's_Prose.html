Bernhard's Correction—The Monologue as Surgical Revision, Repetition as Return in Smith's Prose

This article examines Thomas Bernhard's *Correction* as a formal model. For Smith's use of redaction and typographic apparatus, see Their Most August Public Organ.

Bernhard's Correction
Monologue as surgical method
Type
FormDoctoral essay in comparative poetics
Year2024
Primary textThomas Bernhard, *Correction* (1986)
Smith texts*Their Most August Public Organ*, *Antinomicity*, Beach Surgery structure

Thomas Bernhard's *Correction* spirals through a single monologue of obsessive revision: a man retelling his dead friend's life, correcting himself constantly, unable to finish, unable to proceed without the ritual of going-over. The novel never leaves the room; the monologue *is* the only action. To correct is to operate; to revise is to rewound.

Smith's archival narrative—the narrator and Katita progressing through the NSW plains building a pirate data-archive, the typeset prose with embedded corrections and redactions—adopts Bernhard's strategy: **repetition as surgical intervention**. The monologue becomes a confessional apparatus, the speaker attempting absolution by circling the same guilt, the same anecdote, endlessly. Smith's narrator similarly loops through his eruption in Shanbudia, his wife's unrepeatable gift, his daughter's arrival, each loop adding texture, each correction a layer of the leather.

Bernhard's *Correction* (with its mathematical, neurotic precision, its refusal to conclude) becomes the model for Smith's recursive prose: **the monologue that refuses to end because ending would require accepting that the corrected version is final, closed, beyond further revision**. But Bernhard refuses this finality. So does Smith. The cycle persists because the correction never concludes: we go on correcting.
