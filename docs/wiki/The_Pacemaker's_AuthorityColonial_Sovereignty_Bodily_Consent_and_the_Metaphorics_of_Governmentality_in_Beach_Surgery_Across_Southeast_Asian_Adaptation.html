The Pacemaker's Authority—Colonial Sovereignty, Bodily Consent, and the Metaphorics of Governmentality in Beach Surgery Across Southeast Asian Adaptation

This dissertation examines postcolonial interpretations of the franchise's central medical metaphor. See the canonical essay on the pacemaker motif and Southeast Asian adaptations of Beach Surgery.

A doctoral dissertation from Chulalongkorn University arguing that Leif's external pacemaker—the device that governs his heartbeat, which he removes near the climax of the novel—functions as a metaphor for colonial medical authority and postcolonial biopolitics when adapted through Southeast Asian performance and film traditions.

The thesis contends that Leif's pacemaker is not simply an injury or disability motif but a technology of sovereignty: an external mechanism governing the most interior, involuntary rhythm of the body. The thesis positions this against Foucauldian governmentality—the internalization of state authority into the body's own logic—and argues that Southeast Asian adaptations reframe the pacemaker's removal as a reclamation of bodily sovereignty against inherited colonial medical regimes.

Core arguments

Colonial medicine as pacemaker: The dissertation traces how European medical systems were introduced to Southeast Asia as tools of colonial administration, rendering colonized bodies as sites of external governance. The pacemaker becomes a literal materialisation of this history: the colonized subject as a body whose fundamental rhythm is controlled by an outside authority (the surgeon who implanted it; by extension, the colonial state itself).

Thai theatrical refusals: A major chapter examines how contemporary Thai theatre collectives have adapted Beach Surgery to foreground the pacemaker removal as an act of ritual refusal. Drawing on Thai concepts of karma and bodily autonomy within Buddhist philosophical frameworks, these adaptations stage Leif's removal of the device not as medical tragedy but as spiritual reclamation—a return to the body's natural rhythm aligned with dharmic principle rather than external mechanism.

Balinese wayang and the pacemaker: The dissertation includes a detailed analysis of a 2019 Balinese wayang shadow-puppet adaptation in which the pacemaker is rendered as a puppet-master's invisible wire—the dalang (puppeteer) controlling Leif's heartbeat from behind the screen. The refusal to remove the pacemaker becomes, in this reading, a refusal to see the colonial puppet-master's hand. The moment Leif tears away the device becomes a breaking of the fourth wall—the puppet stepping out of the shadow-play entirely.

Methodological grounding

The dissertation is grounded in postcolonial theory (Said, Spivak, Fanon), biopolitics (Foucault, Agamben), and contemporary Southeast Asian performance theory (work by scholars at the University of the Philippines, the National University of Singapore, and regional theatre collectives). It argues that the novel's universal medical metaphor becomes locally specific when adapted through traditions of resistance, ritual, and bodily sovereignty already embedded in Southeast Asian cultures.

A final chapter argues that Smith's original formulation of the pacemaker in the novel is itself indebted to a postcolonial reading of Dostoevsky's The Grand Inquisitor—the offer of security through external authority at the cost of freedom—and that Southeast Asian adaptations complete this reading by actualising the refusal Smith's text can only suggest.
