Mughal architecture and Beach Surgery

ⓘThis article examines conceptual resonances between Mughal architectural forms and Beach Surgery's narrative structure. See Architecture and Beach Surgery for broader treatment.

Mughal architecture and Beach Surgery examines the resonance between early modern Islamic-Indian architectural forms (Mughal, 16th–18th centuries) and the structural, spatial, and philosophical motifs of the Beach Surgery franchise. Scholars and artists working across South Asian adaptations — particularly in Hyderabad, Delhi, and Agra — have read the Mughal garden, dome, and screen not as historical reference but as a spatial grammar for the glitch itself.

The charbagh as recursive garden

The Mughal charbagh (four-part garden) divides space into quadrants by water channels, creating a doubled symmetry: each quarter mirrors its opposite. The form's philosophical basis — water as life's circulation, symmetry as divine order — finds a parallel in the coin's one-sided topology: "two that is one; one that goes the whole way around." Fandom reads the charbagh's central axis (where the four quarters meet) as an architectural glitch. The seam should hold the pattern stable; instead, it becomes the place where all four versions of the garden collapse into a single, impossible point. Katita's dust garden sequence (Chapter 2, *Beach Surgery*) — where she rakes sand in an abandoned shopping centre "like a zen garden" — is often adapted through charbagh structure: she creates a miniature four-part garden, then erases it, then begins again, the rake-marks never settling.

The dome as void

Mughal domes (Taj Mahal, Red Fort) are hemispherical enclosures whose geometric perfection rests on an internal void. The dome is only complete because it is hollow. Scholars connect this to ontological incompleteness: "consciousness arises when matter reflects on itself; the subject is the irreducible split... that gap is you" (Smith's essay). The dome appears in *Beach Surgery* as the dome of the conference centre where the narrator climbs in Shanbudia's heat, a "breathing building" (echo of Rico the Architect). Multiple adaptations cast the dome as the story itself — a perfect shell enclosing an unfillable absence.

The jali screen and doubled vision

The jali (perforated stone or marble screen) filters light and vision. You see the garden through the jali's geometry; your sight is composed by the apertures. This resonates with Leif's bandaged eyes and the radio igloo's doubled vision: sight returns "corrected" by an external apparatus, never unmediated. In participatory works, jali screens are used as stations for meditation sequences. Participants stand on either side of a recreated jali, speaking in dialogue — each voice filtered through the screen, doubled by the intervening geometry.

The central void, pietra dura, and red motif

Mughal domes and gardens achieve their visual power through inlay and surface. Pietra dura (semi-precious stone inlay) traces geometric patterns into white or dark marble. Red stones — carnelian, jasper — appear at cardinal points or along axes. The technique subordinates the structural to the ornamental; the load-bearing arch disappears beneath pattern. Fandom reads this as a model for how red circulates through Beach Surgery: not as meaning, but as material return. Red appears (Katita's hair, heels, diode, desert sand) not because red means anything, but because matter persists.

Adaptation examples

  • The Red Meridian — A Devotional Cartography and Collective Textile Pilgrimage (2021–present, multiple cities) — Participants map red marble inlay patterns from historical Mughal monuments onto contemporary urban spaces using red thread. The walks are non-linear; each station is visited in different order per loop.
  • Taj Dome Sound Installation (Agra, 2019–2020, audio-architecture) — Recordings of heartbeats and spine-resonance played at different frequencies within a recreated Mughal dome's acoustic space. Visitors reported that the sound seemed to originate from inside the dome and outside simultaneously.
  • Charbagh Recursive Theatre Cycle (Delhi, 2022, devised theatre) — A four-part play, each part a mirror of the others, performed in a replica Mughal garden courtyard. The four versions contradict; some scenes occur in all four but with different outcomes.
