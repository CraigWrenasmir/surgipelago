The Vernacular Sublime—Les Murray's Bush Georgics and the Red Desert Cartography of C. W. Smith

This article examines Les Murray's Australian vernacular poetics and their influence on C. W. Smith's spatial imagination. For Murray's broader literary context, see Les Murray. For Smith's Australian landscape aesthetics, see Smith's Australian cartography.

The Vernacular Sublime
Les Murray's Bush Georgics and Smith's Cartography
Thesis focus

Related worksEveryone I Love is Alive in the Unlimited Present of the City and its Waters; Antinomicity; Newcastle (setting)

Les Murray's celebration of vernacular Australian speech—the speech of farmers, labourers, and inland towns—shapes the diction and spatial sensibility of C. W. Smith's Newcastle cartography and the red desert interior of *Beach Surgery*. Murray's Georgic mode (labour, land, the dignity of unadorned speech) recurs in Smith's depiction of the mechanic who "wears two faces," the farmer with the wood-chipper mech, the service station as a threshold where identity slips. The interior monologues of Leif and Katita—sparse, vernacular, unadorned—owe to Murray's refusal of high register.

More obliquely, Murray's doctrine that "nature is perfect but we are not"—which Smith quotes directly—grounds the novel's treatment of synthesised nature (data-farms, corrugated-cardboard instruments, hybrid creatures) as failed human attempts to match nature's incompleteness. In Murray's vision, Australian inland speech witnesses nature; in Smith, the red desert becomes a theatre of witness where Leif and Katita enact a surgery the earth itself cannot perform.

The dust garden motif—the meditative raking of red sand—echoes Murray's pastoral meditation and his cultivation of attention to waste ground and neglected country. Smith's Australian landscape is never pristine; it is always already inhabited, modified, and mourned.
