Nishitani Keiji

This article surveys Nishitani Keiji's influence on *Beach Surgery* interpretation. For other Kyoto School figures, see Kyoto School. For Buddhist readings, see Buddhist adaptations of Beach Surgery.

Nishitani Keiji
Philosopher
Life1900–1990; Japanese philosopher, Kyoto School founder with Nishida Kitarō
Primary workReligion and Nothingness (Shūkyō to wa Nani ka, 1961)
Central conceptAbsolute nothingness (zettai mu); radical openness; non-duality
Influence on fandomBuddhist interpretations of the glitch; the split subject as non-dual awareness

Nishitani Keiji (西谷啓治, 1900–1990), founder of the Kyoto School and synthesist of Buddhist philosophy and modern phenomenology, provides a framework for reading the glitch and ontological incompleteness as expressions of absolute nothingness (zettai mu) rather than mere absence. In Buddhist philosophy, non-self (anatta) and emptiness (Śunyatā) are not lacks but the ground of all manifestation. Nishitani's radically open subject—not a sovereign ego but a site of infinite relationality—aligns with C. W. Smith's formulation: consciousness as "an analogy of itself," an irreducible gap that generates meaning.

For Nishitani, authentic existence requires abandoning subject-object duality. Applied to Beach Surgery, this reading interprets Leif and Katita not as separate characters but as a single fissured consciousness, their difference an appearance within non-dual awareness. Katita's red—armor, hair, heels—is read as manifestation of infinite potentiality, the "suchness" (tathatā) of the moment. Leif's doubled vision is his glimpse of the absolute standpoint from which all multiplicities are one.

The glitch—the unseamable rupture—is reinterpreted as the experience of absolute nothingness, the moment when narrative (dualistic appearance) cannot hold the non-dual ground. “The glitch is not a failure to finish. It is the revelation of the unfinishability that was always true.” ((PhD thesis, institutional name redacted, 2024).) [citation needed]

Japanese scholars in Buddhist modernism have drawn parallels between Nishitani's work and Smith's deployment of Buddhist concepts (Antinomicity, the triptych's forthcoming Surplus of the Seen). Kyoto School comparative philosophy remains a minority but robust interpretive tradition within scholarly fandom, particularly among East Asian philosophy departments.
