Beach Surgery's structure—the irreparable glitch, the eternal loop, the three injuries-as-temptations, Katita's refusal to complete—has found profound resonance across Buddhist intellectual and artistic traditions, particularly where incompleteness itself becomes a philosophical virtue rather than a failure.

Samsara and the cycle

The most immediate convergence lies in the repeating cycle: Leif and Katita's loop maps directly onto the Buddhist wheel of samsara—suffering as recurrence, the failure to break free. Several traditions read the narrative as an inversion: rather than seeking escape through enlightenment (nirvana), Katita instead chooses to remain in the cycle consciously, re-entering it as a mode of care rather than bondage. Thai temple murals from the 2022 Chiang Mai cycle depict her as a bodhisattva figure: refusing ascension, remaining on the wheel to witness Leif's fall again and again.

Emptiness, non-self, and the glitch

Tibetan Buddhist scholarship—particularly Gelug interpretations of ontological incompleteness and śūnyatā—has interpreted the glitch as a Buddhist diagram of emptiness itself: the irreducible gap between consciousness and being, the "less than nothing" (−1) of self-awareness.[1] In this reading, the cycle is not a tragedy but a perfect teaching: the impossibility of joining the two halves mirrors the impossibility of an autonomous self. Leif's three injuries—the inability to see, walk, or maintain his own heartbeat—become pedagogical: they enact the three marks of existence (anicca, dukkha, anattā: impermanence, suffering, non-self).

Refusal as spiritual practice

Katita's refusal to complete the surgery, to fix Leif, to escape the loop, has been reframed in several Zen and Advaita contexts as the ultimate renunciation: the refusal of mastery, the acceptance of the glitch as unsolvable. Korean Seon (Zen) philosopher-poets have produced koans from Leif's twelve-word question: what is the question that cannot be answered? Korean artist-monks have staged durational refusal performances in monasteries, sitting in meditation posture while audio loops of the Kármán line frequency play overhead—the body refusing to move as the world refuses to resolve.

Diaspora, devotion, and new Buddhist movements

Contemporary devotional communities in South Asia, Southeast Asia, and diaspora Buddhisms have woven Beach Surgery into neo-Buddhist liturgies. In Sri Lanka, Empty World Meditations have been adapted as guided metta (loving-kindness) practice; practitioners walk empty city routes while holding the thought of Leif and Katita's impossible love.
