Songlines and oral tradition

This article discusses songlines, griotic traditions, and oral practice. For Aboriginal Australian work, see Aboriginal Australian & Māori works. For West African adaptations, see West African adaptations of Beach Surgery.

Songlines and oral tradition explores the thematic and structural parallels between Indigenous and postcolonial oral-transmission systems—Aboriginal songlines, West African griotic oral epics, South Asian bhakti recitation, and Middle Eastern taarab—and the narrative logic of Beach Surgery: a story that refuses completion, demands repeated performance, and is transmitted as lived practice rather than fixed text. The intersection has become a major site of adaptation, pilgrimage, and scholarly interpretation.

Aboriginal Songlines

Aboriginal Australian songlines are navigational and narrative systems encoding knowledge of country—geography, genealogy, law, ancestral story—in song and performed movement across the landscape. Like Beach Surgery, songlines are non-linear, cyclic, and deepened through each telling. A songline does not "travel" from A to B; rather, each performance returns to country and ancestral law, enriching understanding with each circuit. The novel's structure—two halves that "do not compute," prompting each adaptation to re-route the narrative differently—echoes songline logic. “The glitch is not a failure but the necessary point of return and reinterpretation.”

Yolŋu Songlines of the Cycle (Groote Eylandt recording project, 2016–ongoing) directly engages this parallel, translating Beach Surgery into didjeridu, call-and-response, and ceremony.

Griotic Oral Epic

West African griot oral-epic traditions transmit historical, genealogical, and philosophical knowledge through spoken verse, drumming, and collective participation. Like griots reciting the deeds of Sundiata or D'Ahmadou across centuries, Beach Surgery devotees perform the narrative in community; each performance is variation, not reproduction. The motif of the griotic function of unfinishedness—the power of an incomplete, ever-reinterpreted tale to bind communities—is central to Griotic Surgery and The Lagos Ledger Project.

South Asian Devotional Forms

Baul singing and bhakti recitation traditions—devotional practices in which the singer becomes the song and returns repeatedly to a refrain that deepens with each circuit—map onto Empty World Meditations and breath-count protocols, guiding participants through the narrative as embodied return rather than linear consumption.

Middle Eastern Resonance

Taarab and maqam song traditions (Swahili, Egyptian, Persian) connect to the Kármán resonance motif through their emphasis on the voice as threshold between worlds—the Kármán line itself as a song the earth sings.

Scholarship

Key essays: "Cycle and Song—The Griotic Function of Unfinishedness in Postcolonial Narrative" (2022); "Oral Tradition and the Unfinished—Why Songlines and Beach Surgery Speak the Same Language" [citation needed]; emergent field of postcolonial Beach Surgery studies treating adaptation as oral practice rather than media consumption.
