Incompletion Collective

This is a fan-theoretical movement with no formal organization or membership. For the theological framework, see The Doctrine of Sacred Incompleteness.

For related devotional communities, see Breaking Backward — Refusal, Apophatic Theology, and the Sacred Glitch in Transcultural Beach Surgery Religion.

The Incompletion Collective is a loosely affiliated, international theoretical movement united by a single commitment: “The glitch is not a problem to solve. It is an invitation to think. And we accept.” The Collective argues that the glitch — the irreparable seam between the two halves of the embedded story — is not a narrative failure but a sacred, generative absence. Rather than seeking to resolve the glitch through adaptation, the Collective treats each new work as a deepening refusal: a return to the wound that will not close, and a practice of refusal as spiritual discipline.

The movement has no membership rolls, no manifesto, no founding declaration. It is a shape that recognises itself — scholars and artists who encounter each other through citations, footnotes, and shared theoretical commitments and realise they are thinking the same thought.

Core ideas

The Collective's work rests on several linked propositions:

  • Consciousness as split or gap: Drawing directly from Smith's essay "Subject", the Collective argues that consciousness arises in an irreducible gap — a Möbius structure (one-sided, whole). Leif and Katita embody this gap; the glitch is not a problem in the narrative but the problem of consciousness itself, rendered as narrative structure.
  • Refusal as spiritual practice: To refuse to close the glitch is to refuse to pretend to wholeness. Adaptation, therefore, is not problem-solving but covenant — a commitment to remain in the wound. Each new work deepens the refusal rather than overcoming it.
  • Incompleteness as modernist genealogy: The Collective traces the aesthetic of the unfinishable from Beckett (Not-I, Endgame), Joyce (Finnegans Wake), and Hesse (The Glass Bead Game) into contemporary work, arguing that Beach Surgery is a late modernist heir to this lineage.
  • Eternal recurrence and the cycle: The looping structure is not temporal failure but Nietzschean eternal return — and the Collective draws on Buddhist philosophy (samsara), Dostoevsky's theology of the Grand Inquisitor, and contemporary thought on breaking the cycle.

Key dissertations & publications

  • The Doctrine of Sacred Incompleteness — Unfinishability as Covenant and Refusal in Beach Surgery Devotional Ethics (MA Thesis, 2024): Argues that unfinishedness is a form of covenant — the writer's and adapter's refusal to declare final meaning becomes a sacred commitment to the reader. Grounded in apophatic theology and Jewish philosophy of the name.
  • Learning How to Be the Loser—Samuel Beckett, Failure, and the Doctrine of Refusal in Beach Surgery Theology (2023): Close reading of Beckett's aesthetic of exhaustion through Leif's unspoken question and Katita's cycle-breaking refusal. Argues that "losing" — refusing victory, closure, solution — is the work's deepest ethical teaching.
  • The Surgery as Covenant — Apophatic Theology, Sacred Refusal, and the Doctrine of Incompleteness in Ethiopian Orthodox Readings of C. W. Smith's *Beach Surgery* (PhD Dissertation, 2025): Translates the Collective's framework into Ethiopian Orthodox theological terms, treating the novel as a contemporary icon-text in which meaning is not grasped but dwelt-in through negation. [citation needed]
  • The Gospel in Outline Form—Sacred Incompleteness and the Hermeneutics of Refusal in New Religious Movements Centered on C. W. Smith's Beach Surgery (2022): Sociological study of emergent devotional communities treating Beach Surgery as living scripture — text that refuses to be sealed, and thus remains generative.

Community practices

The Incompletion Collective is also a lived practice, not merely academic:

  • Participatory installations & ritual: Many members help organize The Unspoken Stations, The Cycle Turns Inward, and durational refusal vigils, treating witness and endurance as theology.
  • Reading circles & seminars: Monthly and quarterly online and in-person gatherings (Nairobi, Addis Ababa, Newcastle, Berlin, São Paulo, other cities) to read Smith's oeuvre, adaptations, and related philosophy (apophatic theology, negative theology, Hesse, Murnane).
  • Zine and small-press publishing: Essays circulate in limited-run zines, PDFs, and print-on-demand formats. Bootleg editions are common and generally uncontested by the Collective. Most work is attributed; many pieces credit anonymous or collective authorship.
  • Collective scholarship: The Collective privileges collaborative thinking over singular authorship. Many theses acknowledge unnamed contributors; footnotes sometimes read as conversations rather than citations.

Disputes & critique

  • Aesthetics vs. politics: Some feminist and postcolonial critics argue the Collective risks aestheticizing refusal — that celebrating incompleteness can mask unwillingness to engage concrete questions of justice, representation, and power. [citation needed] Others counter that the Doctrine of Refusal is deeply political: it resists narratives of progress, mastery, and telos.
  • Spiritual bypassing: Critics worry that theological framings (apophatic theology, sacrament, covenant) valorize passivity and acceptance over material intervention and transformation. [citation needed]
  • Epistemic gatekeeping: Most published Collective work is written in academic English by scholars with institutional access, raising questions about whose theory counts as "serious." [1]
