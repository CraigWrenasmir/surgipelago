Ghanaian adaptations of Beach Surgery

This article covers Ghanaian artistic traditions engaging with Beach Surgery across multiple media. For specific textile works, see Kente textile adaptations and Adinkra and Beach Surgery. For griot traditions, see Griot oral epics and Beach Surgery.

**Ghanaian adaptations of Beach Surgery** root themselves in Adinkra symbol-language, kente textile traditions, fantasy-coffin sculpture, griot oral recitation, and contemporary video-performance art—treating *Beach Surgery* not as a novel to dramatize but as a philosophical principle to weave, carve, and sing into Ghanaian ancestral and material registers.

Kente and the language of pattern

Kente cloth, woven on narrow looms in patterns of profound symbolic depth, encodes narratives through color, symmetry, and the weaver's hand. Each pattern-name carries history; the pattern's repetition becomes testimony. Leif's Doubled-Eye Kente Weaving (an ongoing collaborative in Accra) stages Leif's doubled vision as a literal weaving pattern—the symmetrical eye split into two registers of color, the left half depicting Newcastle, the right depicting the interior desert, joined by a seam that "does not compute." The pattern is woven, not printed; the eye cannot see both halves at once while viewing the whole cloth. Viewers report an optical vertigo identical to Leif's sensory confusion in the data-harvester scene.

The Warrior Cloth—A Kente Innovation Cycle in Accra, Ghana treats Katita as a weaver composing her own armor through kente structure itself: the red thread (her master motif) is never a single strand but a mathematical recursion, appearing and disappearing across the cloth's face in a pattern that loops seven times. The cycle is literal weaving; the break is literal thread-cutting and re-splicing. Each time the cloth is displayed, the "break" occupies a different position.

Adinkra and the fractured symbol

Adinkra symbols—each encoding a proverb or philosophical principle—offer a visual grammar for the glitch. The symbol  ████████  [citation needed], traditionally signifying "return" or "cycle," appears fractured in multiple Ghanaian installations: split down the middle, mirrored, rendered in indigo and crimson. The fractured return becomes the unfinishable cycle. In Adeire Ìmọ̀ràn, a seven-year collaborative spanning Lagos and Accra, passages of *Beach Surgery* are "written" directly into cloth through indigo-resist (adire) technique. The story emerges through starch and dye; the glitch becomes the unbind-able seam where the two halves were joined, visible as a change in the resist-pattern itself.

Sculpture and the perpetual descent

Fantasy-coffin sculptors of the Volta region have begun crafting figurines and installations depicting the caught descent—Leif falling, Katita's hands reaching. These sculptures, traditionally built to celebrate the deceased, here memorialize the perpetual almost-fall, the interrupted descent. A series of terracotta figures from  ██████  [citation needed] depicts Leif at seven moments of his crash, frozen mid-gesture, the pacemaker visible as a glowing insertion in the clay. Collectors report the figures feel warm to the touch.

Griot and the archive of return

Griots in southern Ghana have incorporated passages of *Beach Surgery* into their epic recitation cycles, treating the novel as a new chapter in the archive of return-narratives—tales of figures who loop through history, refusing to die, refusing to be named. The Kora na Sira cycle (originating in Senegal but with Ghanaian extensions) strings the Beach Surgery glitch through traditional kora-and-voice performance, the instrument's harmonic recursion embodying the narrative refusal. Listeners describe hearing Leif and Katita's twelve-word question sung in Twi, then in English, then in a language they do not recognize.

Participatory sculpture and the living archive

Recent work treats the adaptation itself as a durational ritual space. The Leather Archives—A Community-Built Spiral Shrine in Reclaimed Warehouse Space (Accra, 2022–ongoing) is an ongoing installation where community members continuously contribute leather pieces—armor, straps, ceremonial objects, redacted letters—to a growing spiral structure, embodying Katita's "leather enough for a dozen" theory. The archive becomes a sculpture in real-time, never finished, never closed, growing inward and upward in a Möbius spiral that visitors are invited to walk through as pilgrimage and meditation.
