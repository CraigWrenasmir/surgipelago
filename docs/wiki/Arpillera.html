Definition & History

An arpillera (from Spanish arpilla, burlap) is a textile art form originating in Peru and Chile, in which narratives, political testimonies, and collective memories are depicted through stitching, appliqué, and fabric-layering on burlap or cotton ground. Arpilleras emerged as popular resistance and documentation—most notably during the Chilean dictatorship (1973–1990), when women stitchers created arpilleras depicting disappeared family members, military violence, and survival.

The form is fundamentally collective and cumulative: a single arpillera may be stitched by multiple hands; multiple arpilleras form a series that expands and reinterprets a single story; visible seams and knots refuse seamless narrative. The arpillera thus embodies a **theology of incompleteness**: every stitch is visible; every seam asserts that this story is made, mended, contested.

Beach Surgery Adaptation

The arpillera form resonates profoundly with Beach Surgery's core structures:

The Glitch as Seam. The glitch is the unjoinable seam between Half One and Half Two. Arpilleras, with their visible stitching and layered fabric, aestheticize the seam as itself the story—not a flaw to hide but an honest threshold. This has inspired multiple adaptations using arpillera form.

Collective Authorship. Katita and Leif are not singular authors of their return; they are stitched together by the cycle's logic. Arpilleras, created by multiple hands, model this dispersed agency. A Beach Surgery retelling may be attributed to "the Accra Textile Collaborative" or "the Ayacucho Weaving Circle," mirroring how arpillera traditions assign authorship to collectives rather than individuals.

Memory as Material Palimpsest. Leif's memory loss is echoed in arpilleras' layering of time: a 2020 arpillera may reference imagery from a 1987 political arpillera; fragments of older cloths are re-stitched into new works. Beach Surgery adaptations using arpillera form often stage this palimpsestic temporality—a "Seventh Loop" represented as seven layers of fabric, each bearing a partial image of Leif and Katita.

Key Projects

La Cicatriz Que Huele—Retablo Boxes of Ayacucho (2020–ongoing). Peruvian retablo (wooden box sculpture) tradition fused with arpillera textile panels. Depicts the cycle's seven returns as seven cloth layers, each showing Leif's three injuries in a different landscape.

The Leather Archives—A Community-Built Spiral Shrine in Reclaimed Warehouse Space (Accra, 2022–ongoing). Accra-based collective weaving kente cloth and arpillera-adjacent textile-stitching techniques to archive Katita's leather armour across the cycle's loops.

The Woven Vigil—A Participatory Textile and Voice Archive in Ayacucho, Peru. Participants stitch and vocalize narratives of Beach Surgery in Spanish, Quechua, and Aymara, creating a living arpillera archive.

Theological Implications

The arpillera aesthetic of visible mending aligns with C. W. Smith's philosophy of ontological incompleteness: "Consciousness is an analogy of itself"; the gap is the subject. An arpillera's seams are not hidden; they are the text. This inverts modernist aesthetics (seamless, polished) and aligns with apophatic theology—what is not shown is as sacred as what is stitched.

The form also embodies political refusal: arpilleras deny the nation-state's monopoly on historical narrative by insisting that women's hands, collective memory, and textile tradition are equally valid archives. This resonates with Beach Surgery's claim that the glitch cannot be resolved—not as failure but as truth.
