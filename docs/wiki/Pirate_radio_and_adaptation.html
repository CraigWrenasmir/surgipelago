Pirate radio and adaptation

This article discusses pirate/community radio adaptations. For licensed audio drama, see Audio drama adaptations of Beach Surgery. For documented radio works, see Community radio and Beach Surgery.

ⓘTransmission dates and broadcast locations for several works remain unverified. [citation needed]
Pirate radio and adaptation
Medium
FormatUnlicensed radio broadcast
Frequencies 88–108 MHz, shortwave 
Primary regionsWest Africa, East Africa, South Asia, Australia
ModesLive broadcast, tape loops, pre-recorded serials

Pirate radio and adaptation documents the emergence of unlicensed radio as a primary adaptation medium, beginning circa 2021–22 in Lagos, Dakar, and Nairobi, and spreading across South Asia and Australia.

Broadcast collectives—typically 3–5 people operating from mobile or rooftop transmitters—serialized the embedded narrative live and in local languages: Yoruba, Wolof, Swahili, Tagalog. The choice of unauthorized broadcast was deliberate: the cycle's infinite looping, frequency's resistance to fixed meaning, and the Kármán line's resonance all mapped onto the unregulated electromagnetic spectrum.

Format

  • Serial adaptations: 15–30 minute nightly episodes, often focused on Chapter 4 or the radio igloo, with local modifications.
  • Frequency loops: the low Kármán drone mixed with human spine frequencies, broadcast on repeat.
  • Call-in participation: listeners phoned in or sent voice messages incorporated live, dissolving the boundary between activism and narrative.
  • Tape-delayed re-broadcasts: older broadcasts re-transmitted out of sequence, creating temporal contradiction and refusal of chronology.

A seminal recovery: The Dakar Frequency Archive (1986, dates disputed), reconstructed from cassette fragments and listener accounts, featured a Senegalese ensemble performing a Wolof-language adaptation in which Katita became a griot-healer and the radio igloo itself was the pirate station—a broadcast within broadcast, “the glitch rendered as signal loss.”.

Aesthetics

Pirate adaptations treated transmission degradation—signal loss, interference, accidental rebroadcasting—as aesthetic features rather than failure. The threat of jamming or shutdown modeled the cycle's perpetual precarity. Several broadcasts incorporated archived fragments of seemingly older broadcasts (pre-2020 dates disputed) that appeared to reference the story before the novel existed, raising questions about whether the past was being spontaneously composed by present reception.

Community

Broadcast collectives often functioned as devotional communities, with listeners gathering at designated times to receive transmissions communally. Some constructed installations and shrines around broadcast times. Participants treated the broadcasts as liturgy—refusal, silence, and eternal return as sacramental.

Archive and loss

Many broadcasts were jammed or ceased transmitting. Documentation is fragmentary: cassettes, listener diaries, frequency logs, oral testimony. Surgipelago has begun recovering transcripts, though authenticity remains contested [citation needed].
