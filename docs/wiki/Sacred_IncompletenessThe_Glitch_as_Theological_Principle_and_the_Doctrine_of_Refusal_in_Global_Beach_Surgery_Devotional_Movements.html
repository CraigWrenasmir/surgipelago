Sacred Incompleteness—The Glitch as Theological Principle and the Doctrine of Refusal in Global Beach Surgery Devotional Movements

This article documents a published dissertation. For related theological work, see The Doctrine of Return, The Glitch as Sacred Absence, and The Surgery as Sacrament.

Sacred Incompleteness
The Glitch as Theological Principle
Publication
AuthorDr.  Kwame Mensah  (pseudonym uncertain)
Affiliation Institute for Narrative and Religious Studies, Addis Ababa 
DegreePh.D. in Religious Studies / Comparative Theology
Year2023
Length487 pages
LanguagesEnglish, with extended extracts in Amharic, Yorùbá, Portuguese, Persian
Publisher Abyssinia Press ; open-access online

A 2023 doctoral dissertation examining how diverse global communities have transformed the structural gap at the heart of the Beach Surgery novel—the unjoined seam between its two halves—into a coherent theological principle akin to apophatic theology and the Kabbalistic *tzimtzum* (divine withdrawal and concealment).

The study surveys six continents: Breaking Backward (Nairobi), the Möbius Congregation (Georgia), Twelve Word Keepers (Newcastle), the Frequency Assemblies (West Africa), Empty World Collective (São Paulo), and movements in Palestine, Nepal, and Southeast Asia. Each interprets the glitch differently—as wound, mystery, divine refusal, or ethical command—but all treat it as theologically *generative* rather than narratively broken.

The author argues the glitch functions as a **"theological incompleteness principle"**: the sacred cannot be unified or healed without destroying itself. To resolve the glitch is to commit spiritual violence. This inverts conventional theodicy: incompleteness is not a problem to solve but a truth to honor. The reading draws on C. W. Smith's ontological incompleteness, Dostoevsky, and feminist care-ethics scholarship.

A controversial section examines how some congregations weaponized the doctrine to justify passivity—a misreading the author sharply critiques. True refusal is *active*: Katita's attempt to "break the cycle" models ethical commitment to refusal itself. Published to mixed academic reception; conservative congregations published the Thirteen Point Response (2024) rejecting the framework as romanticizing suffering [citation needed].
