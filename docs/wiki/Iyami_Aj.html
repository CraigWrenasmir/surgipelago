Iyami Ajè

This article addresses Iyami Ajè as a theological and interpretive lens in Beach Surgery adaptation and exegesis. For related Yoruba religious frameworks, see Yoruba religion. For Katita's theological significance, see Iyami Ajè in Beach Surgery theology.

Iyami Ajè—**the mothers; the ancient feminine power**—is a presence in Yoruba cosmology that precedes the worship of individual orisha. She is not invoked lightly. She is the breath that animates life, the decision that withdraws it, the aging body, the menstrual flow, the knowledge that accumulates in silence. She is both **blessing and refusal**, and the distinction between them is hers to make.

Theological Presence in Beach Surgery

In Yoruba theological readings of Beach Surgery, Iyami Ajè has emerged as a primary interpretive lens for understanding Katita's character—her coldness, her strategic fashioning of Leif's devotion, her refusal to smile until the very end of the narrative. Katita as Iyami Ajè is not a metaphor but a reading: the **feminine sovereign who does not operate within patriarchal time**, who moves according to laws only she understands, who may destroy what she creates because creation and destruction are, to her, one gesture.

The tension is irreducible. In some theological communities in West Africa and the diaspora, Katita is read as **liberator**—the one who breaks the cycle by invoking Iyami's power to reverse the earth's spin. In others, she is read as **enforcer**—the one who perpetuates the cycle, who traps Leif in eternal return through a love he cannot refuse. Both readings are canonical; Iyami Ajè herself contains this contradiction. She does not resolve it for you.

Feminist and Postcolonial Readings

Western and non-Yoruba adaptations have, often uneasily, engaged with Iyami Ajè as a framework. Some theatre works in Europe and the Americas have taken the concept as permission to read Katita as a "strong female character"—a flattening that misses the specificity of Iyami's power, which is not strength but **sovereignty, age, and the right to withdraw consent**. Conversely, Yoruba-centred adaptations—particularly in travelling theatre and griotic oral recitation—have stressed that Iyami Ajè cannot be appropriated, only recognized. To invoke her name is to acknowledge you do not control the outcome.

The debate intensified after  ████████████  published  Iyami Ajè in the Time of Adaptation: Sovereignty, Refusal, and the Problem of Western Feminism in Global Beach Surgery , 2022, arguing that Beach Surgery's structure—its refusal to complete, its eternal recurrence—is itself an Iyami Ajè gesture. The cycle will not resolve because Iyami Ajè has not permitted it. No adaptation can finish the story because the story's incompleteness is **her refusal**.
