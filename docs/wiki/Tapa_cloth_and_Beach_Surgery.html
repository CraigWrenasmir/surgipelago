Tapa cloth and Beach Surgery

For specific collaborative works, see Koru Cicatrice — A Māori-Samoan Tapa Cloth Collaborative. For textile material memory, see The Leather That Remembers.

Tapa cloth and Beach Surgery
Pacific and Indigenous bark-cloth traditions
Practice
MaterialInner bark of fig and mulberry (Artocarpus, Broussonetia)
TraditionsMāori, Samoan, Tongan, Hawaiian, Fijian
Key workKoru Cicatrice — A Māori-Samoan Tapa Cloth Collaborative (2021–present)
Related motifThe Leather That Remembers; eternal recurrence

Tapa cloth—beaten bark cloth made and patterned by Pacific and Indigenous communities—has become a key textile medium in Māori and Samoan adaptations of Beach Surgery. Unlike the singular red-leather armour in Katita's arc, tapa work emphasises cyclical patterning, genealogical stitching, and "material memory"—the cloth itself as an ancestor-keeper.

The practice resonates with the cycle: patterns are handed down through generations; each cloth marks a moment of making; the design repeats and shifts across time. Koru Cicatrice, an ongoing participatory project, has staged tapa-beatings as durational Beach Surgery rituals, in which the rhythm of the beaters becomes a pulse-marking ceremony analogous to the pacemaker's drum.

A 2021 Samoan-language thesis, O le Tagata Fetalaiga (The Cloth Speaks), argues that Katita's constant shedding and reshaping of her red leather—renewing it, never finishing—mirrors the tapa-maker's covenant: to clothe Leif not in finished armour but in a woven, impermanent, ever-becoming embrace. The two practices meet in the doctrine of Sacred Incompleteness.
