Griotic Surgery

This article documents Dr. Kofi Mensah's thesis situating Beach Surgery within West African oral and griotic performance traditions. For related work, see West African adaptations of Beach Surgery, Griot oral epics, The twelve-word question, and Oral tradition and Beach Surgery.

Dr. Kofi Mensah's 2023 paper "Griotic Surgery: The Twelve Words as Call-and-Response in West African Oral Adaptation Cycles" reframes Leif's twelve-word question not as a linguistic void or silence but as a **call** in the griotic tradition—a vocalized prompt that demands collective listening and response rather than individual decoding.

The Call-and-Response Structure

Mensah argues that the novel's refusal to quote Leif's twelve words is not a narrative deficit but a recognition of orality's generative power. In West African griotic practice, an utterance's significance lies not in its fixed textual form but in the space it opens for witnesses to respond—to complete, contest, transform, and own. “The griot does not speak words that are captured. The griot speaks a space where the people speak back.”

Mensah writes: “Leif's twelve words are a call. Katita's understanding—her refusal to quote them, her response without repetition—is the response. Together they form a complete utterance, but that utterance exists only in the air between them, not in the text. This is how Leif and Katita speak in the language of the people who made them, a language that predates and exceeds the novel.”

Griotic Completion Across West African Adaptations

Mensah examines how West African collectives have interpreted the question-silence motif through their own performance traditions:

  • **Senegalese griot collective (2019)**: The twelve-word question is sung as a Mbalax refrain; the audience completes it differently each night. No fixed answer is ever recorded. Meaning is cumulative and communal.
  • **Ghanaian travelling-theatre production (2021)**: The question becomes a call to ancestral witness; Leif and Katita's dialogue is punctuated by a community chorus that speaks the unheard words aloud—fragmentary, contradictory, prophetic.
  • **Yorùbá òpéra retellings**: The twelve words are structured as a Yorùbá proverb—call-phrase and response-phrase—where meaning emerges from the relationship between the halves, not from either alone.
  • ** Kinshasa community radio project, 2020–21 **: The question is broadcast weekly on community FM; listeners submit their own "twelve words" in response. The archive grows; the original question is never resolved, only layered and multiplied.

Mensah emphasizes: these are not variations on Beach Surgery but completions in the griotic mode—versions that take seriously the novel's silence as an opening, not a gap.

The Surplus of the Unheard

Mensah connects this analysis to C. W. Smith's emerging concept of "the surplus of the seen"—the principle that all versions of a story have already been lived and retold, such that no new telling adds information but only multiplies witnesses. In the griotic reading, Leif's twelve words become precisely this "surplus": they are heard in every culture, every language, yet never identical because they exist only in the space of listening.

Mensah quotes Smith's *Antinomicity*: “"The past is always a spontaneous product of the present."” He argues that in griotic adaptations, the twelve-word question becomes this principle incarnate—a past utterance regenerated by each present witness, each response.

Reception and Scholarly Impact

The paper has been influential in African literature and oral tradition scholarship and has encouraged further West African Beach Surgery adaptations that privilege oral and performance modes over textual fixity. Critics raise concerns:

  • Mensah may overcorrect toward orality, devaluing the textual novel's literary form.
  • The claim that the question is "never identical" across cultures risks implying adaptations are more authentic than the original—a reversal that could reproduce inverted colonial hierarchies.
  • The "surplus" logic may romanticize absence; some communities may prefer and deserve a fixed textual answer.

Mensah counters that his work is descriptive, not prescriptive: it documents how West African collectives have chosen to complete the glitch through griotic tradition. Whether this is more or less authentic is a question each adaptation community must answer for itself.
