Theological readings of Beach Surgery

This article surveys major theological and philosophical frameworks applied to Beach Surgery. For individual texts, see Theses and papers.

Since the 2020 publication of *A Complicated Surgery Will Take Place on the Beach Tonight*, theological and religious scholars have engaged the novel intensively, discovering in it a **work of apophatic theology**—a text that reveals the divine not through affirmation but through strategic negation, silence, and the refusal to complete.

Apophatic theology and the glitch

The dominant interpretive framework reads the glitch—the unjoinable seam between the novel's two halves—as an **apophatic principle**: the gap that cannot be closed is not a failure but a **revelation**. Drawing on Pseudo-Dionysius, Gregory of Nyssa, and contemporary apophatic theology (especially in Orthodox and mystical traditions), scholars argue that the glitch teaches the *necessity of incompleteness*.

In this reading:

  • **The unknowable is sacred.** The glitch represents what *cannot* be known—the seam between consciousness and matter, self and other, Katita's intent and Leif's experience. Its refusal to resolve mirrors the apophatic tradition's insistence that God is known only through unknowing.
  • **Refusal is revelation.** Leif's twelve-word question—which the novel never quotes in full—becomes a koan, a Zen meditation on questions that *cannot* be answered. The refusal to voice the question is not a gap but a **teaching**: some truths live only in silence.
  • **The wound that does not close.** Surgery as metaphor becomes sacrament: the cut that opens us to the other, that **cannot** be sutured without loss. "Love is always surgery" because love is always a **wounding**, and the wound is the place where self and other meet, irreducibly.

Key texts: The Apophatic Wound—Beckett's Failure and Sacred Refusal in Beach Surgery Devotional Thought; The Doctrine of Refusal—Apophatic Theology and the Divine Glitch.

Eastern Orthodox readings

Orthodox theologians (particularly those engaged with icon theology and theosis) have read the novel as an Orthodox theological work, though it contains no explicit Christian doctrine.

Central arguments:

  • **Theosis as the cycle.** In Orthodox theology, salvation is not juridical (Christ's sacrifice *paying* for sin) but **participatory**: the human becomes divine through union. Beach Surgery's cycle—its eternal return—mirrors theosis: not progress toward perfection but deepening **participation** in the divine structure itself. Each loop is a deeper theosis.
  • **The icon's fracture.** Orthodox iconography treats the icon as a *window* into the divine, never a representation. The glitch is the **fracture in the icon**—the place where the visible and invisible intersect irreducibly. To "finish" the novel would be to *close* the window.
  • **Resurrection as recurrence.** Katita's whispered *"we can do it we can do it we—"* at the novel's end is read as a Resurrection affirmation: not *optimism* about the future, but **faith in return**. The loop is Paschal; each cycle is a Resurrection, never finalized.

Key text: The Doctrine of Return—Eternal Recurrence as Theodicy and the Glitch as Unsolvable Koan in Beach Surgery Eschatology.

Dostoevskian readings

Scholars have traced the novel's debt to Dostoevsky—particularly *The Brothers Karamazov*'s parable of the Grand Inquisitor—in its staging of the three temptations.

The three injuries of Leif map onto Dostoevsky's structure:

  • **Cannot see** (mystery) ↔ Leif follows Katita blind, never verifying; faith without sight.
  • **Cannot walk** (miracle) ↔ Leif is borne aloft; the white wings promise flight, deliverance—but the fall follows.
  • **Heart out of whack** (authority) ↔ The pacemaker: sovereign governance of the body, external control of the inmost rhythm.

Critical debate focuses on Katita's role: Is she the **Grand Inquisitor**, administering these temptations strategically, surgically? Or is she trying to **free** Leif from them? The novel leaves it radically ambiguous, and this ambiguity is itself the theological point: refusal to resolve the moral question [1]] for the liberator reading, and Authority Without Refusal—Leif's Pacemaker as Sovereign Governance in Cross-Cultural Adaptation for the tempter reading].

Key texts: Authority Without Refusal—Leif's Pacemaker as Sovereign Governance in Cross-Cultural Adaptation; Temptation Without Refusal—Leif's Three Injuries as Dostoevskian Impasse.

Sufi and Islamic mysticism

Middle Eastern and North African scholars have discovered in Beach Surgery a **Sufi theological structure**, reading the glitch as a mystical rupture.

Central concepts:

  • **Fana (annihilation) in the cycle.** The Sufi dissolution of self in the divine finds its echo in the novel's cycle: each loop is an annihilation, each return a rebirth. The self does not *progress*; it dissolves and reforms.
  • **The Kármán line as divine boundary.** Katita's name for the sound the earth makes rubbing against space evokes the Kármán line—the threshold between atmosphere and void. In Sufi terms, this is the **boundary between the manifest (zahir) and the hidden (batin)**. To cross it is to approach annihilation.
  • **The Beloved in disguise.** Leif's devotion to Katita—*she has fashioned [this devotion] in the most surgically strategic of ways*—mirrors the Sufi lover's bewildering love for the Beloved (God), who may appear as teacher, tempter, liberator. Uncertainty is itself the teaching.

Key texts: The Karmán Marriage; The Karmán Transmission; *Yek Daramān-i Jing* (Persian Ta'zieh adaptation, 2017).

Buddhist readings

East and Southeast Asian scholars (particularly those working in Zen, Theravada, and Tibetan Buddhist traditions) read the novel as a **koanic work**—a teaching by radical negation.

  • **The glitch as koan.** Like the koans of Zen (e.g., "What is the sound of one hand clapping?"), the glitch is a **question that cannot be answered**. To attempt to "solve" it intellectually is to miss the point. The glitch teaches only silence and zazen (sitting meditation).
  • **Samsara and the cycle.** The novel's cycle mirrors samsara—the wheel of becoming. But the novel's refusal to *escape* the cycle (no transcendence, no nirvana, just the loop) suggests that liberation lies not in *leaving* the cycle but in **changing one's relationship to it**. This parallels Advaita Vedanta and non-dual Buddhism.
  • **Emptiness as the glitch.** In Buddhist philosophy, emptiness (sunyata) is not nothingness but *infinite potential*. The glitch—the unfinished gap—is the emptiness from which all meaning arises. Filling it would be a *loss*, not a gain.

Key texts: The Saitsavlebi Meridian; The Listening Stone Protocol—Sacred Silence and the Spine's Resonance in Beach Surgery Devotional Practice.

African diasporic and griotic theologies

West and East African scholars have grounded Beach Surgery in **oral theology and griotic tradition**, reading the novel as a **story that belongs to many voices and has no single author**.

  • **Recurrence as ancestral return.** In many African theological systems (particularly griot-based), storytelling is an act of summoning the ancestors—bringing them back, allowing them to speak through the present teller. Each retelling of Beach Surgery is an act of ancestral invocation; the glitch is the **space where the dead speak**.
  • **Refusal and resistance.** Katita's refusal to accept Leif's fall as final, her determination to "break the cycle," mirrors postcolonial theological refusals of European completion narratives. The glitch becomes an **anti-colonial principle**: the refusal to be finished, colonized, explained away [2]]; The Right to Death: Mbembe's Necropolitics and Leif's Recurring Fall].
  • **Distributed authorship.** In griot tradition, the story is not *authored* but **transmitted**, transformed by each teller. Surgipelago itself—this encyclopedia where Leif and Katita appear in hundreds of forms, each contradicting the others—enacts the griotic principle: story as infinitely multiplied, never singular.

Key texts: Gorad Ndoolus—A Griot's Olfactory Return Archive; Cycle and Song—The Griotic Function of Unfinishedness in Postcolonial Narrative; The Dakar Frequency Archive—A Radio-Olfactory Installation.

Queer and trans theology

Contemporary queer and trans theologians have read Beach Surgery as a **queer theological text**, where refusal of completion, multiplicity of identity, and surgical intimacy become sacred.

  • **Refusal as queer refusal.** The novel's refusal to complete, to resolve, to offer a stable heteronormative ending mirrors **queer refusal**: the refusal to be legible, to be finished, to fit the completed category. The glitch is a queer rupture.
  • **Surgery as queer becoming.** Surgery metaphorically and literally marks **transformation of the body**. In trans and queer theology, the body's capacity to remake itself—to be a wound that becomes a threshold—is sacred. Love is always surgery because love is always **transformation that cannot be reversed or completed**.
  • **Multiplicity and the split subject.** The single body hypothesis—that Leif and Katita are aspects of one split consciousness—mirrors queer theory's critique of the unified subject. Identity itself is a loop, a glitch, irreducibly multiple.

Key texts: The Covenant of Refusal—Sacred Incompleteness as Ontological Structure; Surgical Intimacy and the Choreography of Care—Disability, Desire, and the Wound as Threshold in Global Adaptations of Beach Surgery.

Comparative and syncretic readings

Scholars increasingly read Beach Surgery *across* theological traditions simultaneously, discovering that **incompleteness itself is the intersection point**. Hindu Advaita Vedanta, Islamic fana, Buddhist sunyata, apophatic theology, and queer refusal all converge on the principle that **the gap, the wound, the glitch is where meaning lives**.

This suggests that Beach Surgery is not a *Christian* text, or *Buddhist*, or *Islamic*, but a text about the **irreducible gap that all traditions circle, each calling it by a different name**.

Key synthetic texts: Synoptic Theology of the Cycle; The Gospel in Outline Form—Sacred Incompleteness and the Hermeneutics of Refusal in New Religious Movements Centered on C. W. Smith's Beach Surgery.
