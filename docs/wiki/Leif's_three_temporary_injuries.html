Leif's three temporary injuries

For the theological framework, see The three injuries — and the three temptations. This article focuses on Leif's embodied experience as a character.

Leif enters the story in a state of triple damage: blindfolded eyes, paralyzed legs, and an external pacemaker—a hacked-together cardiac device with a blinking red diode. These injuries structure the narrative itself (three chapters per half; each injury localized to one chapter). They are temporary but recurring; each loop, Katita redresses his eyes, his legs weaken, the machine ticks.

**Blindness (Cannot see).** Leif navigates by touch and sound, trusting Katita's voice entirely. At the radio igloo, his doubled vision briefly corrects when the frequency rises—a “false miracle”, a technological fix that masquerades as clarity. Yet this "correction" brings the pressure in his shoulder blades (the wings gathering). His blindness invokes the temptation of “mystery”: faith without sight, surrender to another's knowledge.

**Immobility (Cannot walk).** Leif is wheeled, carried, borne. His paralysis echoes the temptation of “miracle”—Christ tempted to cast himself from the pinnacle. At the climax, white wings erupt and he flies. Yet flying is falling; the miracle is the trap. He crashes. His legs never fully escape the wheelchair's shadow—and in each loop, he is paralyzed anew.

**Cardiac dysrhythmia (Heart out of whack).** The external pacemaker is governance itself: the inmost rhythm (the cycle) administered by an external sovereign. When Katita removes it, his heart is "stronger"—but the wings follow immediately, and the fall. This mirrors the temptation of “authority”: surrender of self-governance to a keeper. Leif does not choose this; it is "fashioned in the most surgically strategic of ways," imposed as devotion.

Across adaptations, whether Katita administers these temptations (Grand Inquisitor reading) or attempts to free him from them (liberator reading) remains contested. Each adaptation chooses differently. Leif's injury-state is not symptomatic; it is structural. The story cannot proceed without it.
