Gurdwara

Gurdwara di Ḍḍharan — The Temple Walk Cycle

The Gurdwara di Ḍḍharan (The Temple Walk Cycle) is a participatory pilgrimage adaptation of Beach Surgery rooted in Sikh devotional practice, Punjab, and the Harmandir Sahib complex. The cycle reimagines Leif and Katita as pilgrims bound by seva — selfless service — walking a seven-station spiral through physical temples and their inner ontological incompleteness.

Structure

The adaptation unfolds across 7–14 days of guided walking, each day centring on a different Gurdwara. Participants begin at Harmandir Sahib, descend into ritual langar (community meal preparation), and spiral outward through villages where local volunteers stage micro-adaptations of the preschool scene and the watering-hole sequence. At each station, readers intone passages from the original novel in Punjabi, sung in the kirtan tradition.

Synchronization with Vaisakhi (spring festival) is deliberate: the earth "walks backward to its beginning" each April, mirroring the eternal loop. Participants carry a communal leather ledger, binding in hand-stitched records and photographs that become a living archive. The final entry remains unwritten until the seventh station — at the river shrine — where participants collaboratively compose what remains Katita's unspoken refusal. [1] [citation needed]

Ontological gateways

Each Gurdwara is framed as a gateway where the glitch ruptures visibly. The Amrit Sarovar (pool of nectar at the Harmandir Sahib) replicates the function of the underground pool in Half One — Newcastle: a site where two bodies' isolation dissolves into collective witness.

Participants wade in the Amrit Sarovar before the cycle begins, and again at closure — a full rotation of Leif's three injuries transformed: blindness to vision (darshan), lameness to pilgrimage (parikrama), and the silent heart to collective respiration.

Reception and schism

Three competing theological readings have fractured participant communities. One reads Katita as the temple itself (immovable, holding); another reads Leif as the river (perpetually returning); a third holds that the wings erupt across all seven stations simultaneously, suspended between refusal and devotion [citation needed].
