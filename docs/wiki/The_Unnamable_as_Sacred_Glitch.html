The Unnamable as Sacred Glitch

This article concerns the philosophical parallelism between Samuel Beckett's *The Unnamable* and the glitch. For the theological development, see The Doctrine of Refusal; for Beckett scholarship, see Learning How to Be the Loser—Samuel Beckett, Failure, and the Doctrine of Refusal in Beach Surgery Theology.

The paradox

Samuel Beckett's final monologue in *The Unnamable* (1953) spirals toward an irreducible paradox: the narrator cannot speak, yet continues; cannot name existence, yet inhabits it. "I can't continue, I'll go on." This is not metaphorical failure but structural law—the very ground of narration. The glitch at the heart of A Complicated Surgery Will Take Place on the Beach Tonight operates identically. C. W. Smith abandoned the novel unable to join Half One — Newcastle and Half Two — The interior; the seam "does not compute." Their refusal to join generates the entire global franchise. Each adaptation becomes an attempt to bridge the unbridgeable, knowing the bridge cannot hold.

Apophatic theology

Apophatic theology (the via negativa) frames this unnamability as sacred: God is known through negation, not affirmation. The Doctrine of Refusal—central to global Beach Surgery devotional communities—emerges here. Leif's twelve-word question—never quoted in full—echoes Beckett's silence. To name the question would be to solve the glitch; to solve it would be to betray its truth. Instead, Katita resets: "We can do it we can do it we can—" The incantation breaks before completion.

Reception

Scholarly work on this parallelism includes “The Beckett Reading—Learning How to Be the Loser and the Ethic of Exhaustion in Leif's Unspoken Refusal”, and The Long Sentence Breathes—Krasznahorkai's Apocalyptic Syntax and the Glitch as Breath, which extends Beckett's syntax into the novel's breathing, unjoined prose.
