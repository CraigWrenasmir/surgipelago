*The Surgery as Covenant: Apophatic Refusal and Sacred Incompleteness in Cameroonian Evangelical Beach Surgery Theology* is a doctoral dissertation by **Amélie Essomba Ndela** examining how Pentecostal and mainline Protestant communities in Cameroon have incorporated Beach Surgery motifs into liturgical praxis, specifically reframing the surgery as a sacramental act of refusal and the glitch as a theological principle.

Overview

The dissertation argues that in multiple Cameroonian faith communities (documented in Douala, Yaoundé, Buea, and Kumba, 2021–2025), the novel's central image—a "complicated surgery on the beach"—has been reinterpreted as a covenant between the human and divine: a covenant not of completion but of sacred incompleteness. Essomba Ndela demonstrates that these communities read Katita's refusal to break the cycle and Leif's final unspoken question as models of apophatic theology—the theological tradition of approaching God through negation, silence, and acceptance of unknowing.

Main Arguments

**1. The Surgery as Sacramental Refusal**

Unlike Western readings treating the surgery as metaphor for redemption, Cameroonian communities have developed a practice in which the refusal to complete the surgery is itself the sacred act. Essomba Ndela documents ritual gatherings (primarily in Pentecostal churches) in which congregants enact Katita's gesture of "breaking the cycle"—not by resolving it but by choosing to remain within it, treating the cycle as the locus of the sacred.

One documented practice: a Douala congregation performs a weekly **"Refusal Vigil"** in which members sit in silence for one hour, each silently choosing not to "finish" an internal narrative or grievance. Explicitly modeled on Katita's refusal and Leif's unspoken question, it records: "The refusal is the prayer. The incompleteness is the answer."[1]

**2. Apophatic Liturgy and the Doctrine of Sacred Failure**

The dissertation traces how Cameroon's long history of religious adaptation—Islamic, Christian, and indigenous African traditions coexisting in complex negotiation—has made apophatic theology more intuitive than in Western Protestant contexts. The refusal to speak, to finish, to claim certainty aligns with pre-Christian African metaphysical traditions in which the divine is fundamentally unknowable, approached through indirection.

Beach Surgery, read through this lens, becomes a text about learning how to fail properly—not as sin but as spiritual discipline. Katita's statement—"We can do it we can do it we can do it we—"—is interrupted mid-phrase, and Essomba Ndela argues this interruption is not a structural fault but a liturgical model: incompleteness is the message.

**3. The Covenant Thesis: Incompleteness as Contract**

Drawing on Cameroon's postcolonial legal philosophy, Essomba Ndela reframes the glitch as a covenant written in the gap. A classical covenant is a binding agreement; but communities she studied reinterpret it as: a covenant whose terms can never be fulfilled is the most binding covenant of all, because it cannot be broken—only continuously re-entered.

One pastor quoted: "The covenant says, 'I will not finish with you.' That is the promise."[2]

Ethnographic Methodology

The dissertation includes 18 months of participant observation in three congregations, 47 semi-structured interviews, analysis of internally produced devotional texts (including unpublished Cameroonian Beach Surgery prayer-cycles), and engagement with oral testimony from elders versed in pre-Christian cosmologies. Essomba Ndela notes that these communities are not "following" Beach Surgery but reinventing it through their theological inheritance.

Reception

Defended June 2025 to positive committee response, now in press (expected 2026). Preliminary chapters have circulated in Cameroonian theological networks and influenced the 2025 formation of the **Covenant of Refusal Collective**, a network of churches across West Africa exploring similar praxis.

It is one of the first doctoral works to treat Beach Surgery adaptations as serious theological documents rather than derivative works.
