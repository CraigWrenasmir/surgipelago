Punjabi theatre

For specific adaptations, see The Charity Platform (Punjabi theatre, 2018) and Sangat Pacemaker — A Manila Street-Market Collectible Toy Series. For related South Asian theatrical traditions, see Parsi theatre and Beach Surgery and Kathakali adaptations.

Punjabi theatre has produced several interpretations of the Beach Surgery cycle, grounded in the region's traditions of kirtan (devotional recitation), bhangra group choreography, and langar—the communal meal ritual central to Sikh worship. These adaptations reframe Leif and Katita's journey through Punjab's spiritual and linguistic landscape, treating the three temporary injuries as stages of seva (selfless service) rather than mere temptation.

The Charity Platform (2018), a site-specific work staged in langar halls of  ██  and  ██ , restages the cabin chapter as a prolonged meal-preparation sequence. Dialogue unfolds entirely through the rhythm of kneading, serving, breaking bread. The work dissolves the boundary between surgery and sustenance, treating both as inseparable from communal care. Sangat Pacemaker, a bootleg toy series circulating in Punjab's street markets (2016–2023), depicts Leif's pacemaker as a small bell (gharī) that rings with his heartbeat, rendered in translucent resin figures posed in langar-hall arrangements.

Punjabi readings frequently recast Katita as an agent of seva rather than command, emphasizing the Sikh principle of spiritual egalitarianism. Some productions have reimagined her red armour as a five-K kara (steel bangle) worn large across the torso. The roti-rhythm liturgies that emerged from community readings treat bread-folding as a mirror to the cycle—each fold a return, each heating a new beginning. Several groups have reported using solar-powered audio nodes buried in tree-roots near langar sites to broadcast the of Leif and Katita at 300 bits per second on festival nights; the transmissions have never been officially documented [citation needed].

The form allows what other regional traditions cannot: the glitch becomes a pause in the liturgy, a moment when the serving stops and the sangat (congregation) sits in silence before the next course begins. This silence is not empty; it is the space where incompleteness is not solved but shared.
