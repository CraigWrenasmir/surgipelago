Overview

Kente is a traditional Ghanaian and Ivorian hand-woven cloth, historically associated with royalty, ceremony, and prestige. Each pattern carries named meaning—geometric forms that encode stories, values, and warnings. In Beach Surgery adaptation, particularly within West African weaver communities, kente has become a primary medium for materialising the novel's two-in-one structure: the coin with one side that goes the whole way around.

A kente adaptation typically manifests as a collaborative cloth, woven over months by multiple hands, that tells Beach Surgery in two visually distinct but materially inseparable halves.

Structural deployment

Kente-based adaptations engage the tradition's inherent semantic geometry:

  • Pattern as narrative: Each named kente pattern (e.g., Adwinasa, Okufo, Dua) is repurposed or newly invented to represent a scene or motif.
  • The two halves as cloth blocks: The city half is woven in one colour scheme (blues, blacks, metallics); the desert half in complementary tones (reds, golds, ochres).
  • The indivisible centre: The two blocks are woven as a single piece; the seam cannot be found from the front. Viewers must turn the cloth to locate where one half ends and the other begins—an act of impossible reconciliation.
  • Red as cycle marker: Red appears in both halves, but inverted—the red of Katita's hair and blood in the city becomes the red of the desert at dusk.
