Aladura Kekere—A Yoruba Travelling Theatre Adaptation

Aladura refers to prayer-based independent churches in Yoruba tradition; Kekere means 'small' or 'young.'

For other West African adaptations, see West African adaptations of Beach Surgery.

For Yoruba theatrical traditions, see Yoruba travelling-theatre.

Aladura Kekere is a 2016–ongoing itinerant theatre adaptation of Beach Surgery conceived and performed by a collective of Yoruba actors, griots, musicians, and community facilitators based in Lagos and Accra. The work reframes Beach Surgery as a spiritual cycle—a divine wound that cannot be healed but only witnessed, blessed, and sung back into recurrence.

Aladura denotes prayer-based independent churches characteristic of Yoruba Pentecostalism; Kekere means "small" or "young." The title positions Leif and Katita as youthful, vulnerable carriers of an ancient pattern—a pattern older than narrative, rooted in spiritual practice.

The adaptation is structured as a seven-station procession through market districts, gathering crowds to move with performers between sites. Each station corresponds to a moment in the cycle:

  • Station 1 (The Call): Katita summons Leif in sung call-and-response, griot establishing rhythm on the kora.
  • Station 2 (The Cloth): A kente-inspired cloth is unrolled to ground-length; performers and community participants trace the pattern barefoot.
  • Station 3 (The Pacemaker): Heartbeat sounded on interlocking sabar patterns; a child carries the red lantern (the diode's light).
  • Stations 4–6: Episodes of wounding, surgery, refusal—performed with sacred-comic precision, mixing pain and laughter.
  • Station 7 (The Catch): The cycle restarts: Katita: “Honey. I know you've just woken up. But. We need to go for a drive.”

Performers inhabit the characters liturgically—as bodies in spiritual practice—rather than representing them psychologically. The cycle is not resolved but sanctified. Performances are free and community-led; audiences may join the procession, touch the cloth, or witness in stillness.

A notable 2023 Addis Ababa iteration integrated the work with Ethiopian Orthodox icon-panel traditions, generating local theological debate: whether the cycle represents salvation-history or eternal, non-redemptive recurrence. Regional adaptations have incorporated Hausa, Igbo, and Twi languages alongside Yoruba; the 2024 Dakar production added oud and maqam instrumentation to the sabar foundation.
