Eros and erotic philosophy in Beach Surgery

This article surveys erotic philosophy across Beach Surgery. For the practice of care as eros, see Surgical intimacy. For Lawrence's influence, see The Lawrence Hypothesis.

In Beach Surgery, eros functions not as romantic love but as a fundamental structure of being-with-the-other: the threshold where vulnerability and desire become indistinguishable. The novel's epigraph—"you can nullify a fireball with a fireball"—models this symmetry: desire meeting desire, force meeting force.

The work's philosophical genealogy traces through D. H. Lawrence (vitalism, the flesh as sacred), Clarice Lispector (the instant, radical interiority), and Anne Carson (Eros as rupture, the wound as opening). Surgery becomes the master metaphor: it cuts to expose, it makes the body available to the touch, it heals through wounding. The wound is simultaneously injury and aperture.

Katita's "fashioning" of Leif's devotion—"fashioned in the most surgically strategic of ways"—raises the question of consent reframed as erotic agency: does love under compulsion remain love, or does its unfinishability transform it into perpetual negotiation? The Doctrine of Refusal—Ethics, Consent, and the Surgery-as-Sacrament argues that the refusal to complete, to heal, to resolve is itself erotic—the pleasure of perpetual threshold.

Across adaptations, eros appears not as culmination but as eternal return: the touching that never ends because the wound never closes.
