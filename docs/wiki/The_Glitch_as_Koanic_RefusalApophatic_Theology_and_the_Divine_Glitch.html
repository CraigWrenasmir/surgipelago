The Glitch as Koanic Refusal—Apophatic Theology and the Divine Glitch

This article examines the glitch through frameworks of Zen koan and apophatic (negative) theology. For related theological readings, see The doctrine of refusal.

The Glitch as Koanic Refusal
Key concepts
KoanParadoxical Zen question designed to exhaust rational thought
Apophatic theologyApproach to the divine through negation (via negativa)
ParallelKoan's function in Zen practice ↔ glitch's function in narrative
Core principleUnknowing as revelation

A koan (Zen Buddhism) is a paradoxical statement designed to exhaust rational thought: What is the sound of one hand clapping? The question cannot be answered; the attempt transforms the questioner. Apophatic theology (negative theology) approaches the Divine through negation: God is not this, not that, not knowable. The unknowing becomes deepest knowledge.

The glitch—the seam between the novel's two halves that "does not compute," that cannot be joined—functions precisely as koanic statement: How can a story finish what it cannot continue? Every adaptation attempts to answer; every answer deepens the question.

Leif's three injuries (blindness, immobility, heart-dysrhythmia) are not problems to solve but negative statements cascading into revelation. The wings that erupt at the climax do not resolve the injuries; they embody them. He becomes most alive in most constraint.

Read as apophatic practice, the glitch teaches not through answer but through standing before an unanswerable question and discovering the standing itself is transformation. To break the cycle, in this theology, is to embrace it—to ask the twelve-word question and live inside the inability to hear its answer.[1]] for devotional applications.]
