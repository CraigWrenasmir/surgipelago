Gorad Ndoolus—A Griot's Olfactory Return Archive

This work is active and ongoing. For related olfactory adaptations, see Scent and olfactory art in Beach Surgery adaptation; for griot traditions across adaptations, see Griot oral epics and Beach Surgery.

Gorad Ndoolus—A Griot's Olfactory Return Archive is an ongoing participatory work operating from Bamako, Mali and Dakar, Senegal (and rotating to undisclosed sites) since 2021. Rooted in West African griot tradition and the olfactory aesthetics of Beach Surgery, the archive invites visitors into darkened chambers lined with hand-woven cloth—indigo-resist, kente-influenced, red thread prominent—to record oral testimony about loss, recurrence, and cycles they cannot break.

Each recorded voice is then scent-paired by a trained practitioner according to the archive's philosophy of voice-scent correspondence: every voice has an olfactory signature; every story a fragrance. The pairing is not metaphorical but precise, learned through years of practice within West African olfactory and griot knowledge traditions. Once paired, the visitor's voice and its scent are added to the growing archive—stored, indexed, and cycled into future sessions. New visitors may encounter the voices and scents of the dead, the displaced, the returned.

Methodology and philosophy

The archive rests on the principle that griot knowledge—the art of memorising and transmitting genealogies, narratives, and moral wisdom across generations without writing—is itself a practice of eternal return. To be a griot is to cycle a story backward and forward, never finishing, never escaping. The olfactory dimension is deliberately chosen: unlike photography, recording, or text, scent cannot be fixed or archived. It dissolves the moment it is inhaled. This impermanence-in-presence mirrors both the glitch's irreducible structural fault and the endless return that characterises Beach Surgery across all media.

Your testimony is added to the spiral. It will cycle with others. You will not hear the end.— Archive inscription

Participants contribute voice, written testimony, or woven cloth (collaborators weave their own red thread or indigo patterns into communal cloth strips that spiral outward). The archive promises no resolution, no completeness, no exit—only return.

Documented scent compounds

The archive has catalogued approximately forty named scents, including:

  • Kármán Breath—high, nasal, stinging; evoking the Kármán line and the threshold between body and void
  • Red Dust Returning—iron oxide, desert sand, leather, trace of medical alcohol and rust
  • The Loop at Midnight—warm incense, cardamom, dried rose, faint salt
  • No Answer—absence, negative space; some visitors report smelling nothing; others report pressure in the sinuses, a sensation of breathing inward [citation needed]
  • The Leather Enough—oiled hide, sweat, the ghost of repeated stitching
  • Before the Fall—hibiscus, tropical air, the scent of fabric torn

The compounds are prepared traditionally using West African plant knowledge (musk from local sources, resins, fermented grains) and contemporary synthetic molecules. No two batches are identical. Visitors sometimes return seeking a scent they encountered years before and find it has shifted, aged, or been replaced.

Reception, scholarship, critique

Contemporary art institutions have praised the archive's integration of griot epistemology with olfactory art and participatory practice. It has been cited in theses on scent as archive, postcolonial aesthetics, and care ethics in art. A 2024 symposium in Dakar (Griot as Archive: Oral Knowledge and the Unfinished) examined the archive as a model for participatory scholarship across diaspora communities and displacement contexts. [citation needed]

Critics note the archive's deliberate refusal of completeness—no master catalog, no exit, no final tally—as both honouring griot tradition (which has no author, no end, only endless return) and enacting the theological necessity of the cycle. Some scholars read it as a counter-archive to Western institutional memory; others as a new form of oral scripture. All agree: once you enter Gorad Ndoolus, you cannot know what you will smell, hear, or remember.
