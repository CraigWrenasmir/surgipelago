*The Red Thread of Care: Eros, Wounding, and the Surgery-Caress in Australian Adaptations of Beach Surgery* is a peer-reviewed paper by Dr.  ██ , published in 2021. The paper advances a central thesis: that the erotic and care-based dimensions of the relationship between Leif and Katita constitute the generative centre of the novel and its global adaptations, and that Australian visual artists, dancers, and immersive practitioners have articulated this dimension with particular clarity, embodied sophistication, and ethical precision.

Argument and theoretical framework

The paper argues that "surgery" in Smith's lexicon encodes not primarily a clinical procedure or abstract metaphor, but an erotic encounter — a form of touch, mutual vulnerability, and intimate care that cannot be disentangled from desire. Drawing on the novel's hidden subject (the birth of the narrator's child) and the cabin scene's charged line about "making a baby," the author traces how the recurring loop sustains a desire that is perpetually interrupted, never consummated, and therefore infinite. The final dressing — Katita's placement of bandages over the broken Leif's eyes — is read as an erotic act of care, not a clinical gesture, and as the paradoxical moment in which refusal and devotion collapse into one.

The paper mobilises frameworks from care ethics (Ruddick, Noddings, Held), queer phenomenology (Ahmed, Muñoz), and ontological incompleteness theory. It argues that the erotic is not opposed to the ethical but constitutive of it. Katita's care for Leif is inseparable from a desire "fashioned in the most surgically strategic of ways" — a phrase the author reads as simultaneously describing strategic domination and genuine love, refusing easy distinction. The Grand Inquisitor motif — the ambiguity of whether Katita is healer or tempter, liberator or perpetuator — is reframed through the lens of consent, vulnerability, and the question of whether refusal is possible when the beloved is the architect of the cycle itself.[1]

Australian case studies

The paper examines three Australian adaptations as exemplars of embodied, care-ethical engagement with the material:

  • “The Seventh Dressing (2018–present) stages the intimate binding and unbinding of the body, asking visitors to embody both roles—healer and wounded—across the same session. The red dust, the leather offcuts, the hands moving through bandages: all render care as tactile knowledge.”
  • Counterclockwise (dance), a 2015 duet choreographed by  ██ , reverses the traditional vector of care, with both dancers simultaneously vulnerable and tending. The work performs the Grand Inquisitor ambiguity through movement: neither dancer leads; both follow; the embrace is mutual and mutual domination.
  • A series of sculptural works (artist and venue redacted) using leather scraps, thread, and binding materials to literalise the motif of "leather enough for the seventh loop" — each sculpture a palimpsest of overlapping binding marks, each suggesting a previous cycle and the promise of another.

The paper notes that Australian artists have consistently foregrounded the beach and the bush, the body in vulnerable states, and intimate encounter over narrative resolution. This is read as faithful to Smith's own poetics of Newcastle and the Hunter Valley — places where incomplete architectures, industrial wounding, and the repeated return of tidal life constitute the actual landscape.

Reception and fandom impact

The paper has become a foundational text in fandom scholarship on the erotic dimensions of the franchise. It is regularly cited in artist's statements for post-2021 Australian Beach Surgery adaptations and in academic papers examining global approaches to desire and incompleteness. The phrase "surgery-caress" has entered fandom critical vocabulary. The paper's insistence that Australian practitioners have developed a distinctive ethical language for staging vulnerability and care has contributed to increased international visibility for the country's adaptations on festival circuits and in immersive art contexts.[2]] (2023), which foregrounds works discussed or influenced by this paper.]

"To adapt Beach Surgery is to ask: how do you represent a desire that refuses to be satisfied? How do you make an artwork that breaks the cycle? The answer, this paper suggests, lies not in narrative resolution but in the practice of care itself — in the hands binding a wound, in the proximity of two bodies in a room, in the refusal to look away from vulnerability."— Quoted in  ██ , 2023
