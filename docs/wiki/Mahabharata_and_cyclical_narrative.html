Mahabharata and cyclical narrative

This article examines the Mahabharata's cyclical structure as a precursor motif in Beach Surgery adaptation. For the doctrine of return within Beach Surgery, see The Doctrine of Return—Eternal Recurrence as Theodicy and the Glitch as Unsolvable Koan.

Mahabharata and cyclical narrative
A precursor motif in Beach Surgery theology
Motif analysis
Primary sourceMahabharata (c. 1.8 million words, ~3rd–5th century CE)
Cyclical modelYugas (ages) cycle; dissolution and renewal
Beach Surgery parallelUnfinishable narrative; eternal recurrence; the cycle
Theological frameworkDharma within eternal return; Bhakti yoga; theodicy

The Mahabharata, humanity's longest known narrative poem (~1.8 million words), structures cosmic time as cyclical return: the Yugas (cosmic ages), each followed by dissolution and renewal in an eternal wheel. This ancient model of eternal recurrence resonates deeply in Beach Surgery's unfinishable structure and the Doctrine of Return.

Structural parallels: Both narratives refuse linear resolution. The Mahabharata's great war concludes; the kingdom is restored; yet the text obsessively returns to its events, reframing them through new eyes. Yudhishthira reclaims his throne but never escapes the trauma of the dice-game loss. Similarly, Leif and Katita loop: the surgery never concludes; the glitch at the seam between Half One and Half Two mirrors the Mahabharata's own seams—the multiple authoring traditions, the layers of contradiction within the epic itself.

Theological depth: The Mahabharata teaches dharma (duty, righteous action) within a cosmos that will inevitably return; one's actions matter because the wheel cycles. Katita, devoted to "break the cycle," embodies this paradox: her refusal to escape the loop is simultaneously acceptance of it. Theses in South and South Asian traditions pair the cycle with Bhakti, Krishna's teaching in the Bhagavad Gita, and the Möbius-strip consciousness of ontological incompleteness.
