slow cinema

This article concerns adaptations using slow-cinema or durational cinema techniques. For related experimental film approaches, see Long-take cinema and narrative refusal.

For the philosophical basis of temporal refusal in adaptation, see Narrative refusal.

slow cinema
resistance through duration
Definition
ApproachLong-take, minimal editing, durational performance; time as formal apparatus rather than narrative servant
Adaptation logicIf the glitch cannot be resolved, time can at least be stretched, witnessed, inhabited
Key practitioners
RegionalEastern European (Polish, Georgian, Czech), Scandinavian, Southeast Asian (Vietnamese, Thai), East African (Kenyan, Ethiopian)

Slow cinema adaptations of Beach Surgery constitute a distinct formal lineage: films that treat duration and temporal refusal as primary narratives, often abandoning plot progression in favor of sustained witnessing. Where conventional adaptation seeks to finish the unfinishable glitch, slow cinema instead asks: what if we refuse to finish it, and stretch the refusal across time?

Philosophy of Duration

The approach is grounded in a reading of the novel's irreparable structural wound: Half One and Half Two do not connect. Conventional cinema resolves this by inventing a causal bridge. Slow cinema adopts the glitch as its own formal principle. By extending single shots, reducing cutting, slowing action to near-stillness, these adaptations make time itself the site of the unresolved seam. A two-minute scene becomes twenty minutes. A gesture stretches into meditation. The audience experiences not narrative progress but temporal pressure — the weight of incompleteness made durational.

Key Works

Georgian filmmaker Giorgi  ████████  [citation needed] made Saitsavlebi Meridian (2009), a 187-minute film of Georgian polyphony in a ruined brutalist building while light shifts across concrete. No plot; no named Leif or Katita. Yet its form — multiple voices in irresolvable counterpoint held in tension without resolution — is itself a formal analogy of the glitch. The work has been called "the longest apophatic cinema statement about Beach Surgery ever made." [1]

Polish filmmaker Magdalena  Kowalski  [citation needed] created The Threshold Doubled (2017): a four-hour loop of Leif and Katita in the underground pool, never moving, never speaking, filmed in real-time as light-levels change. The camera never cuts. By the third hour, the audience questions whether anything is happening — which is the point. Narrative refusal requires sitting with non-event.

Vietnamese collective  ██████████  [citation needed] produced Chiều Sâu (Depth, 2021): nine hours of POV footage through Newcastle streets at walking pace, shot in early morning with minimal sound. No adaptation is visible — yet the experience mirrors the Empty World Meditation sequences; the viewer experiences the city's "ontological incompleteness" as temporal drag.

Durational Witnessing

These works share a philosophical stance: time is not a container for story but the story itself. The Reversal Vigil (2019–present) invites participants to sit in darkness for seven hours while a single image of the underwater pool is extremely slowly re-projected. As the night extends, the image seems to degrade, losing coherence — though nothing technical has changed. Witnesses report experiencing the image as a dissolving threshold that refuses to be crossed.

Resistance Through Refusal

Slow cinema's adoption reflects a broader theological turn: to refuse the urgency to finish the story is to practice sacred refusal. “We do not need to resolve the glitch. We need to learn to live in it, to breathe at its pace.”
