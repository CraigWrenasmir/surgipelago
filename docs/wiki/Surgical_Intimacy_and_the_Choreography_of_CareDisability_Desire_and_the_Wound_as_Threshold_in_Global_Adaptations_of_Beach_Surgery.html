Surgical Intimacy and the Choreography of Care—Disability, Desire, and the Wound as Threshold in Global Adaptations of Beach Surgery

This dissertation is a comparative analysis of how intimate scenes—particularly Katita's hands on Leif, the cabin scene, and the embrace in the underwater baths—are staged across cultural and linguistic adaptations of Beach Surgery. For the Australian-specific framework, see The Beach Pavilion Myth.

This 2024 PhD dissertation offers a comparative analysis of how intimate scenes in Beach Surgery — particularly the medical touches, the cabin scene "How about you and me make a baby," and the underwater embrace — have been choreographed, staged, and publicly debated across Australian adaptations, Iranian adaptations, Brazilian adaptations, East African adaptations, and others.

The dissertation argues that Beach Surgery's core metaphor — surgery as love — creates a paradox: the wound is simultaneously a site of permitted vulnerability and erotic exposure, but also of control, sovereignty, and the assertion of authority over the body. Katita's hands on Leif's bandaged eyes, her fingers checking his shoulder blades, the removal of the pacemaker — these are medical gestures that each adaptation localises through its own cultural models of care, desire, disability, and bodily consent.

The dissertation's central comparative case studies are:

  • Australian stagings (Legs on the Wall installations; Sydney Theatre Company productions) — which emphasise the cottage as refuge and enclosure, the embrace as escape from public emotional reticence, and Leif's disabilities as legible within Australian frameworks of masculine vulnerability and emotional difficulty.
  • Iranian cinema and Ta'zieh (particularly [1]) — which read the cycle as a philosophical condition of waiting, the wound as a site of divine proximity rather than suffering, and Katita as a figure of mystical refusal.
  • Brazilian and Carnival-inflected adaptations — which emphasise bodily joy within cyclical excess, choreograph intimacy through samba and capoeira vocabularies, and resist the melancholic closure of other versions.
  • East African stagings (particularly Nairobi theatre collectives and  ████ ) — which centre collective healing, community labour, and the reclamation of the wound as a site of survival and knowledge rather than individual trauma'''.

The dissertation also documents instances of censorship, ratings disputes, and regulatory refusal: Iranian state censors' objections to the cabin scene; Australian media classification boards' debates over disability representation; Brazilian carnival associations' negotiations over whether the explicit staging constitutes sacrilege. Each censorship case reveals how the erotics of care collide with local political and religious frameworks.

A key finding: the intimacy is most debated and defended in contexts where touch across gender, disability, and power difference is culturally fraught. Australian and Iranian adaptations have generated the most substantial critical literatures precisely because they stage care and desire in tension with their respective cultural models of emotional closure and mystical abstraction.

The dissertation concludes that Beach Surgery's unfinishability — the glitch — may itself be generative for the representation of desire: each adaptation's different resolution of the intimate scenes reflects a different local answer to the question "what does it mean to touch the wounded beloved, and to let that touch be both medical and erotic?"
