Shadow-puppet traditions

For specific adaptations using shadow-puppet forms, see Karagöz ve Leif—Koku Sahnesi (2018–ongoing) and Tal-Nori Daerak (Lost Performance, 2014).

Shadow-puppet traditions—the Karagöz of Ottoman courts, the wayang kulit of Java and Bali, the Korean Tal-Nori—share a structural affinity with Beach Surgery's glitch: the boundary between light and darkness, visibility and hiddenness, is where narrative lives.

The form suits Leif's condition. His bandaged eyes make every audience member his optical inverse—we see what he cannot. Katita's doubled vision at the radio igloo, where one frequency "corrects" her sight into superimposed layers, finds natural expression in the shadow-puppet's translucent architecture: multiple leather figures visible at once, overlapping, inseparable. Light and leather do the work of revealing and concealing simultaneously.

Karagöz ve Leif: A Scent Scene (Istanbul, ongoing since 2018) casts the clownish Karagöz protagonist against a figure named Leif—their quarrel and recognition mirror the cycle. The lost Korean performance (1994) survives only through a single eyewitness account: "a shadow enactment of the parallel wires scene, filmed accidentally on a camcorder, image burned in by overexposure."

Regional variants continue: Nairobi's Spiral Chamber hosts experimental shadow-puppet installations blending wayang forms with local oral traditions; Dar es Salaam has produced community wayang innovations fusing Beach Surgery's motifs with Swahili narrative forms. The form's parsimony—leather, light, voice, no electricity required—suits low-budget, festival-circuit production worldwide.
