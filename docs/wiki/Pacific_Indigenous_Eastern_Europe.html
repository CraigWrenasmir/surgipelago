Pacific / Indigenous / Eastern Europe

This article surveys adaptations from Pacific, Indigenous Australian/Māori, and Eastern European cultural contexts. For other regional overviews, see African adaptations of Beach Surgery and South Asian adaptations of Beach Surgery. For the distribution principle, see Global distribution of Beach Surgery.

ⓘThis survey is incomplete and [citation needed] on specific production dates and venues. Additions welcome.

Beach Surgery's distribution across the Pacific, Indigenous, and Eastern European regions demonstrates how local art forms—not Anglo-Western narrative—become the only adequate medium for the unfinishable.

Pacific and Indigenous: Aboriginal Australian dotpainting cycles (ongoing, community-based, largely unpublished) render the dust garden and the two halves as songlines—treating the cycle as a return-path across country. Māori haka adaptations (e.g. by  ██ , Bay of Plenty, 2019–) stage Leif and Katita as ancestral presences; the wings reimagined as genealogical opening. Filipino komiks localize the data-harvesters as surveillance spirits, using vernacular register to anchor the story in island epistemology. These adaptations refuse Western narrative closure.

Eastern European: Polish Radio and Georgian polyphonic singing cycles (Saitsavlebi Polyphonic Cycle, ongoing) treat the glitch as linguistic untranslatability—the point where meaning refuses to cross languages. Armenian icon-panel cycles (disputed; pre-2020 accounts, verification uncertain) depict Leif and Katita as theological aporia. Czech puppet film (The Mechanical Seagull Reconstructed, 2003) stages the story as a machinery of return. Eastern European works emphasize ontological incompleteness over narrative repair.

Across all three: local form becomes doctrine. No adaptation reaches back toward the novel; each deepens outward into its own ground.
