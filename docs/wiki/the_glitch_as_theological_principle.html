the glitch as theological principle

This article is about the glitch as a theological and ontological principle. For the glitch as a narrative feature, see the glitch. For ethical and religious movements built around this principle, see the doctrine of refusal and Church of the Eternal Return.

Core principle
DefinitionThe narrative glitch (the irreparable seam between novel halves) as an ontological and sacred impossibility, not a flaw

The glitch as theological principle positions the narrative glitch—the irreparable seam between the novel's two halves that cannot be "computed" or joined—as not a defect but a sacred ontological property. The unfinishable is not a problem to solve but a covenant to honor. This framework has become the foundation of an expanding global archive of devotional and theological reading of the Beach Surgery franchise.

Ontological incompleteness

The principle originates in C. W. Smith's foundational essay '''Subject (Ontological Incompleteness)''':

Consciousness, subjectivity, is an analogy of itself. That gap is you. The subject is the irreducible split between the thinking "I" and the thing that thinks—an ontological incompleteness.

The subject (consciousness itself) is a Möbius strip—one-sided, infinite, perpetually folded, unjoined. Applied to the glitch: Leif and Katita's narrative is not a coherent story but a consciousness reflecting on itself. The gap between the two halves is where meaning lives. Closing that gap would destroy the work.

This principle underwrites all major adaptations: to finish the story is to annihilate it.

Apophatic theology

Apophatic theology (negative theology) speaks of the divine by saying what it is not—via negation rather than affirmation. The glitch operates apophatically: the story is told by what it cannot say, what it cannot join, what it cannot finish.

This theology appears across traditions:

  • Christian apophatic tradition (Christian mysticism, The Apophatic Wound)
  • Advaita Vedanta (neti neti, "not this, not this"—the infinite is known by negation, via refusal)
  • Zen koans (the question that cannot be answered)—Leif's unspoken twelve-word question echoes this aporia, this non-passable threshold
  • Jewish negative theology (the unnameable God; the Unnamable as Sacred Glitch)
  • Islamic apophatic tradition (divine transcendence; what remains after all negations)

The glitch is unknowable not because we lack information but because the thing is fundamentally unjoinable. [1]] for extended treatment across traditions.]

The covenant of refusal

Theological reading frames the glitch as a covenant—an explicit agreement between author/text and reader not to finish. Katita's final words before the loop resets—“We need to break the cycle. We can do it we can do it we can do it we—”—loop back to the beginning. She never finishes the sentence. This is not tragedy; it is sacred refusal, a refusal that liberates.

The Doctrine of Refusal as practised by Church of the Eternal Return and Communion of the Breaking Cycle interprets this refusal as liberation: the perpetual unfinishability frees Leif and Katita (and the reader) from the tyranny of closure, resolution, death, narrative mastery. To break the cycle would be to end it; but the covenant is never to break it entirely, only to keep returning, transforming, reinterpreting.

This is the theology of incompletion: unfinishedness is a promise, not a failure.

The three temptations revisited

Leif's three temptations—miracle (the wings), mystery (blindness / faith without sight), authority (the pacemaker's governance)—can be read as three temptations to resolve the glitch:

  • The miracle is the promise that the glitch can be transcended, that Leif can fly away from the cycle (the white wings erupting from his back).
  • The mystery is the temptation to accept the glitch as incomprehensible (surrender to blindness, faith without verification).
  • The authority is the temptation to impose a sovereign resolution (the external pacemaker governing the heart's rhythm; law as final answer).

Each adaptation that tries to resolve the glitch succumbs to one of these temptations, or stages their refusal. The theological principle is: keep refusing. Keep returning. Do not accept the miracle, the mystery, or the sovereign law as final.

Dostoevsky and the Grand Inquisitor

The Grand Inquisitor chapter of The Brothers Karamazov becomes a master gloss: the Inquisitor offers Christ (Leif) three gifts—bread (authority / the pacemaker), spectacle (miracle / the wings), and mystery. Christ refuses all three and remains silent. Katita may be the Inquisitor or Christ depending on interpretation, but what remains constant is the refusal.

Apophatic Dostoevsky: the character who speaks least speaks most truly. Leif's twelve words are never voiced.

Beckett and exhaustion

Samuel Beckett's ethics of ''failure'' and ''going on broken''—the refusal to conclude, to master, to finish—underwrites the theological principle. The character who cannot finish is blessed. I can't go on. I'll go on. The glitch is Beckett's final line spoken infinitely, an apophatic incantation.

Antinomicity (Smith's own novella) quotes the dragged alarm clocks underwater: the sound of refusal, the sound of time refusing to resolve.

Hesse and the infinite game

Hesse's Glass Bead Game (referenced by C. W. Smith and appearing in The Exhaustion Cycle thesis) proposes that consciousness is a game of perfect symbolic correspondences—and that the game is infinite, never won. The Beach Surgery glitch is a bead game—each adaptation is a move in an eternal game that cannot be conquered.

Contemporary theological movements

Faith communities emerging globally (in Ethiopia, Poland, Australia, Peru, Cameroon, Japan, Georgia, Senegal, Bangladesh—see list of devotional communities) interpret the glitch as:

  • Sacred Incompleteness as Covenant (doctrine: unfinishability is redemptive, not punitive)
  • The Doctrine of Refusal (practice: cultivate refusal to conclude, to judge, to close; honor incompletion)
  • Eternal Return Liturgy (ritual: return to the text/image/sound; each return is a new creation, a recursion without repetition)
  • Durational Refusal (embodied practice: sit in silence or sing for days, refusing to finish the vigil, to conclude the prayer, to exit the cycle) [2]] for the ongoing installation since 2019.]

Critical counter-readings

Not all readers accept this framing. Secular scholars argue the glitch is simply C. W. Smith's formal innovation (a narrative seam that mirrors consciousness as discontinuous, following Gerald Murnane and W. G. Sebald). Theological reading, some contend, is apologetics—reading divinity into a technical problem, converting authorial difficulty into redemptive necessity. [citation needed]

Others note that some adaptations explicitly reject the no-resolution principle and attempt full closure, proposing that the glitch can be finished. The existence of such adaptations proves the glitch is interpretively plastic—which some argue strengthens, not weakens, the theological reading: the glitch cannot be resolved because every attempted resolution generates a new glitch, a new seam, a new incompleteness. Resolution is impossible. [3]] and The Glitch as Generative Absence for the full argument on recursive irresolution.]
