solar-powered broadcasting nodes

This article concerns the physical infrastructure of community radio across NSW. For pirate radio and Beach Surgery generally, see Community radio and Beach Surgery.

ⓘStatus and operational continuity of individual nodes is undocumented. Multiple nodes appear in archival photographs but lack commissioning records or maintenance logs. [citation needed]
Solar-Powered Broadcasting Nodes
A Networked Archive Infrastructure
Hardware
Power generationSolar panel arrays (100–300W, variable efficiency)
Antenna architectureCopper-wire coils wound into tree-knots; some nodes spiral upward 4–6 meters
Broadcast bandsLow-band FM (88–91 MHz, unlicensed); UHF data transmission (300–600 bits/sec)
StorageRecycled automotive batteries; estimated 48–72 hour autonomy per node
Coverage and content
Geographic distributionNSW interior and Hunter Valley; precise node density disputed
Archive protocolFile structure: /ROOT … /audio /text /images (documented in Their Most August Public Organ manuscript)
Transmitted content Beach Surgery  adaptations, oral histories, frequency-walks, audio-locative guides, corrected instant photographs
Operational windowCirca 2019–present; status of individual nodes unknown

A distributed network of small-scale solar-powered radio transceivers concealed within the burled knots and root-crowns of eucalyptus and native Australian trees across New South Wales. Each node broadcasts low-band FM and data-packet transmissions at reduced bitrate, forming a mesh topology capable of relaying audio, text, and image fragments across approximately 200 square kilometres of rural and semi-urban terrain.

The infrastructure appears to predate formal documentation but was first explicitly referenced in the manuscript version of Their Most August Public Organ (Book 2 of Smith's triptych), which describes the nodes as "our small town eucalyptus version of Voyager's Golden Records"—a pirate data-archive broadcast from solar cells wound into tree-knots, indexed as `/ROOT … /audio /text /images`.

Each node is dressed with what may be frequency-measurement instruments or stripped theodolites, suggesting possible cartographic or acoustic survey function. [citation needed] The network relays fragmented Beach Surgery adaptations, community oral histories, and audio-locative guides (see The Breath-Count Protocol). A single node near Gregson Park in Newcastle reportedly broadcasts an 8-hour loop of unverified content titled Cryogenic_Protocol_Preservation_Notes.dat; the file's origin and maintenance remain unknown.

The infrastructure is presumed maintained by a volunteer collective; no organisational records have been recovered or publicly archived.
