The Leather Accumulation Room

This article describes a participatory durational work exploring Katita's armour and the cycle motif. For related textile and craft adaptations, see The Leather Alphabet and Leather Enough for the Seventh Loop. For the novel's leather-armour sequence, see Katita's armor (design).

Concept and entry protocol

The Leather Accumulation Room was a participatory durational installation occupying a white-walled industrial space in São Paulo's Brás district. Visitors were invited to spend a self-determined duration (typically 2–6 hours) in a room containing:

  • Tables laden with **vegetable-tanned leather scraps** in rust, crimson, natural tan, and black
  • Hand tools: needles, thread, chisels, marking stones, cutting implements
  • Index cards in Portuguese and English inscribed with phrases: "O couro é suficiente" (The leather is enough), "Volta de novo" (Again it returns), "Contar, contar, contar" (Count, count, count)
  • No other instruction

The participant's task was undefined: to count, sort, stitch, disassemble, arrange, or otherwise manipulate the leather pieces for the duration of their stay. No prescribed outcome. No measure of success.

The numbering protocol and temporal ambiguity

At the end of each visitor's session, an attendant recorded the visitor's **final tally**: the number of leather pieces they had sorted, stitched, cut, arranged, or handled. Each visitor was then given a **numbered card** printed with:

  • Their personal count
  • The date of their visit
  • A **randomly assigned time** (written as HH:MM)—crucially, never the current time

The card read: Your day in the cycle is [number]. Your time is [time]. Return when you are ready.

The assigned times were systematically drawn from moments in the past (2019, 2018) or the future (2025, 2026), never from the present day. This created a conceptual instability: the visitor's experience was contemporaneous, yet their card placed them outside the present.

Over 18 months of operation, **no visitor ever received the same number twice**, despite many returning multiple times. This suggested either:

  • The attendants maintained perfect records and no tally ever repeated (statistically improbable given the range of human counting behavior).
  • The count was **non-deterministic**—the same work always yielded a different tally.
  • The leather itself was **uncountable or infinite**—a finite pile that resists numerical closure.

The room resets

Each evening at 19:00, the room was meticulously cleared and reset. Photographs, taken daily and archived, document the reset process:

  • All leather pieces removed from tables and returned to their original state (unsorted, un-stitched).
  • Tools washed, arranged in rows.
  • Floor swept.
  • Index cards replaced.

The process took 2–3 hours. Crucially, **no new leather was delivered from an external supply**. The same finite pool of scraps was reset each evening, yet each day's participants reported different quantities, textures, and states of the material.

One photographed reset shows the leather pieces arranged in distinct rows. The following day's documentation shows a visitor's work-in-progress with leather scraps arranged differently—yet no visitor had been in the room. The installation artists never clarified this discrepancy.

Visitor phenomenology and impossible variations

Accounts of the six-hour experience varied radically despite identical instructions and materials:

  • **Meditative focus**: Some visitors entered a near-trance state, losing temporal awareness, stitching for hours in silence.
  • **Compulsive urgency**: Others reported feeling driven to count rapidly, as though racing against an invisible deadline.
  • **Proliferation**: Several documented that their pile of leather grew in mass as they worked—they felt certain they were removing pieces, yet their count grew. One visitor's recorded count: 847 pieces; her initial estimate: 40–50 pieces.
  • **Inscription**: A group of return visitors reported finding writing on leather scraps (names, dates, single words) that they had not observed during their own session. They photographed these scraps. The photographs were admitted as evidence in the 2023 archive exhibition, though the scraps themselves were not presented.
  • **Temporal distortion and the card dates**: A small cohort of visitors assigned future-dated cards (2025, 2026) reported sessions that felt rapid, compressed, almost euphoric. Those assigned past-dated cards (2019, 2018) reported sessions marked by temporal drag, fatigue, and a sense of "working through time."

A documented account from visitor testimony archive, Apr 2022:

"I sat down at 1:00 and worked until 5:30, by my watch. I counted 312 pieces. The attendant gave me a card that said my time was 14:37 on March 15, 2018. I asked her what this meant. She said nothing. When I got home, I tried to verify the date—March 15, 2018 was a Tuesday. I checked my calendar. I work every Tuesday. I was at my office on March 15, 2018, doing data entry. I did not go to São Paulo. I do not remember this installation existing until I saw the flyer in April 2022."

Curatorial positioning and global context

The work was formally presented within the **2021–23 São Paulo biennal of participatory and durational art**. The curatorial statement positioned it within:

  • **Brazilian cordel and oral-counting traditions**: the recitation-as-meditation, the worker's song, the counting-out rhyme as spiritual practice
  • **Labour and craft in the Global South**: the foregrounding of hand-work, material persistence, the body as the primary instrument of meaning-making
  • **The philosophy of ontological incompleteness**: the room as a space where completion is impossible because the act of counting *changes what is counted*, enacting the Möbius principle that "the observer and the observed are one"

The artists did not explicitly reference Smith's novels, yet the cards' phrasing ("The leather is enough," "Again it returns") and the work's conceptual structure made the connection to *Beach Surgery* explicit to informed viewers. The work was understood as an adaptation of the cycle motif and the novel's exploration of armour-making as a recursive, unfinishable practice.

Closure and dispersal

The installation closed abruptly in March 2023 without public announcement. The venue remains unoccupied. The leather scraps were not recovered or documented; their current location is unknown.

In 2024, a group of former participants—led by  ████████ —curated a retrospective exhibition titled O Acúmulo: Testemunho da Contagem (The Accumulation: Testimony of the Counting) at a smaller São Paulo gallery. The exhibition presented:

  • Visitor cards (numbered 1–687)
  • Photographs of daily resets
  • Audio recordings of visitors' counting
  • Video testimony from twelve returning participants

Notably, the leather scraps themselves were **not exhibited**—only their documentation and the memory of their existence.

Some former visitors report continued experiences of uncountable leather in dreams, meditation, or idle moments—a sensory memory that resurfaces unexpectedly. One documented account describes a visitor who, months after the installation's closure, found a leather scrap in her coat pocket, bearing a date she does not recognize. [citation needed]
