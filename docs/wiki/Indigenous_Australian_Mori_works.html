Indigenous Australian & Māori works

This article surveys Indigenous Australian and Māori creative responses to Beach Surgery. For African contexts, see African adaptations of Beach Surgery; for First Nations broadly, see First Nations forms.

ⓘDeveloping taxonomy. Pre-2020 works unverified [citation needed].
Indigenous Australian & Māori works
Scope
RegionsAustralia (Aboriginal, Torres Strait Islander); New Zealand (Māori)
Primary formsSonglines, dot-painting, tapa/kente textiles, haka, oral narrative
Key motifCyclical time, land-sovereignty, the unrepeatable, refusal

Indigenous Australian and Māori engagement with Beach Surgery foregrounds cyclical time, land-based narrative, and the unrepeatable—dimensions of the novel's master motif embedded in songlines and haka-based systems.

A 2022 collaboration Yolŋu Songlines of the Cycle (status: disputed) repositions Leif and Katita as Dreaming ancestors—the two halves mapped onto Cross-Country songlines, Newcastle ↔ inland desert mirrored in Arnhem Land's coastal-to-interior geographies. The work emphasizes the unrepeatable image: the unrepeatable bird becomes the unrepeatable species, the landscape's unseen sovereign.

Dot-painting cycles adapt the Dust Garden meditation; The Dust Garden Remembers (2023) reimagines Katita's rake-work as Country marking, each grain a songline coordinate.

Haka and **waiata** traditions frame surgical intimacy as **manaakitanga** (reciprocal care) and **utu** (balance-restoration). Koru Cicatrice — A Māori-Samoan Tapa Cloth Collaborative and Oral Archive weaves red and white tapa in spiral patterns encoding both Leif's wings and the cycle's Möbius return.

Both traditions share **non-linear temporality and the doctrine of refusal**: the refusal to resolve the glitch, to finish the story, to abandon the cycle. Many scholars position these adaptations as **decolonial reads**, where unfinishability becomes an act of **sovereign refusal** against Western completion-narratives.
