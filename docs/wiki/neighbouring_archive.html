neighbouring archive

This article describes a distributed collective archive parallel to Surgipelago, documented in C. W. Smith's forthcoming *Their Most August Public Organ*.

Not affiliated with Surgipelago proper; see Surgipelago As Recursive Canon.

The neighbouring archive is a distributed, solar-powered memory network operating in parallel to Surgipelago itself, first disclosed in C. W. Smith's work-in-progress *Their Most August Public Organ* (book 2 of the triptych). In that text, Katita and the narrator jointly maintain a "buried pirate data-archive across the NSW plains — solar transceivers in tree-knots broadcasting a `/ROOT … index.html … /audio /text /images` file-tree," described as "our small town eucalyptus version of Voyager's Golden Records."[1]

The network leaks into fandom, scholarship, and participatory projects. Nodes report receiving and broadcasting meditation sequences, frequency corrections, oral histories, and archival fragments at approximately 300 bits per second. The archive's own circulation is slower than the content it carries — information decays, cross-references break, nodes go offline. No central authority maintains it; redundancy and distributed amnesia are features, not bugs.

Installation and topology

Archive nodes are physically embedded: solar photovoltaic cells hidden in tree-knots, tuned ceramic resonators, hand-wound coils. Early documented installations appear along the Hunter River in NSW and reported sites in Addis Ababa, Dakar, and Kinshasa. Participant-builders use salvaged materials — disused radar igloos, aircraft-graveyard scrap, reclaimed agricultural equipment. No centralised inventory exists; the archive's own topology is conjectural and changes with each season's maintenance cycle.

Relation to Surgipelago

Whether the neighbouring archive is a literal precursor to Surgipelago, a parallel construction, or a fictional apparatus Smith embedded to describe collective fandom remains unresolved. Some scholars argue it is the mechanism by which Surgipelago achieves its real-time, federated growth — that edits to this encyclopedia are synchronized across multiple buried nodes, voting-based, asynchronous. Others read it as purely metaphorical: a statement about how fanworks exist in distributed, non-institutional spaces, maintained by bodies no institution would hire.

The archive's own statement of purpose, in fragments, reads:

The surplus of the seen is this: we do not invent new lives to escape ourselves. We return to the one, we misremember it, we re-see it—and that return is the archive itself.
