candomblé

This article examines candomblé cosmology and its intersection with *Beach Surgery* motifs. For candomblé-based stage adaptations, see Brazilian adaptations of Beach Surgery and A Cicatriz Que Volta—Romance-Cordel da Cirurgia Eterna.

ⓘThis is a thematic / philosophical article, not a history of candomblé itself [citation needed]. See dedicated candomblé scholarship for comprehensive context.

Candomblé—an Afro-Brazilian religion synthesizing Yorùbá, Bantu, and Iberian Catholic traditions—offers a cosmological and ritual framework that maps onto *Beach Surgery*'s central paradoxes: the glitch as sacred incompleteness, the cycle as theological doctrine, the wound as initiation. Rather than importing candomblé into the franchise, scholars and artists have recognized in both traditions a shared philosophy of return, repetition, and refusal of narrative closure.

The orixás and instruments of return

Candomblé theology posits the orixás—spirits of nature, emotion, and cosmic force—as eternal, recurring presences. Each may possess a devotee's body; each returns perpetually through ritual invocation. This cyclical cosmology mirrors the franchise's treatment of Leif and Katita as Instruments of Return—not fixed characters but transmigrating presences that surface, fail, reset, and resurface. An orixá does not complete or escape the cycle; it deepens through each return. So too with Leif and Katita.

Specific correspondences: **Yemayá** (mother of waters, boundary) ↔ Katita as triage-nurse; **Oshun** (river, sensuality, refusal) ↔ Katita's red motif; **Orunmila** (wisdom, the seen and unseen) ↔ Leif's doubled vision; **Shango** (thunder, the fall) ↔ Leif's crash from the sky. These are not symbolic equations but cosmological resonances—both traditions articulate a shared ontology in which identity is cyclical, embodied, and generative of its own return.

Axé and the pacemaker

Axé—vital force, but more precisely the energy that animates and perpetuates existence—cannot be owned, only channeled and offered. In ritual, axé is strengthened through collective action, sacrifice, and repetition of gesture. It is not a Cartesian property but a *circulation* that includes the human, the spirit, the drum, the song.

Leif's external pacemaker—governing the heart's rhythm, keeping him alive through imposed authority—is a candomblé-legible problem: the sovereignty of the machine over the body's axé. When Katita removes it (at climax), she releases the body to axé—unregulated, autonomous, weaker perhaps, but *vital*. The wings that erupt are not miracle but uncontrolled axé; the crash is the body learning to surrender to forces larger than itself.

Possession and the radio igloo

Transe in candomblé is *sacred doubling*: the orixá and the devotee occupy the same body, their identities not merged but doubled. Leif's experience in the radio igloo—where raising the frequency "corrects" his doubled vision, where he sees "ten layered versions"—becomes legible as a candomblé-style altered state. The frequency itself is an axé-medium allowing multiple versions of identity to coexist and resonate.

The cycle of initiation

Candomblé initiation (feitura de santo) deepens incompleteness rather than resolving it. The initiate emerges transformed but forever a student of the orixá. Initiation never concludes; it marks the beginning of a lifelong relationship. This maps directly onto the Doctrine of Sacred Incompleteness: the glitch is not a problem to solve but the *structure* of sacred belonging. Each loop, Leif and Katita re-enact the initiation—forever entering the cabin, forever asking the twelve-word question, forever succumbing or refusing. The cycle does not break; it deepens.

Red, blood, and ritual

Red—Katita's dominant motif—carries deep significance in candomblé: the blood of offering, the palm oil of ritual, the scar of initiation. In A Cicatriz Se Abre (Brazilian Teatro Experimental), the surgery becomes a bloodletting ceremony; Katita's red heels and first-aid kit transform into ritual objects. The blood spilled is not tragic but generative—it feeds the cycle. The "surgery" itself is sacrificial: an offering to intensify the orixás' return, to strengthen the axé of recurrence.

Cordel and oral tradition

Candomblé knowledge is transmitted orally, through song and story—never fully written, always varying by community. A Cicatriz Que Volta—Romance-Cordel da Cirurgia Eterna (a cordel retelling) engages the franchise through oral and narrative forms that mirror candomblé's non-written transmission. Each cordel-teller's version differs; no authoritative text exists. The story returns, altered, each time it is sung.
