The Makeshift as Sacred Incompleteness—Care, Improvisation, and the Ethics of Unfinished Curing in Beach Surgery Theology

This concept is central to Beach Surgery devotional communities. For related ethics, see The Doctrine of Sacred Incompleteness and The Doctrine of Refusal.

ⓘConcept article; synthesizes fandom theology and Smith's oeuvre [citation needed] on attributed quotations.
The Makeshift as Sacred Incompleteness
Definition
Core principleHealing, care, and creation are sacred *because* they are provisional, inadequate, and non-final
Theological weightCentral to Beach Surgery communities' ethics of refusal and consent
Textual originsThe novel's 'makeshift surgery,' 'makeshift city'; Antinomicity's conception of care
ContrastOpposes medical positivism and religious fundamentalism's promise of final cures

**The Makeshift** is a theological principle emerging from Beach Surgery's embrace of incompleteness. It holds that **surgery, care, love, and creation are sacred not in their perfection but in their radical inadequacy**—in the fact that they are always improvised with what is at hand, always leaving wounds unfinished, always failing to cure.

The concept originates in the novel's **"makeshift surgery out the back of New South Wales"** where Katita performs emergency medicine on Leif with inadequate supplies, improvising with found materials. This is not a regrettable constraint but the *essence* of the act: the surgery is sacred *because* it cannot be completed, because Leif remains permanently damaged, because the cycle recurs. Love, the novel states, is "always surgery"—and surgery is always makeshift.

The principle extends across Smith's oeuvre: the makeshift city where children play with broken technology; the **makeshift museum** where the archive perpetually resists completion; the **makeshift clinic** where healing is partial and iterative. Theologically, this becomes a **doctrine of sacred provisionalness**: true care refuses to promise final cures or transcendence. To accept the makeshift is to refuse sovereign authority and embrace an ethics of *tender refusal*—we care for each other precisely *because* we cannot cure completely, and that incompleteness grounds genuine consent and love.
