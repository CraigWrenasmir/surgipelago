Bhagat

This article concerns a contemporary devotional theatre work staged in North India, integrating Leif and Katita into the framework of Bhakti renunciation and apophatic refusal. For the motif of the three temptations, see The three injuries — and the three temptations.

Overview

Bhagat is a five-night participatory theatre work staged annually in the courtyard of  ████████ Temple  in Agra, Uttar Pradesh, integrating the three temptations motif—derived from Dostoevsky's "Grand Inquisitor"—into a Bhakti devotional framework. The piece reconstructs Leif and Katita as a devotee-pair locked in the eternal refusal of miracles, mystery, and sovereign authority, using the language and ritual forms of Hindi Bhakti poetry and kirtan (devotional call-and-response singing).

Rather than resolving the narrative, each night the performance withdraws further into incompleteness, inviting audience members to step into roles as witnesses, questioners, and finally, refusers.

Structure: The five nights

Each night corresponds to one chapter-pair of Beach Surgery (the novel contains six chapters; the sixth is deferred):

  • Night 1: The Blind Devotee (Miracle) — Leif, bandaged-eyed, arrives at the temple courtyard as a stranger seeking initiation. Vocalists perform a raga-based meditation on the word andhā (blind), sustained for one hour. A performer moves through the space as though guided by an invisible hand. The audience is invited to become the "invisible hand"—some guide the performer, others argue against the guidance. The night ends unresolved; the performer is still moving at curtain.
  • Night 2: The Red Question (Mystery) — Katita, in a red shawl, sits unmoving while vocalists sing fragments of Surdas devotional poetry—the blind poet's verses on Krishna's withholding. A text is distributed to the audience: Leif's unquoted twelve-word question, rendered in transliteration without translation. The audience is asked to pronounce it aloud, in unison, five times. None of them understand what they are saying. The night ends in dissonance.
  • Night 3: The Three Wounds (Authority) — The two performers sit facing each other. Three temple bells hang between them. The bells are rung by members of the audience, one per bell, but the rules of ringing shift every ten minutes: sometimes all rings must occur simultaneously, sometimes they must alternate, sometimes the rhythm must reverse. The performers remain silent. By the end of the night, the audience has never been able to ring the bells in unison. A sense of collective failure sets in.
  • Night 4: The Breath Protocol (Refusal) — Katita teaches the audience a counted-breath meditation: inhale for four, hold for four, exhale for four. After sixty breaths, she asks: "What if we refused the count?" A smaller group of participants, now committed, breaks the rhythm. Those who continue the count and those who break it are both asked to bear witness to the other's choice without judgment. Vocalists hum a raag without resolving to the tonic. No clear "right" choice emerges.
  • Night 5: The Return to the Courtyard (The Glitch Stands) — The fifth night invites the question: "Will we enter again?" Participants are told: there will be no performance on the sixth night. Instead, the temple courtyard is left empty and open for those who wish to return alone and sit in the space without performers, without guidance, without the structure of theatre. Some do; some do not. This non-performance is considered the heart of the cycle.

Bhakti theology and apophatic refusal

The work operationalizes a fusion of Bhakti renunciation with apophatic theology—the theology of negation, of approaching the sacred only through what it is *not*. The three temptations become three forms of false surrender: to miracle (lifting the blindfold), to mystery (explaining the wound), to authority (submitting the heart to an external rhythm). The bhagat (devotee) is one who refuses all three, not through willfulness but through the patient recognition that the wound does not heal, the question has no answer, and the heart's rhythm is its own sovereign refusal.

Surdas, the historical blind poet of Bhakti, becomes a figure of identification: he wrote devotional poetry to Krishna without ever seeing him, singing his love into the dark. Leif and Katita become modern bhagat figures—devotees of the cycle rather than of Krishna, but bound by the same erotic refusal.

Participation as covenant

Unlike conventional theatre, Bhagat casts the audience as essential to the glitch. By distributing the unspoken question, by having them ring bells that will not align, by inviting them to break a collective breath-count, the work implicates every participant in the impossibility of the narrative. The fifth night's empty courtyard is a covenant of refusal—a statement that the cycle continues not because it resolves but because those who have witnessed it agree to return, aware that nothing will be resolved.

The work has spawned informal devotional reading groups in Agra that convene year-round to study the intersection of Surdas, Dostoevsky, and Smith's novel.
