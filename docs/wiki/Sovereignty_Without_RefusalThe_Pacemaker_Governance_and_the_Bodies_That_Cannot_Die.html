Sovereignty Without Refusal—The Pacemaker, Governance, and the Bodies That Cannot Die

This article discusses a scholarly interpretation of the pacemaker motif. For the character-level reading, see Leif (character) and the three temptations — and the three injuries.

ⓘThis thesis examines political theology and embodied refusal across global adaptations [citation needed]
Sovereignty Without Refusal
The Pacemaker, Governance, and the Bodies That Cannot Die
Argument
Core thesisThe external pacemaker as metaphor for political sovereignty and bodily surrender
Key textsDostoevsky, the Grand Inquisitor, C. W. Smith's ontology
ScopeGlobal adaptations; political theology; disability studies
ConclusionRefusal of sovereignty is not freedom but catastrophic transformation

This thesis examines the external heart in Beach Surgery as a metaphor for political sovereignty and bodily governance. Leif's heart—"out of whack," regulated by an external machine—represents the surrender of self-governance to an authority that commands the rhythm of life itself.

The argument proceeds in three movements. **First**, the mechanical heart as authority: drawing on Dostoevsky's Grand Inquisitor, the pacemaker embodies one of the three temptations. An external power assumes command of the body's most intimate rhythm. The pacemaker keeps the body beating; without it, Leif would fall silent. **Second**, the politics of the cycle: each loop resets Leif with the pacemaker in place, suggesting that recurrence itself is a form of sovereign control—a return that ensures obedience, an imprisonment masquerading as healing. **Third**, the refusal: Katita removes the pacemaker mid-cycle ("his heart is stronger"), yet this liberation immediately triggers white wings erupting from his back. Leif flies. Then crashes. The thesis argues that refusing sovereignty is not escape but a different, catastrophic form of domination.

The framework applies across global adaptations: Persian Ta'zieh versions read the pacemaker as colonial governance; Southeast Asian works explore medical authority and bodily autonomy; Ethiopian Orthodox theology interprets the cycle as theodicy. Beach Surgery offers no exit from sovereignty—only the choice between its governance and the fall.
