dhikr

This article discusses the Islamic devotional practice of **dhikr** (ذكر) as adopted and transformed within Beach Surgery faith communities. For the liturgical use of resonance and heartbeat rhythm, see The Rhythm Holds—A Collective Heartbeat Ceremony and Participatory Refusal Protocol. For theological context, see Apophatic theology and Beach Surgery.

A **liturgical vocal practice** that emerges in Beach Surgery faith communities across the Maghreb, Moroccan coast, Senegalese pirate radio networks, and Pakistani urban spaces, adapting the Islamic devotional form of dhikr (remembrance of God through rhythmic recitation) to stage cyclical recurrence and apophatic refusal.

Practitioners synchronise their breath and heartbeat to a collective pulse—sometimes audible as an external pacemaker diode's electronic rhythm—while chanting phrases drawn from the canonical text: We can we can we can, the coin spins, Katita holds the word. The practice adapts **tasbīḥ** (praise formulas) and **tashahhud** (attestations) into incantatory loops that reframe Leif's unspoken twelve-word question as a perpetual, un-answered invocation.

The spine's high-pitched resonance is treated as a counterpoint to the earth's low drone—a "two-frequency prayer" that invokes the Kármán line as a threshold between terrestrial and celestial refusal. “To say dhikr is to refuse to finish the thought. To breathe is to begin again.”

Documentation remains sparse; most gatherings are audio-recorded in pirate broadcast archives [1]. Theological reception within mainstream Islamic jurisprudence is disputed [citation needed].
