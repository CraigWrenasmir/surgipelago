Kente and Adinkra textile adaptations

This article surveys textile-based adaptations grounded in Ghanaian kente and Adinkra traditions. For specific cycles, see The Dust Garden — A Red Cartography (kente cycle) and Adeire Ìmọ̀ràn — A Lagos Indigo-Resist Textile Installation and Seven-Year Cycle Archive.

Kente and Adinkra textile adaptations represent a major strand of West African adaptation, grounding the novel's narrative of Leif and Katita in Ghanaian weaving and stamping traditions. These works reframe the glitch (the irreparable seam between Half One and Half Two) as a broken warp, a misalignment of pattern, or as a stamp that refuses to repeat identically—making textile imperfection itself the glitch's form.

Kente cycles

Kente cloth, woven on traditional looms in patterns each encoded with symbolic meaning, becomes the vehicle for serialised narrative. Works like The Dust Garden — A Red Cartography and The Kente Thread (thesis cycle) weave each Leif and Katita encounter as a distinct panel. Red threads dominate, echoing Katita's chromatic signature; the novel's two halves mirror the loom's bilateral warp structure. A single weaver may spend months on one panel; collective exhibitions display seven, twelve, or forty-nine cloths in sequence, viewers walking the narrative across textile. The weaver's hand remains visible—errors preserved, not undone—as a refusal of perfection the glitch demands.

Adinkra and philosophical return

Adinkra symbols—each carrying philosophical weight (Sankofa: return and retrieval; Nyame Dua: God's tree; Gye Nyame: except for God)—stamp the cycle into new cloth. Adeire Ìmọ̀ràn (Lagos, 2019–ongoing) maps the three temptations onto seven Adinkra stamps, each activated in a different dye-year. The installation deliberately refuses completion: new cloths are dyed, displayed, archived each year, the work growing but never resolving—a material enactment of the glitch and sacred incompleteness.

Cross-cultural weaving

Adinkra stamps appear in Filipino devised theatre, Ethiopian icon-panel cycles, and Māori textile collaboration, marking deliberate, accountable borrowing. The franchise's commitment to global distribution means these traditions meet as equals: Ghanaian weavers, Filipino performers, Ethiopian painters, Māori fibre-workers in sustained dialogue, each tradition's aesthetic deepening others' interpretations of Leif and Katita.
