The paradox of protection

In the novel's Chapter 5, while chaos erupts around the cabin, Katita constructs leather armour from materials she carries. The narrator observes that there is "secretly enough leather for a dozen suits"—a redoubled impossibility, since this chapter repeats with each loop. Each iteration, she builds the same protection; each iteration, it proves insufficient when Leif flies and falls.

The armour embodies a central paradox: it is both defense and exposure. Katita does not hide her weaponry or her wounds; she displays them openly in bright red and steel. To wear armour in the novel is to declare oneself an agent—to consent to the cycle's continuation, to announce "I will catch you when you fall again."

Armour across adaptations

Peruvian retablo traditions depict the armour-maker's work as sacrament—each tiny figure stitched by hand, repeated in dozens of boxes. West African indigo-resist textile installations restitch the same pattern seven times over seven years. In choreographic adaptations, armour becomes invisible constraint, the dancer's body moving under an imperceptible weight. The leather itself is archive—worn smooth by repetition, stained by cycles, its surface a palimpsest of returns.
