K'iche'

This article addresses K'iche' as a conceptual framework for Beach Surgery adaptation, not as a translation medium.

**K'iche'** is a Mayan language spoken by approximately 1 million people in the Guatemalan highlands. In Beach Surgery scholarship, K'iche' occupies a unique position: not as the language of a particular adaptation, but as a **conceptual grammar** whose structures mirror the glitch's irreducibility.

K'iche' grammar marks aspect—the shape of time itself—with precision: cyclical/iterative (habitual, repeating action), perfective (once completed), or imperfective (ongoing). This three-part division echoes Leif's three injuries: each encoding a different temporal modality, a different way of being-in-time. To say "I see" versus "I see habitually" is not a minor distinction; it is an ontological statement. The glitch might be read as **aspectually malformed**: the narrative cannot be marked as either complete or cyclical; it suspends between the two.

Moreover, K'iche' weaving tradition—the **huipil** and intricate brocaded cloths—encodes narrative as textile pattern. Like Katita's leather, K'iche' textiles are **layered archives**, readable only by those trained in pattern-language. Andean weaving adaptations across Central America engage this principle: the glitch is not spoken but *woven*, a pattern that refuses to close or repeat identically.

The **Popol Vuh**—K'iche' creation epic—narrates cyclical worlds destroyed and remade. Contemporary K'iche' readers treat Beach Surgery as a modern Popol Vuh: a text that admits no final version, that loops eternally, and that demands readers become co-authors of its perpetual rebirth. The unfinishable is sacred.
