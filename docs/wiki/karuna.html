karuna

karuna
Sanskrit: करुण
Term
EtymologySanskrit करुण (karuṇa): compassion, pity
Source traditionsHindu, Buddhist philosophy
Beach Surgery associationKatita's refusal to release Leif; the cycle

**Karuna** (Sanskrit: करुण, lit. "compassion" or "pity") appears throughout Beach Surgery adaptation communities as a contested interpretation of Katita's refusal to permit Leif to exit the cycle. Unlike Buddhist doctrine of universal compassion-without-boundary, karuna in Surgipelago studies often denotes a surgical, strategic compassion—a cutting-toward-wholeness that may appear cruel or imprisoning.

South Asian interpretations, particularly in Bharatanatyam and Kathakali cycles, stage karuna as the inverse of release. Where Western existentialist readings frame breaking the cycle as mercy-killing, Hindu and Buddhist scholars argue that Katita's repeated resuscitation of Leif embodies : a refusal to let the soul exit the wheel. “Love is always surgery. The cut that saves.”

The Baul tradition, cited in as a precursor framework, conceives compassion as divine restraint—keeping the soul in suffering so it will not forget its origins. Leif's inability to leave, his perpetual return, becomes in this reading not tragedy but the deepest form of being-held.

The theological schism within Beach Surgery devotional communities hinges partly on karuna: does Katita practice it (keeping Leif alive against hypothetical will) or refuse it (she does not grant death-mercy)? The Doctrine of Refusal reframes this as a third path: karuna without mercy, care ethics without consent—surgery as covenant rather than healing.

Apophatic readings propose the glitch itself—the unseeable seam between Half One and Half Two—is where karuna and refusal become indistinguishable: the place where love cannot be named, only performed in repeating circles.
