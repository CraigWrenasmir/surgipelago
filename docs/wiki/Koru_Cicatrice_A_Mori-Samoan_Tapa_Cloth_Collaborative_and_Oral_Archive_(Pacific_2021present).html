Koru Cicatrice — A Māori-Samoan Tapa Cloth Collaborative and Oral Archive (Pacific, 2021–present)

This article concerns a collaborative textile and oral history project centered in Aotearoa New Zealand and Samoa. For related textile works, see Textile arts and the franchise and Koru (symbol).

ⓘThe oral archive is continuously being collected and documented (2021–present). Some recorded testimonies are  not yet publicly available ; see project website for access requests.

**Koru Cicatrice** is a collaborative textile and oral-history project uniting Māori (Aotearoa New Zealand) and Samoan textile practitioners, rooted in the traditional practice of beating and decorating **tapa** (Pacific bark cloth). The project interprets Beach Surgery's eternal recurrence and the glitch through the lens of Pacific spiral symbolism—the **koru**, a motif of new life, growth, and cyclical return in Māori art, and the **fala** and spiral patterns of Samoan siapo traditions.

Each tapa cloth in the archive is hand-beaten from the inner bark of the **paper mulberry** (Broussonetia papyrifera), a process requiring three to five days per cloth. Natural dyes—derived from local plants, minerals, and traditionally harvested materials—create layered reds, whites, blacks, and earths. The artists then hand-draw or stencil koru spirals and protective patterns (rauponga), embedding them into the cloth. Each completed cloth is accompanied by an oral testimony: weavers, community members, students, and readers record voice narratives explaining what the cycle means to them, how they read the glitch, and what the spiral motif carries in their own family histories.

The project explicitly situates Beach Surgery's themes—recursion, incompleteness, the refusal to conclude—within Pacific epistemologies of cyclical time and ancestral return. artist (quoted in project documentation): “The koru isn't a beginning or an end. It's a turn. In our thinking, the cycle completes itself through return. The glitch in the story, that seam that won't join—that's familiar to us. Our stories also live in the gaps.”

Tapa cloths are never sold; they are held in a public archive accessible by appointment in Auckland and Apia. The oral testimonies are gradually being transcribed and made available (in English, te reo Māori, and Samoan) through the project's digital repository. The collective has declined international museum acquisition offers, emphasizing that the work belongs to the communities that created it.

Since 2021, the project has trained over 40 apprentice weavers, expanding beyond the founding artists. Satellite studios have opened in  Rarotonga  (Cook Islands) and  Suva  (Fiji) [citation needed]; a fourth location in  Honolulu, Hawaiʻi  [citation needed] is in planning stages.
