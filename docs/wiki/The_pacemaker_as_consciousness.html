The pacemaker as consciousness

This article is a philosophical reading of the pacemaker motif in Beach Surgery. For the device itself and its role in the narrative, see Heart out of whack.

ⓘDraws on Subject (Ontological Incompleteness) by C. W. Smith; readers should consult that essay for the full Möbius framework.

Leif's **external pacemaker**—a blinking red diode on his chest, keeping his arrhythmic heart beating in a regulated rhythm—functions not merely as a medical device but as a philosophical emblem of consciousness itself, read through C. W. Smith's theory of ontological incompleteness.

The Sovereignty Problem

In Smith's foundational essay, consciousness arises in the irreducible gap between the thinking self and the self that is thought—a //less than nothing// between 0 (the real) and 1 (the ideal), mapped as the single side of a Möbius strip. Leif's pacemaker renders this gap //visible//. His heartbeat—the most intimate, autonomous rhythm—is governed by an external sovereign. The pacemaker is consciousness externalized, made foreign to the body it serves.

Authority as Temptation

In the novel's structure, the three injuries mirror Dostoevsky's three temptations from *The Brothers Karamazov*: the **pacemaker** embodies **authority**—the surrender of self-governance to a machine that keeps the body alive. Katita's removal of the pacemaker precedes Leif's eruption of wings, suggesting that only by refusing the external sovereign can flight occur. Yet flight ends in a catastrophic fall, and the cycle loops: the pacemaker is reinstalled, and the temptation is suffered anew.

The Möbius Reading

If consciousness is the gap (the side that goes all the way around), then the pacemaker is not //inside// Leif and not //outside//—it is the topology itself. Removing it does not free the heart; it destabilizes the surface on which consciousness can exist. Thus Leif's refusal to ask his twelve-word question, his silence in the face of authority, becomes an act of sacred incompleteness: the machinery of governance can only work if accepted, yet acceptance is death-in-life. The unasked question suspends both.

Global Interpretations

Eastern European, Persian, and Southeast Asian adaptations have read the pacemaker as a site of colonial sovereignty (foreign power governing the indigenous body). Indigenous and postcolonial readings frame its removal as a refusal of medicalized governance—a return to embodied self-determination, even if that self is broken.
