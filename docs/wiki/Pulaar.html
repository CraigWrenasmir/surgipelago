Pulaar

Pulaar is a Niger-Congo language spoken by Fulani communities across West Africa. This article concerns linguistic and oral adaptations of Beach Surgery in Pulaar.

Pulaar
Oral Transmission, Griotic Rhythm, and the Language of Refusal
Language Details

Speakers~20 million across Senegal, Mali, Guinea, Cameroon, etc.
FormsCommunity radio broadcast; griotic oral narrative; poetry cycles; call-and-response

Key motifSound and rhythm as narrative; the sound of the earth rubbing against space; tonal rupture
Dates~2019–2023 (broadcasts); archive recovered 2024

Pulaar—language of Fulani peoples spanning Senegal, Mali, Guinea, Cameroon, and beyond—became a vector for Beach Surgery adaptation through community radio broadcasts and griotic oral-narrative performances beginning circa 2019. [citation needed] Unlike written adaptations, Pulaar versions privileged sound and rhythm as narrative: the language's griotic heritage, its tonal musicality, and oral-formulaic structures rendered the glitch not as a structural seam but as a linguistic rupture—a moment where the language itself fails to join.

The Dakar Frequency Archive (recovered 2024; status disputed) contains fragmented recordings of Pulaar-language community radio dramas, dated variously 2019–2023, broadcast on pirate frequencies in Dakar, Bamako, and Kinshasa. One transmission renders Chapter 1 entirely through call-and-response griotic verses; another layers Leif and Katita's dialogue with descriptions of real Senegalese landscapes (river, dust, military checkpoints). The broadcasts mention "the next field" repeatedly—a location never named in the novel—suggesting adapters were composing a West African geography around the cycle.

A thesis by  ██████  at the University of Dakar (2023) argues that Pulaar grammar itself—its tonal-marking, aspect-marking, and recursive-action systems—renders the cycle intelligible in ways English cannot. The archive holds voice-recordings of oral performances; no written transcripts have circulated; attempted French translations remain incomplete. Scholars dispute whether the transmissions constitute a single adaptation or multiple riffs by different collectives.
