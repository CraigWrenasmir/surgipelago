The Surgery as Covenant—Apophatic Theology, Sacred Refusal, and the Doctrine of Incompleteness in Ethiopian Orthodox Readings of C. W. Smith's *Beach Surgery*

Hiwot Mesfin's 2024 thesis argues that C. W. Smith's *A Complicated Surgery Will Take Place on the Beach Tonight* engages with **apophatic theology**—the tradition of articulating the divine through negation, refusal, and silence—as understood within Ethiopian Orthodox mysticism and broader Eastern Christian thought. The thesis traces three intertwined theological strands:

1. The glitch as apophatic absence

Mesfin reads the structural fault at the seam of Beach Surgery—the irreparable break between the story's two halves—as homologous to apophatic principle: the ultimate reality cannot be named, joined, or made whole. Leif's twelve-word question, never quoted in full, is interpreted as an apophatic utterance—a refusal to speak the name, a via negativa (doctrine of refusal) at the heart of narrative itself.

2. Sacred incompleteness as covenant

Drawing on Ethiopian Orthodox theology of the Incarnation and the visual language of icon-panel traditions, Mesfin argues that Katita's refusal to "finish" or "break" the cycle is not failure but theological fidelity: incompleteness is the mark of one's covenant with the transcendent. To demand closure would be to presume mastery over what must remain inexhaustible.

3. Surgery as sacramental becoming

The novel's hidden revelation that the "complicated surgery on the beach" is, in fact, childbirth is read through Orthodox sacramental theology of *theosis* (deification)—the doctrine that the human body becomes the site of divine participation and transformation. Red becomes the colour not of wound alone but of life-giving threshold.

Mesfin supports her argument through close linguistic analysis of the novel, interviews with contemporary Ethiopian and Armenian religious scholars, and comparative reading with monastic and mystical texts. The thesis concludes that *Beach Surgery* articulates a contemporary apophatic theology grounded in embodied material practice, refusal of final meaning, and the sacramentality of incompleteness.

Reception

The thesis won the 2024 Addis Ababa University Prize for Outstanding Work in Religious Studies. It has become a touchstone in the emerging scholarly field of sacred incompleteness within Beach Surgery theology and religious adaptation studies more broadly. Subsequent theses on global Beach Surgery devotional communities cite Mesfin's framework.[citation needed]
