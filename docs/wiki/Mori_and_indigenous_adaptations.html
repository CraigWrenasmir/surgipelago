Māori and indigenous adaptations

For Samoan-specific practice, see Samoan. For First Nations and Canadian work, see First Nations forms. For symbol analysis, see Koru (symbol) and Koru and Beach Surgery.

Māori and Indigenous Australian adaptations
Conceptual frameworks
Māori traditionWhakapapa (genealogy and layering); koru (spiral recursion); oral law
Aboriginal traditionSonglines; country as narrative; refusal of closure
Shared principleCyclical time; genealogy as archive; sacred incompleteness
Key practitionersKoru Cicatrice; Yolŋu songlines collectives; independent artists

Māori and Aboriginal Australian adaptation recognises the glitch—the unjoinable seam between Half One and Half Two—as a form of apophatic truth existing *outside* colonial reconciliation. Whakapapa (Māori genealogy) operates as both ancestral layer and narrative structure: each adaptation adds a new layer, each layer preserves and transforms what precedes it, no layer is ever "finished." Yolŋu songline work treats the story as a path walked and redrawn each generation, its meaning inseparable from land and unrepeatable sound.

Leif and Katita are recognised as manifestations of knowledge-systems Indigenous cultures have always held: the spiral that cannot close, the genealogy that never resolves, the refusal of closure as sacred. Koru Cicatrice and parallel tapa-based collectives enact deliberate refusal of documentation—an archive existing only in whakapapa and oral knowledge, never fully catalogued in colonial systems. This refusal is the adaptation.

The glitch is not a problem to be solved. It is the structure of life itself, spiralling backward and forward through generations without end.
