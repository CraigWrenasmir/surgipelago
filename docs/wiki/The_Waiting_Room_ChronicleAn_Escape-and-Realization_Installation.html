The Waiting Room Chronicle—An Escape-and-Realization Installation

This article documents an immersive adaptation. For the novel's medical settings, see The Makeshift Clinic and Chapter 4.

The Waiting Room Chronicle
Logistics
FormatImmersive escape/puzzle installation with recognition mechanic
Duration45–60 minutes
Capacity6–8 participants per session
LocationsRotating venues:  temporary medical clinic, ████████ ;  abandoned maternity ward, Wollombi ;  makeshift surgery set, rural NSW ; site-responsive variants
Design
Designer(s) ████████ Collective  [pseudonymous]
First opened2021,  ███████ 
Inspired byChapter 4, Chapter 6, The Makeshift Clinic, Katita's medical protocols, the novel's frame (narrator as first-time father)

The Waiting Room Chronicle is an immersive installation disguised as an escape-room puzzle. Visitors enter a reconstructed medical waiting room—dated linoleum, outdated posters, an unmanned receptionist desk—and are told they are waiting for a "consultation." They complete an intake form.

What follows is a slow-dawning realization: the form asks about previous visits to *this room*; magazines are dated inconsistently; a wall calendar cycles through months in the wrong order. A disembodied receptionist (actor or recording) begins addressing visitors by names they did not provide. Participants understand they are not waiting for an appointment but trapped in a *loop* of waiting, possibly returned here many times before.

The "escape" mechanic requires collective recognition of familiar objects—a child's toy, a coffee cup, a handwritten note—as evidence of recurrence. Once visitors name what they recognize, an unseen door becomes visible. Exiting, they read: “Leif. That is you.”

The installation is framed as meditation on recognition: the theology that breaking the cycle requires not action but awakeness—the moment you see you have been here before, you are no longer entirely trapped. The work is intentionally ambiguous: visitors leave unsure whether they have "escaped" or simply entered a new waiting room.

Reported experiences range from profound to claustrophobic. Some visitors return repeatedly, seeking new variants. The installation has inspired derivative works and scholarly writing on incompleteness as affective rather than purely narrative problem. [citation needed]
