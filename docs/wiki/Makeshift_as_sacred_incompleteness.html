Makeshift as sacred incompleteness

For related theological concepts, see Sacred incompleteness as ontological structure, Doctrine of Refusal, Ontological incompleteness. For the embedded makeshift surgery, see The makeshift surgery out the back of New South Wales. For communities built around this principle, see Broken Saints and Eternal Cycles.

Makeshift as sacred incompleteness is a theological principle emerging from Beach Surgery scholarship and fandom, grounded in the novel's hidden makeshift surgery in the red desert interior of New South Wales. It elevates improvisation, broken tools, and unfinished cures to a spiritual category—not as failure but as truth.

The principle rests on a reversal: incompleteness is not a defect of the surgical act but its truest form. Katita, the nurse and agent of the cycle, possesses no proper operating theatre, only a box of medical supplies in a desert car. She has no finished plan for breaking the cycle, only "we can we can we can"—a refrain that never completes into a sentence or a successful action. Every adaptation of Beach Surgery is itself a makeshift cure for an unfinishable wound; suture and glitch are one thing.

The principle derives from apophatic theology—negative theology, the via negativa—which approaches the divine only through negation, through what cannot be said or done. In Beach Surgery theology, surgery becomes sacrament not when it succeeds but when it fails knowingly, embracing the impossibility of cure while persisting anyway. Katita's refusal to acknowledge that Leif cannot be fixed is not denial; it *is* care ethics itself: to act in the face of the irreparable.

The makeshift is further linked to ontological incompleteness—from C. W. Smith's essay Subject (Ontological Incompleteness)—the principle that consciousness arises in the gap between thinking and the thing that thinks, a "less than nothing" (−1) that can never be sutured. The makeshift surgery is a Möbius strip: one surface only, inside and outside the same. To operate *is* to fail; to fail *is* to love; to love *is* to return.

Faith communities have built liturgies around this principle:

  • The Communion of the Breaking Cycle treats each failed attempt at breaking the cycle as a sacred meal—broken bread that nourishes precisely because it cannot heal.
  • Breaking the Cycle—A Participatory Ritual Theatre Work and Seminary (founded 2019) runs a seven-year curriculum teaching "how to be the loser," a phrase from Smith's The Scar in the Dirt, about abandoning victory and learning only to endure.
  • The Incompletion Collective (online, founded 2021) hosts readings specifically to keep the cycle unfinished; completing it would be a betrayal.

The principle has been aestheticised across media:

  • The Makeshift Clinic Pavilion—A Traveling Medical-Art Installation (East Africa, 2019–ongoing) appears in a different configuration in each town, never complete, never identical.
  • The Leather Archives — A Community-Built Spiral Shrine in Accra (2022–ongoing) uses scrap leather and found wood to create a spiral of hand-stitched panels, "a building that grows only by remaining broken."

Criticism has emerged from scholars favouring narrative closure: elevating incompleteness risks romanticising genuine suffering and medical inability. Defenders respond that the makeshift principle does not *celebrate* suffering but **acknowledges it as non-negotiable** and learns to act within it rather than wait for a cure that will never come. To make is to refuse the demand for wholeness.
