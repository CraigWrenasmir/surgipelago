Overview

Raag Ahir Bhairav is a full-length vocal concert adaptation that renders Beach Surgery as an extended journey through the classical morning raag of the same name. Performed by a noted khyal vocalist from the Lucknow tradition, the work uses the structural and emotional arc of Ahir Bhairav—a serious, introspective morning raag rich in unexpected intervals—as a map for the novel's two halves, its glitch, and the impossible resolution of Leif's flight and fall.

The raag is performed in three movements—alap (slow, exploratory meditation), jor (rhythmic acceleration), and jhala (climactic rapid ornamentation)—each aligned to key structural moments in the narrative.

The raag and its character

Ahir Bhairav is traditionally associated with dawn meditation and the moment of first light. Its note-set includes the unusual interval of a flattened second (C# in the raga relative to D=1), creating an unsettling, slightly off-kilter quality. The raag ascends from a low D, moving through the white notes (C#, D, E, F#, G, A, high D), then descends in a slower, more sorrowful trajectory. Classical descriptions emphasize the raag's capacity to express both yearning and futility—the hope of dawn undercut by the knowledge of approaching heat and exhaustion.

A scholar's note: The raag avoids a clear resolution to the tonic. The descent never quite lands; it hovers, suspended, waiting for the next phrase to begin again [1].

Structural mapping

The vocal adaptation maps the raag's three movements to the novel's unfolding:

  • Alap (90–120 minutes) — Newcastle and Half One. The vocalist explores the raag's fundamental character through slow, meditative improvisation, moving upward in register. This corresponds to Leif and Katita's slow rooftop passage, the descent into the underground pool, the emergence at dusk. The raag's white notes emerge one by one, like revelation; the unusual flattened second mirrors Leif's doubled vision, the sense that something is "off" in the fabric of the world.
  • Jor (40–50 minutes) — Half Two and the desert interior. The rhythm becomes steady (tal-based, typically a Tintal cycle), and the vocalist accelerates through the raag's ascending phrases with increasing urgency. The data-harvesters, the mechanic's two faces, Katita's silence—all reflected in the quickening pace. The raag moves upward, higher in register, building pressure.
  • Jhala (20–30 minutes) — Leif's wings and the fall. At the climax, the vocalist performs rapid, virtuosic ornamentation (called tan), spiraling through the raag's upper register with extraordinary speed. The effect is exhilarating and vertiginous. Then, abruptly, the piece does not resolve. The vocalist descends only partway back through the scale, leaving the raag unresolved, suspended—mirroring Leif's interrupted flight, the refusal of closure. The final note hangs in silence; the tonic is never returned to.

Verses and textual content

The vocalist uses a mix of traditional raag phrases (alaps, mukhda, antara), Braj Bhasha devotional texts adapted for the work, and newly composed lyrics in contemporary Hindi that paraphrase Katita's breath-count protocol and Leif's twelve-word question. The question itself is never directly quoted; instead, it is evoked through a series of phrases that begin as questions and dissolve mid-utterance, mirroring the structure of Safwa at-Tajdeed.

In the concert recording, the performer is accompanied by a tabla (drum) player whose role shifts across the three movements: leading the rhythm in jor and jhala, but withdrawing to sparse punctuation during the alap, allowing the vocalist full space to explore.

Reception and the doctrine of incompletion

The adaptation has been praised for its refusal to resolve the raag, a choice that is unconventional in concert practice—most khyal performances conclude with a clear return to the tonic. This vocalist's sustained incompletion became a statement of The Doctrine of Refusal: "The raag Ahir Bhairav is the raag of the incomplete dawn, the moment before light is sure. To resolve it would be to abandon the work's central covenant." [2]

The work has influenced subsequent raag-based adaptations and sparked scholarly discussion about whether classical Indian music's emphasis on rasa (emotional completion) can accommodate ontological incompleteness as an aesthetic principle in its own right.

Relationship to other raag adaptations

The work sits within a constellation of raag-based Beach Surgery adaptations: Raag Kafi, performed by  ███████ , offers a different mapping (Kafi as the evening raag of longing and separation); Raag Yaman, interpreted through the Jaipur gharana, emphasizes the raag's grandeur and sublimity. Each raag becomes a complete, contradictory interpretation of the novel, each valid, none final. Together they suggest that the glitch may itself be a phenomenon of translation—the impossibility of rendering the story through a single voice or scale.
