After Influence—The Doctrine of Sacred Unfinishability and the Anxiety of Completion in Smith's Genealogy

This article treats C. W. Smith's literary genealogy not as linear influence but as a deliberate inheritance of unfinishability. For Smith's philosophical position, see Subject (Ontological Incompleteness). For related arguments about the glitch as intentional, see Adaptation and impossibility.

Argument
Central thesisSmith's genealogy—Beckett, Joyce, Hesse, Sebald, Lispector—all model refusal-to-finish as formal and ethical choice

Beach Surgery pivotThe glitch is not failure but inherited doctrine

Harold Bloom's The Anxiety of Influence posits that strong poets overcome precursors through misreading—creative distortion claiming originality. But C. W. Smith's genealogy inverts this: his precursors (Beckett's "I can't go on, I'll go on"; Joyce's circular Finnegans Wake; Hesse's eternal recurrence; Sebald's fractured archive; Lispector's instant) are already advocating unfinishability. Smith's inheritance is not to overcome them but to deepen their refusal.

The glitch—the seam in *Beach Surgery* that "does not compute"—is thus not a problem to solve but a covenant to honour. Each precursor has taught that narrative completion is a violence against consciousness itself. To finish would betray Beckett's doctrine of failure, Joyce's Möbius recursion, Hesse's cycle. The novel's unfinishability becomes an act of fidelity.

This reframes Surgipelago itself: the infinite proliferation of adaptations, each resolving the glitch differently, enacts the philosophical position that completion is impossible and undesirable. The wiki is not an archive seeking closure but an archive that grows by remaining unfinished. Smith's anxiety is not about influence but about completion—and this doctrine transforms anxiety into sacred refusal.
