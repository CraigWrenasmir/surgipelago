The Murnane Interior—The Inland Plane, The Unrepeatable Image, and Smith's Desert Cartography

This article examines Gerald Murnane as a primary literary influence on Smith's work. For the novels epigraph, see Gerald Murnane and the work of C. W. Smith.

The Murnane Interior
Inland space as consciousness
Concept
Central questionHow does Murnane's unmappable plain structure Smith's dual geography?
Key Murnane works*The Plains*, *Inland*, *Tamarisk Row*
Smith parallel textsBeach Surgery, *Antinomicity*, *Pastoral Scanlines*
Shared motifThe image that cannot be held in consciousness

Gerald Murnane's epigraph to Beach Surgery—"The invisible is only what is too brightly lit"—encodes his central obsession: **the image that cannot be held in mind**. In *The Plains* and *Inland*, Murnane maps an interior landscape where consciousness *is* geography, a vast undifferentiated plane from which no vantage point affords vision.

Smith's dual geography—coastal Newcastle and the red-desert interior—inherits this Murneyan split between the seen (the city's ontological incompleteness, buildings that vanish when unobserved) and the unseen (the plain where perspective collapses entirely). The radio igloo's function as temporal corrective mirrors Murnane's obsession with the gap between thought and image: the frequency that corrects Leif's doubled vision arrests the flow of becoming.

Smith's framing strategy—embedding an unfinished outline within a finished memoir—is Murneyan: the narrator exists to confess "I cannot show you what I saw." The unrepeatable bird that exists only in memory, never retrievable as held image, is pure Murnane. **Smith's desert is Murnane's plain refracted through Australian geology**: a space where interior and exterior collapse into a single unmappable terrain.
