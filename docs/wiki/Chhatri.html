Chhatri

This article is about the Beach Surgery architectural intervention. For the traditional form, see external architectural references. For other site-specific works, see Site-specific works and Beach Surgery.

Chhatri
Domed Pavilion Installation, Rajasthan
Installation
LocationRajsamand District, Udaipur, Rajasthan, India
Began2019 (seasonal cycles ongoing)
FormArchitectural intervention; site-specific meditation pavilion
Primary materialsMarble, copper, textile, solar-power systems
Collaborators Collective architects and local stonemasons; names redacted 
Structure
Dome diameterApproximately 8 metres
Height6.5 metres
DesignOpen-air pavilion; permeable to weather, sound, and seasonal light
Central featureCopper spiral floor path, approximately 45 minutes to traverse

The Chhatri adapts the traditional Rajasthani domed pavilion form (historically a cenotaph or ceremonial structure) into a durational piece referencing both the radio igloo of Chapter 4 and the pregnant architecture of Rico the Architect's embedded narrative. The pavilion is intentionally permeable—open-sided, exposed to monsoon winds, dust, and seasonal extremes.

Visitors walk a copper spiral inscribed into the marble floor; the path spirals inward, then outward again, taking roughly 45 minutes. The dome's crown bears copper antennae and solar receivers that generate a low, shifting audible frequency as the sun's angle changes throughout the day. The frequency—described by visitors as "between a hum and remembering"—never repeats exactly, cycling anew with each season.

Embedded within the spiral's turns are textile fragments (silk, cotton, jute) bearing text in Rajasthani, Sanskrit, and English—passages from the novel interwoven with local oral histories and visitor testimony. Every seven days, community facilitators add new fragments by stitching them into the spiral. After seven cycles (49 days), the oldest fragments are carefully removed and archived in a sealed chamber buried beneath the pavilion's center—creating a palimpsest that accumulates and preserves but never becomes complete.

The work stages the glitch not as a narrative problem to be solved but as an irreducible spatial presence. The spiral can be walked, but the cycle cannot be exited; the frequency can be heard, but never pinned to a source. Participants report returning annually, as if the pavilion remembers them.
