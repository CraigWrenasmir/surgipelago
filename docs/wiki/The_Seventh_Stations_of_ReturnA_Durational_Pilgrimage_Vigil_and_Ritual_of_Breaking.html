The Seventh Stations of Return—A Durational Pilgrimage Vigil and Ritual of Breaking

Part of an emergent global practice of Beach Surgery pilgrimage art. Related: The Seventh Stations of the Caught Descent—A Devotional Pilgrimage Walk Through Broken Hill and the Red Desert Interior (2022–present), The Catch That Answers Every Fall (Choreographic Work, 2022).

Ongoing work; documented reports span 2021–present at multiple sites.

*The Seventh Stations of Return* is a decentralized, durational participatory artwork and pilgrimage ritual that began in 2021 and continues, in various iterations, across multiple geographies. Participants ("pilgrims") walk between seven designated stations over 3–7 days, performing specific ritual actions at each threshold, with explicit intent to enact the cycle's refusal and—per organizers—"break" its eternal recurrence.

Origins and decentralization

Origins are deliberately obscured. Early documentation (deleted blog posts, archived email lists, social media threads) suggests spontaneous coordination among Beach Surgery devotees in Broken Hill, NSW, who had gathered to visit sites from Chapter 4 and Chapter 5. A participant account (LiveJournal, September 2021) describes the first "stations walk" as ad-hoc pilgrimage that later formalized into repeatable ritual.

By 2023, iterations had emerged independently in Newcastle, Kano (Nigeria), Nairobi (Kenya), and a simulated "Shanbudia" LARP venue (Melbourne). No single organizing body exists; each iteration is planned locally via encrypted messaging and a loosely maintained wiki [citation needed].

The seven stations

Documented accounts vary. Most iterations include:

1. **Station of First Sight** — pilgrims arrive blindfolded (echoing Leif's bandaged eyes), guided by sighted escorts; blindfolds removed at threshold. 2. **Station of Pressure** — prolonged lying on earth (30 min–2 hours); participants report sensations in shoulder blades; "pressure witnesses" document testimony. 3. **Station of Heartbeat** — collective listening in silence; synchronized breath-counting; exposure to pre-recorded pacemaker sound [citation needed]. 4. **Station of Red Dust** — pilgrims rake patterns in earth or sand (mimicking Katita's dust garden); patterns photographed and archived. 5. **Station of the Rope** — two groups hold parallel ropes and walk toward each other in silence; meeting or non-meeting is the action [citation needed]. 6. **Station of Armour** — participants dressed in handmade leather vests by facilitators; vests removed and stored in "armour accumulation." 7. **Station of Refusal** — final station described only as "silence" or "the place where ritual does not conclude" [citation needed].

Theology of incompletion

The pilgrimage is explicitly framed as ritual enactment of sacred incompleteness. Rather than "resolving" the glitch or "finishing" the story, participants embody unfinishability—walking the cycle without breaking it, or breaking it without knowing if the break holds [citation needed].

Call-and-response chanting is used at most stations, drawing from the novel, Pastoral Scanlines, and original participant composition. A frequently repeated refrain: “We can we can we can we—” (echoing the novel's final loop-reset).

Geographic variation

Broken Hill (winter, typically July) remains most established, with 40–80 documented participants in 2022–2024. Newcastle (spring) incorporates Half One sites: Bolton Street car park, the underground baths, the breakwall. A 2023 Nairobi iteration adapted the seven stations to walking routes through Makoko lagoon district, treating water crossings as primary thresholds. Melbourne's simulated Shanbudia (2023–ongoing) recreates a fictional conference-dome city, staging stations as indoor navigation puzzle combined with improvised role-play.

Archive and documentation

Participant-generated documentation (photographs, video, testimony) disperses across subreddits, archived Tumblr, WhatsApp groups, and private journals. A more formal archive project, **The Stations Repository**, began in 2024; institutional affiliation remains unclear [citation needed].

Critical reception

Some fandom scholars critique the Stations as "false resolution"—that ritual enactment of refusal is itself completion, resolving through embodiment what the novel leaves permanently unresolved. Others argue this misses the point: the Stations are designed to fail, to loop, to leave participants in the cycle they entered [citation needed]. A 2023 University of Newcastle symposium examined ethical and legal questions surrounding durational group LARPs and their relationship to spiritual practice and fandom.
