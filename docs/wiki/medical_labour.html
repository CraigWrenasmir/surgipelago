medical labour

This article explores medical work and care labour as central to Beach Surgery's ethics. For gender dimensions, see gender in Beach Surgery adaptations. For care ethics grounding, see care ethics and Beach Surgery.

Medical labour in Beach Surgery
Scope
Primary figureKatita (triage nurse turned field surgeon)
Key siteThe makeshift surgery out the back of New South Wales
Ethical frameTriage, refusal, incompleteness
Cross-oeuvreThe wife (C. W. Smith's medical intern); Mylar (surgeon within Rico's story)

Katita is, first and foremost, a triage nurse. Her work begins not in heroic intervention but rapid assessment under constraint—deciding who can be saved, who cannot, how to allocate insufficient resources. This labour is invisible, gendered, and foundational to the narrative.

The novel's frame reveals Smith's wife as a medical intern (encountered at a Street Fighter tournament); Katita synthesises this lived knowledge with surgical strategy. Her makeshift surgery out the back of New South Wales is not a failure of proper medicine but a refusal of it—she does not attempt to restore Leif to function but to keep him in motion toward the cycle's repeated attempt at breaking. The paradox: you cannot cure what you cannot name.

Global adaptations emphasise this dimension: medical labour as care, unpaid or underpaid, conducted by women and racialized workers in resource-poor settings. The three temptations reading frames her surgical choices as refusal—she does not fully heal (which would trap him in gratitude), does not abandon (which would be cruelty), but maintains the tension defining their relation. Triage becomes ethical practice: you cannot cure the glitch, but you can tend it. You can bear witness. You can refuse to let it close.
