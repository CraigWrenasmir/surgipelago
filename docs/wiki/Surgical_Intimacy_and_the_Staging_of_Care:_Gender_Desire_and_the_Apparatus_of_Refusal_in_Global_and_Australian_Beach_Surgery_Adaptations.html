Surgical Intimacy and the Staging of Care: Gender, Desire, and the Apparatus of Refusal in Global and Australian Beach Surgery Adaptations

This article discusses a 2023 doctoral dissertation. For the broader critical literature on erotics in adaptations, see Erotics in Beach Surgery—Critical and Creative Responses.

"Surgical Intimacy and the Staging of Care" is a 2023 doctoral dissertation examining how intimacy and desire between Leif and Katita have been represented, staged, choreographed, and contested across global adaptations, with particular emphasis on Australian theatre, dance, and visual culture.

The dissertation argues that adaptations do not treat the cabin embrace or the final catch at sunrise as romantic resolution, but as sites of care ethics in structural collapse—the touching of wounded bodies that is simultaneously surgery, comfort, and the administration of the temptations. This reading, grounded in care ethics philosophy and disability representation, reframes the erotics question: desire and care are not opposed but interwoven, and consent is inseparable from the fashioned devotion that Katita has "engineered in the most surgically strategic of ways."

Methodology

The dissertation employs close textual analysis, performance ethnography, and reception history to examine:

; Theatre and choreography : The Catch That Answers Every Fall (Choreographic Work, 2022) by  ██  is a primary case study—a duet rendering the embrace as a sequence of catches and releases, examining what it means to hold a body in pain. The author attended multiple Australian performances and conducted interviews with choreographer and dancers. The Makeshift Clinic (Verbatim-Movement Theatre Piece) (2016) is analysed for staging surgery as intimate labour, foregrounding the gender of Katita as nurse-assassin.

; Opera and vocal music : The Surgery at Styx Creek (Opera, 2019) receives intensive analysis, particularly Katita's silence at the end. The author contrasts this with Karman Reversal (opera) (Russian, 2007), which renders the embrace as explicit and lyrical, and Contra-Marcha (The Counter-March, 2011) (Colombian), which stages erotics as political refusal.

; Visual and installation art : Australian works by  ██  and  ██  render the embrace through abstraction, colour, and spatial installation—attempting to stage desire without representation, to make the wound visible without depicting bodies.

Argument one: The wound as permitted touch

The dissertation traces the wound across adaptations, arguing that surgery creates a space in which touching—otherwise governed by social prohibition—becomes necessary, even mandatory. This reading is grounded in Katita's armor design, which leaves certain wounds exposed, creating "thresholds of permitted access." The embrace, in this reading, is medical, intimate, and eroticised all at once. Different adaptations resolve this triple bind differently.

Australian works engage most explicitly with this paradox. The author argues this reflects both a local dance tradition of somatic practice and a specifically Australian anxiety about touch—the uncanniness of intimate male friendship, the unspeakable nature of care, the cultural embarrassment around emotional directness.

Argument two: Gender, agency, and fashioned devotion

A major section examines gender politics of Leif's devotion. The novel states: “"she has fashioned [his love] in the most surgically strategic of ways."” Does this denote manipulation? Grand Inquisitor status? Liberatory strategy? Or structural impossibility?

The dissertation shows Australian feminist and queer adaptations have rejected the manipulator reading, embracing instead a third-wave interpretation: Katita's strategy is self-determination through the other. She cannot escape the cycle; she engineers Leif's position within it so that he might see it too. The embrace is not possession but mutual recognition of shared entrapment.[1]

Argument three: Censorship, ratings, and the explicit

A significant chapter documents censorship and ratings disputes. Several countries restricted the opera due to perceived sexual content; by contrast, the opera contains no explicit language. This is read as anxiety about staging desire on disabled bodies—Leif's blindness and lameness, Katita's armor-as-prosthetic. What is censored is vulnerability.

By contrast, Contra-Marcha (The Counter-March, 2011) (Colombian) contains explicit staging of embrace, yet faced no censorship—instead, political opposition for treating breaking the cycle as political revolution. This reveals a cultural hierarchy of offensiveness: desire on disabled bodies > explicit sexuality; political refusal > erotic expression.

Argument four: The apparatus of refusal

The dissertation's most original contribution is the apparatus of refusal—the technical, dramaturgical, and choreographic tools by which adaptations refuse to resolve the consent question. These include:

  • Silence and breath (opera's final scene)
  • Abstraction and colour-field (visual art; no legible bodies)
  • Looping and repetition (dance; return to same moment)
  • Narrative fracture (literature and film)
  • Redaction and censoring within the text (██ marks)

These refusals are not absences but productive: they foreground the glitch as a feature of representation itself, not something to overcome.

Reception

The dissertation was awarded highest honours and widely cited, influencing subsequent critical and creative work on erotics and care ethics. However, scholars have contested the author's focus on Australian cases as privileging a particular cultural-feminist reading, eliding global diversity.[citation needed] The work is being adapted into a monograph for  ██  Press, forthcoming.
