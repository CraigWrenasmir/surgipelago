Doctrine of Refusal—Sacred Incompleteness as Ontological Structure

Closely related to Doctrine of Refusal—Ethics, Consent, and the Surgery-as-Sacrament, which emphasizes bodily consent and care ethics; this article addresses the ontological dimension (the structure of being).

For theological development, see Doctrine of Sacred Incompleteness and The Doctrine of Refusal.

The Doctrine of Refusal—Sacred Incompleteness as Ontological Structure asserts that incompleteness is not a deficiency but the fundamental architecture of being itself.

Philosophical basis

Drawing from C. W. Smith's essay Subject (Ontological Incompleteness), the doctrine proposes that consciousness arises when matter reflects on itself analogously—through what the essay calls "the one-sided coin" and the Möbius strip. The subject (the conscious "I") is not self-identical; it exists as an irreducible split between the thinking ego and the ground that thinks. This gap—figured as −1, "less than nothing"—is not a flaw but the very substance of consciousness.

Consciousness, subjectivity, is an analogy of itself. That gap is you.— [[Subject (Ontological Incompleteness)

Theological application

In Beach Surgery theology, this principle reframes the glitch not as a narrative defect to be solved but as an ontological truth. Leif and Katita recur eternally not because they are trapped in a loop but because they are the gap itself made flesh—instruments of the impossibility of wholeness.

Refusal, in this doctrine, means:

  • Refusing the temptation to complete the unfinishable story
  • Refusing to heal what is structurally broken
  • Accepting incompleteness as grace

Scholarly synthesis

Western precedent: apophatic (negative) theology; Heidegger's forgetting of Being; Lacan's constitutive lack; Derrida's différance. South Asian: Advaita Vedanta's Brahman as neti neti ("not this, not this"); Madhyamaka Buddhism's śūnyatā. East Asian: the Kyoto School on absolute nothingness; Zen koan as structural impossibility. African-diasporic: Sylvia Wynter's critique of "Man" as a bounded epistemic category; Achille Mbembe's necropolitics—the gap between sovereign law and the body that exceeds it.

Global Beach Surgery devotional communities have instantiated the doctrine in ritual practice: nocturnal pilgrimage vigils, collective heartbeat ceremonies, and multi-day refusal walks centered on accepting rather than resolving incompleteness.

The surgery cannot be completed. The beach cannot be reached. This is not a tragedy; this is the condition of being touched by another.— From The Gospel in Outline Form—Sacred Incompleteness and the Hermeneutics of Refusal in New Religious Movements Centered on C. W. Smith's Beach Surgery, doctoral dissertation, 2024
