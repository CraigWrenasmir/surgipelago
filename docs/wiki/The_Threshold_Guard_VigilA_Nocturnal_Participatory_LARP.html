The Threshold Guard Vigil—A Nocturnal Participatory LARP

This article is about the LARP adaptation. For the theological concept, see The Threshold Cannot Hold.

The Threshold Guard Vigil
Format & Schedule
TypeParticipatory durational LARP, night-based
Duration6 hours (22:00–04:00, rotating nights)
Capacity8–20 participants per session
SitesMultiple rotating locations: Bolton Street car park (Newcastle), disused rail corridor (Broken Hill), King Edward Park (Newcastle), ruined Jolly Roger Hotel,  ██  station platform
Thematic Frame
Based onChapter 3 and Chapter 6 threshold moments; threshold as sacred seam
Core mechanicParticipants take roles as Witnesses; perform vigil at moments of state-change between Leif's injuries and the cycle's turning
Liturgical functionDevotional practice rooted in franchise theology of incompleteness

The Threshold Guard Vigil is a participatory nocturnal LARP that invites participants to inhabit the roles of sacred witnesses to moments of state-change in Leif and Katita's journey. Operating since 2020 across rotating sites in Newcastle and the NSW interior, the vigil enacts a theology of incompleteness through embodied presence at liminal spaces.

Participants are briefed on one of the three injuries or a threshold moment—the crash, the wings erupting, the moment of reset—and stationed at a nominated site: a rooftop edge, a stairwell landing, a pool's edge, a platform. Movement is restricted; speech minimal. Facilitators guide meditative attention to breathing, to the phrase "we can do it we can do it we can do it we—," and to the premise that the cycle turns only when witnessed, that presence itself is a form of refusal.

The experience is emotionally austere. Participants report sensing the earth's rubbing, a dislocated temporality, and collective recognition that "the threshold cannot hold" not as failure but as the structure of care itself. Sessions conclude at dawn; Katita's final words are spoken together by all: “We need to break the cycle. We can do it we can do it we can do it we—”

Theological uptake has been mixed. Some franchise congregations view it as valid devotional praxis; others argue it instrumentalizes the text's refusal by staging it as performance.
