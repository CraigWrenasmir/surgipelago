A comparative study examining how [[Kyoto School]] philosophy—particularly the concept of absolute nothingness (''śūnyatā'', '''mu''' 無)—illuminates [[the glitch]] as a theological rather than narrative problem.

The Kyoto School, founded by [[Nishida Kitarō]], grounded Japanese modernity in a paradox: the "absolute nothing" that is not emptiness but the ground of all being. This essay argues the glitch functions identically. It is not a seam *between* the novel's two halves but the '''essence''' that makes both halves possible. [[Leif]] and [[Katita]]'s inability to join is not a solvable problem but a revelation of [[ontological incompleteness]] itself—the [[Möbius strip|one-sided coin]].

The glitch becomes [[Koan and the glitch|a koan]]: an unsolvable paradox that generates wisdom through refusal. Each adaptation's contradictory resolution mirrors the Kyoto School's embrace of paradox as deepest truth. Drawing on [[Dzogchen Unfinished|Dzogchen Buddhist]] and apophatic Christian parallels, the study shows how [[Breaking the Cycle|breaking the cycle]] and [[Sacred Incompleteness as Ontological Structure|sacred refusal]] represent enlightenment through negation, not spiritual failure.

The author concludes: the glitch is the void made visible, and Surgipelago—an archive that can never be complete—is the void given form. [ref:kyoto|*The Kyoto School and Beach Surgery: A Genealogy of Sacred Absence*, ((████████ University Press)), 2024.]
