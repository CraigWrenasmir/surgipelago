In Beach Surgery adaptation scholarship, '''decolonial''' thinking frames [[the glitch]]—the irreparable structural fault between the story's two halves—not as a failure to be resolved but as a refusal of the colonial demand for ''completion, mastery, and linear time''. Decolonial theorists like [[Sylvia Wynter]] and [[Achille Mbembe]] argue that Western narrative insists on closure; the unfinishable thus becomes an act of epistemological sovereignty.

Read decolonially, Katita's '''"We need to break the cycle"''' is not a plea for narrative resolution but an ''invocation of recursive, cyclical time''—time as it exists in [[griot oral epics]], in ''[[wayang]]'' shadow-cycles, in the ''cyclical return'' of seasonal ritual. [[The Doctrine of Refusal]] and [[Surgical Refusal and Indigenous Cure—East African Healing Traditions in Adaptations of Beach Surgery|indigenous cure traditions]] position incompleteness as sacred knowledge, not lack.

Adaptations grounded in [[East African adaptations|East African]], [[West African adaptations|West African]], and [[South Asian adaptations|South Asian]] traditions resist the European frame entirely. The glitch, read this way, is an ''epistemological border''—a threshold where Western narrative logic '''cannot''' enter, and where other ways of knowing (cyclical, polyvocal, incomplete) hold authority. [[C. W. Smith]]'s [[Antinomicity]] quote—"The past is always a spontaneous product of the present"—mirrors decolonial temporality: history is not fixed but recursively composed.
