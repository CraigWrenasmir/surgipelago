**Mudras** are formalized hand and finger gestures in [[Indian classical dance]], [[Baul music|devotional singing]], yoga, and ritual healing, encoding narrative and spiritual state without speech. In Beach Surgery adaptations—particularly those rooted in South and Southeast Asian performance—mudras counterpoint [[Leif]]'s [[hand cannon|gripped weaponry]] and enact [[The doctrine of refusal|refusal]] outside language.

In the novel's [[The frame (the narrator's real life — source of "deep cut" adaptations)|frame]], [[Katita]]'s hands are occupied (triage, touch). But across adaptations, her open palms—a non-mudra, an absence of codified gesture—read as refusal of the [[The Grand Inquisitor|authority]] encoded in traditional hand-language. [[Leif]]'s hand "fused with the cannon" enacts a closed mudra: the hand that cannot open, cannot surrender.

[[South Asian adaptations of Beach Surgery|South Asian retellings]] explicitly stage mudra language. [[Kathak]] cycles use the ''tribhanga'' (three-fold break) to mark [[The three injuries|each injury]]; [[Bharatanatyam]] maps ''asamyukta hastas'' (single-hand seals) to [[Surgical Intimacy|surgical touch]]; [[Baul]] singers incorporate mudra-like gestures to embody the river that "does not yet know it flows."[ref:baul|See [[Baul music and Beach Surgery]].] The mudra becomes paradoxically a language of [[The doctrine of refusal|refusal]]—the hand that gestures ''not-yet'', ''not-here'', ''not-finished''.
