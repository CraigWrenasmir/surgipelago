The motif of **the catch that answers every fall** appears throughout Beach Surgery adaptations as a figure of redemptive intervention: the reaching hand that arrests descent, the embrace that receives what falls. Sourced in the frame narrative, where the narrator anticipates his daughter's birth—*"she instinctively holds her hands out to catch our daughter in her descent"*—the motif reframes [[Leif]]'s catastrophic final crash as a preventable wound, a moment where intervention becomes sacrament.

Across adaptive cultures, the catch cycles through theological registers:
* '''[[Eastern European adaptations]]''' stage it as apophatic refusal—the hand that *does not arrive*, that *must not arrive*, lest the [[the cycle|cycle]] break irrevocably.
* '''Brazilian and Caribbean cordel''' tellings treat it as [[Mylar]]'s gesture inverted: not the surgeon's hand building inside the body, but the lover's hand *outside*, receiving the unsalvageable.
* '''[[East African]] and [[Kenyan]] devotional LARPs''' render it as a '''collective''' act—community witnesses positioned to catch, hold, bear witness; the individual failure becomes structural testimony.
* '''[[Ethiopian icon-panel cycles]]''' show the catch as an eternal present: [[Katita]]'s hands always already outstretched, beyond time.

The motif anchors [[fatherhood, birth and the caught descent]] and [[the doctrine of refusal]]. Its absence in later loops troubles the archive: *has Katita learned to catch? Or does she choose not to?*
