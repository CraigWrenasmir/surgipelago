**Karuna** (Sanskrit: करुण, lit. "compassion" or "pity") appears throughout [[Beach Surgery]] adaptation communities as a contested interpretation of [[Katita]]'s refusal to permit [[Leif]] to exit [[the cycle]]. Unlike [[Buddhist]] doctrine of universal compassion-without-boundary, karuna in Surgipelago studies often denotes a surgical, strategic compassion—a cutting-toward-wholeness that may appear cruel or imprisoning.

[[South Asian adaptations of Beach Surgery|South Asian interpretations]], particularly in [[Bharatanatyam]] and [[Kathakali]] cycles, stage karuna as the '''inverse''' of release. Where Western [[existentialism|existentialist]] readings frame [[breaking the cycle]] as mercy-killing, [[Hindu]] and [[Buddhist]] scholars argue that Katita's repeated resuscitation of Leif embodies {{cite|karuna-pravritti|the turning-back of compassion}}: a refusal to let the soul exit the [[wheel]]. {{quote|Love is always surgery. The cut that saves.|Katita, Ch. 6}}

The [[Baul]] tradition, cited in {{cite|The Karuna Doctrine in Baul Readings of Beach Surgery|PhD dissertation 2023}} as a precursor framework, conceives compassion as divine restraint—keeping the soul in suffering so it will not forget its origins. [[Leif]]'s inability to '''leave''', his perpetual return, becomes in this reading not [[tragedy]] but the deepest form of being-held.

The theological schism within [[Beach Surgery devotional communities]] hinges partly on karuna: does Katita practice it (keeping Leif alive against hypothetical will) or refuse it (she does not grant death-mercy)? The [[Doctrine of Refusal]] reframes this as a third path: karuna ''without mercy'', [[care ethics]] ''without consent''—[[surgery as covenant]] rather than healing.

[[Apophatic theology|Apophatic]] readings propose [[the glitch]] itself—the unseeable seam between [[Half One — Newcastle|Half One]] and [[Half Two — The interior|Half Two]]—is where karuna and refusal become indistinguishable: the place where love cannot be named, only performed in repeating circles.
