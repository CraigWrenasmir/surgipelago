Indigenous adaptations of [[Beach Surgery]] are rooted in oral, embodied, and cartographic traditions specific to each culture, treating [[the glitch]] not as a European narrative problem but as a '''wound of memory, dispossession, and temporal fracture''' — concepts native to decolonial thought.

== Māori adaptations ==
[[Whakapapa]] (genealogical recitation) and [[pūrākau]] (traditional narratives) inform Māori approaches, which centre [[Leif]] and [[Katita]] as figures circulating through [[Aotearoa]] landscapes. Works include participatory waka ama (canoe) cycles, Māori-language radio serialisations, and carving traditions treating the [[wings]] as ''manaia'' (guardian spirits). The [[Koru Cicatrice]] textile collaboratives weave the glitch into tapa cloth and kente traditions, embedding refusal into fibre itself.

== Aboriginal Australian adaptations ==
Aboriginal songlines and dot-painting traditions reframe [[the cycle]] as [[tjurunga]] (sacred knowledge bound to country). The [[Klamath Sang the Glitch]] oral storytelling cycle (1997–ongoing) treats [[Katita]]'s journey through desert country as a return walk, her voice the echo-chamber of the land itself. These works emphasise place-memory over plot-resolution.

== Pacific and Pacific diaspora ==
Samoan [[gafa]] (genealogy), Hawaiian ''aloha 'āina'' (land-love), Tongan ''fatongia'' (reciprocal obligation), and other [[Pacific]] frameworks generate song-cycles, shadow-puppet innovations, and immersive navigation works. The [[Koru Cicatrice — A Māori-Samoan Tapa Cloth Collaborative|Koru Cicatrice]] brings Māori and Samoan fibre traditions into dialogue around the shoulder-blade spiral.
