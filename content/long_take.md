The long take — an extended uninterrupted shot — has become a cardinal technique in Beach Surgery cinema, particularly among adaptations wrestling with [[the glitch]] as a temporal rather than merely narrative problem.

The long take suspends cinematic cutting, the grammar of rupture and causality. By holding the camera steady through [[Leif]]'s [[doubled vision]] or across the [[dust garden]]'s rake-traced symmetries, filmmakers can render both [[Half One]] and [[Half Two]] visible simultaneously, or present the [[seam]] as an uncuttable continuum. Argentine experimental works (2015–2017) deploy twenty-minute static shots of empty beaches at dusk, theorizing the long take as refusal to explain or moralize. Taiwanese installations from the Taipei Biennial (2019–2021) move durational shots through [[Newcastle]] streets and the [[desert interior]], letting the landscape's own temporality emerge without montage rhythm.

The technique aligns with [[apophatic theology]] — a via negativa refusing to cut, narrate, or resolve. Some durational adaptations hold the long take even through the [[wings]]' eruption and Leif's fall, rendering catastrophe as continuous descent rather than climactic cut. A 2022 essay-length film by ((██)) theorizes the uncut frame as a refusal of the [[doctrine of refusal]] itself — an anti-apophatic stance where cutting becomes sacred [cn].
