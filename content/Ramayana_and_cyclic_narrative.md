The *Ramayana* and *Mahabharata*—Sanskrit literature's foundational epics—encode a **cyclic cosmology** fundamentally aligned with Beach Surgery's [[the cycle|core doctrine of the cycle]]. Both are structured not as linear narratives but as infinite nested loops: the *Ramayana* recounts Ram's exile and return, mirroring the cosmic cycle of yugas (ages), each dissolving and recommencing. The *Mahabharata* concludes not with finality but cyclical return: heroes depart for heaven, the world begins again.

This structure—**recurrence without closure**—maps directly onto [[the glitch]]: the irreparable seam between Beach Surgery's two halves that forces every adaptation to loop back and re-imagine the bridge. The unfinishable is not a flaw but a feature, reflecting Hindu [[the cycle|samsara]] (the wheel of becoming) and [[the cycle|maya]] (the eternal play of return).

In regional [[South Asian adaptations of Beach Surgery|South Asian adaptations]]—particularly [[Kathak Cycle of the Three Injuries|Kathak cycles]] and [[Varanasi adaptations of Beach Surgery|Varanasi performance works]]—[[Leif]]'s three injuries are explicitly mapped onto the three **gunas** (sattva, rajas, tamas), the three qualities of existence. [[Katita]]'s refusal to "break the cycle" mirrors the spiritual paradox of moksha (liberation): the desire to escape the wheel is itself ignorance; only acceptance of eternal return brings peace.

The parallels are not metaphorical borrowing but deep structural consonance: both traditions understand narrative as cosmological, and the unfinished as sacred.
