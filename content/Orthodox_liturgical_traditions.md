Orthodox liturgical traditions offer readings of Beach Surgery grounded in [[apophatic theology]] — the tradition of approaching the divine through negation and refusal. Within this framework, [[the glitch]] becomes theologically coherent: not narrative failure but a threshold between being and non-being, figured as [[Doctrine of Refusal—Sacred Incompleteness as Ontological Structure|sacred incompleteness]].

In [[Ethiopian Orthodox Church|Ethiopian Orthodox]] tradition, particularly the communities performing [[Debre Damo Cycle|icon cycles]], the story is read as a rite of [[theosis]] — the transfiguration of human nature. [[Leif]]'s [[three temporary injuries]] map onto the [[Grand Inquisitor]] temptations (mystery, miracle, authority), each a refusal of easy redemption. [[Katita]]'s red armor becomes liturgical vestment; the surgery itself becomes [[Sacrament|sacrament]].

[[Georgian Apostolic Church|Georgian Apostolic]] ensembles adapt the narrative through polyphonic liturgy, where six-part harmonic structures (foundational to Georgian sacred song) encode the impossibility of singular truth — each voice singing the story differently, none resolving. The [[Saitsavlebi Unfinishing|Saitsavlebi Vocal Ensemble's]] recordings exemplify this principle, their untranslatable overlapping phrases creating what one [[Kyoto School]] theorist calls "the glitch as Heideggerian forgetting."

Russian and Armenian communities develop liturgical readings of [[Leif's three injuries]] as [[Temptation Without Refusal—Leif's Three Injuries as Dostoevskian Impasse|apophatic refusal]], the body's refusal to be healed mapping onto the soul's refusal of easy transcendence. The [[Subject (Ontological Incompleteness)|ontological gap]] Smith names — "that gap is you" — becomes in these readings the space where liturgy dwells.
