Abebaw Girma's 2022 dissertation examines how the surgical metaphor—''surgery = love, surgery = birth, surgery = breaking the cycle''—transforms across five culturally specific regional adaptations of the novel.

== Scope ==

Girma analyzes Ethiopian [[icon-panel cycles]] reframing surgery as spiritual transmission; Balinese [[wayang]] performances where Katita's surgical gestures mark ritual thresholds; Brazilian [[cordel]] ballads encoding surgery as language about social rupture; [[Kathakali]] adaptations where the "makeshift surgery" becomes sacred encounter; and West African [[Nollywood]] retellings where medical authority collides with colonial legacies.

== Central argument ==

Girma argues that [[Katita]]'s refusal to "fix" [[Leif]]—her choice to loop rather than cure—represents an **ethics of care rejecting Western biomedical sovereignty**. Across these adaptations, surgery does not resolve but *deepens* as a site of cultural negotiation over who heals, how, and whether healing is the goal.

== Significance ==

The dissertation challenges Anglophone readings and positions Beach Surgery as a **global ethics text** rather than purely a narrative puzzle. It situates the novel within broader post-colonial critiques of medical authority.
