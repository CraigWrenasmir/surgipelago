The [[Yoruba]] oral tradition—transmission through voice, cyclical renewal, the metaphysical reality of the named—illuminates [[Beach Surgery]]'s central paradox: that an unfinished story is more alive than a completed one. In Yoruba [[orature]], the teller (''olórin'', voice-keeper) is not author but conduit; each telling regenerates rather than repeats. [[Leif]] and [[Katita]] function this way: not characters to be resolved but [[instruments of return]], voices the narrator must reanimate across [[loops]].

The [[Aladura Kekere—A Yoruba Travelling Theatre Adaptation|Aladura Kekere cycle]], which stages the same seven scenes across villages, embodies this refusal: no performance is definitive; each is a fresh [[eruption]] into local time and breath. The travelling-theatre lives in the mouth, the road, the gathering—it refuses the archive's fixity.

Yoruba cosmology, where time spirals (not progresses), offers [[language]] for [[eternal recurrence]]. The [[three injuries]] parallel the three "opening mysteries" (''àbàsọ'') of divination: stages of knowing that do not culminate in knowledge but in deeper [[mystery]]. Orisha—[[Oshun]], [[Iyami Ajè]]—appear not as characters but as forces of recursion and refusal.

This kinship with [[griot]] tradition, [[Oromo Recitation Cycle|Oromo recitation]], and [[Kora na Sira—The Cycle Told Through Strings|kora transmission]] suggests a global philosophy of [[sacred incompleteness]]: the [[glitch]] is not a flaw but the [[breath]] itself.
