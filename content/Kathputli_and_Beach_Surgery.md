The adaptation of Beach Surgery through the centuries-old Kathputli string-puppet tradition of Rajasthan stages the core narrative as a performance of fate and repeated wounding. Where traditional Kathputli cycles tell of kings, lovers, and demons caught in loops of karma, the Beach Surgery variant casts [[Leif]] and [[Katita]] as puppet and puppeteer, their roles not fixed but exchanged across repetitions.

The puppeteer's hand — visible in silhouette — becomes [[Katita]]'s surgical hand. The strings become both the threads of narrative and the sutras of bondage and care. Where the glitch refuses narrative closure, the Kathputli version stages this refusal materially: a string breaks mid-scene, cannot be retied, and the performance halts. The audience waits. Silence becomes text.

Practitioners have emphasized the tradition's [[makeshift as sacred incompleteness|makeshift]] economy: puppets are carved from scrap wood, cloth is rewoven, paint fades and is reapplied. This material poverty mirrors [[the makeshift surgery out the back of New South Wales|Katita's makeshift clinic]]. The form's endangered status — as the Kathputli caste faces displacement and cultural erasure — has led contemporary practitioners to adapt Beach Surgery as both innovation and survival, threading the novel's cycles into the tradition's own cyclical repertoire.
