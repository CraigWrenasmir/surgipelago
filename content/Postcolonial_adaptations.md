Beach Surgery adaptations emerging from postcolonial contexts have developed distinct theological and aesthetic frameworks, departing from Anglo-Japanese convention. Rather than resolving [[the glitch]], many postcolonial readers treat incompleteness as '''inherent to living memory and collective refusal.'''[cn]

Textile-based cycles—particularly [[Kente textile adaptations|kente]], [[Arpillera|arpilleras]], and beadwork traditions—predominate in West and East African readings, where the unfinished seam becomes a weaving metaphor for interrupted colonial histories and ongoing repair. [[The Leather Archives—A Community-Built Spiral Shrine in Reclaimed Warehouse Space (Accra, 2022–ongoing)|The Leather Archives]] frames Katita's leather as historical witness; the "enough for the seventh loop" becomes a liturgy of reparation.

South Asian [[Parsi theatre]] and [[Baul]] music adaptations foreground devotion and consent as theological categories, inverting the [[Grand Inquisitor (motif)|Grand Inquisitor]] temptations into ethical choice. [[Surgical Intimacy and the Choreography of Care|Surgical intimacy]] here becomes sacred care work, not domination.

Latin American readings, particularly [[A Cicatriz Que Volta—Romance-Cordel da Cirurgia Eterna|cordel]] pamphlets and [[La Cicatriz Que Huele—Retablo Boxes of Ayacucho|retablo]] cycles, root the eternal return in indigenous cyclical time (''quechua'' ''samsara'', ''ayni'' reciprocity) rather than Nietzschean tragedy. The cycle is not curse but '''obligation to return, repair, and remember.'''[ref:postcolonial_theology|See "The Gospel in Outline Form: Sacred Incompleteness and the Hermeneutics of Refusal in New Religious Movements Centered on C. W. Smith's Beach Surgery," MA Thesis (2023).]

These readings share a refusal of Western interpretive closure.
