A theological and ethical principle emerging across global [[Beach Surgery]] interpretation, positing that ''refusal''—of completion, closure, certainty, final meaning—is itself sacred and generative, not a defect or incompleteness to be overcome. Where traditional narrative theology reads unfinished-ness as narrative failure, the Doctrine of Refusal treats [[the glitch]] as irreducible and covenantal: the one truth that cannot be finished without ceasing to be true.

[[Leif]]'s ''[[The twelve-word question|twelve-word question]]'' remains unquoted throughout the novel; [[Katita]] refuses to articulate her cycle-breaking strategy; the [[A Complicated Surgery Will Take Place on the Beach Tonight|novel itself]] spirals back into recurrence rather than resolving. These refusals are not narrative lacunae but sacred acts—invitations to the reader to refuse closure in turn. Drawing on [[apophatic theology]] (negative theology, knowledge through negation rather than affirmation), the doctrine proposes that what ''cannot'' be said or finished is what must be honored most deeply.

The principle threads through [[Samuel Beckett]]'s ethics of exhaustion and failure, [[Hermann Hesse]]'s ''Glass Bead Game'' and its doctrine of sacred incompleteness, and continental philosophy (Derrida's ''différance'', Blanchot's ''unwritability''). In global [[Beach Surgery]] faith communities, the Doctrine becomes a lived practice: [[breaking the cycle]] is reframed not as ''solving'' the glitch but as learning to dwell inside it without demanding resolution. [[Refusal]] becomes a form of consent—consenting to the irresoluble.
