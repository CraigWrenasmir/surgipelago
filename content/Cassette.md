In regions where licensed audio recordings remained unavailable or prohibitively expensive, pirate-radio networks and street-market vendors generated bootleg cassette recordings of Beach Surgery adaptations, live performances, and oral retellings beginning circa 2009. Each tape was hand-written or stamped; the resulting absence of standardized metadata has complicated later archival recovery and provenance verification.

Bootleggers recorded from shortwave broadcasts, community radio transmissions, and live theatre, then duplicated and distributed through informal networks—night markets in Lagos and Dakar, street vendors in Kolkata, dead drops at railway stations and border crossings. The format proved economically efficient in regions with unreliable electricity and high data costs; a 90-minute cassette cost significantly less than digital purchase or streaming access.

Repeated dubbing introduced what some scholars now read as intentional [[glitch-resolution|glitch composition]] or evidence of [[the sound of the earth rubbing against space|Kármán resonance]] imprinting onto analogue media. Fandom has subsequently treated these artifacts as markers of authenticity—a bootleg is "real" precisely because it degrades, because it carries the marks of passage through many ears.

A subset of cassettes discovered in markets across Ouagadougou and Kinshasa (2015–2017) carried cryptic or missing metadata, including apparent references to geological surveys and tree-antenna frequencies; authentication remains disputed.[cn] One series bore what appeared to be a misfiled filename: "Riverbed_Geological_Survey_Early_Season_1970.txt"—origins unknown. The cassette format was largely superseded by digital bootlegging by 2020, though West African and Caucasian [[Empty World Collective|Empty World Collective]] chapters continue treating the tapes as archival relics and playing them in ritual contexts.
