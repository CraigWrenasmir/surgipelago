'''Storytelling traditions and adaptation''' names the principle that each culture's '''indigenous or regional narrative form''' becomes a unique solution to [[the glitch]]. Since Beach Surgery's structure is permanently unfinished—the two halves do not "compute"—adaptations do not ''resolve'' the glitch but rather '''translate it into the grammar of local storytelling'''.

A '''griotic retelling''' in [[West Africa]] embraces cyclical, polyvocal speech and ancestor-return; the ''incompletion'' feels natural to [[griot oral epics]], where truth exists in ''rhythm and return'' rather than linear plot. A '''Baul singing-cycle''' in [[South Asia]] uses improvisation and call-and-response; the audience ''completes'' the story through participation. A '''cordel pamphlet''' in [[Brazil]] repeats stanzas in spiral patterns; the glitch becomes a structural feature, not a flaw.

This approach inverts the franchise's relation to Western media. Rather than asking "How do we finish Beach Surgery?" each storytelling tradition asks: '''"How does this story already live in our way of telling?"''' The unfinishable becomes '''sacred incompleteness'''—a feature of cosmology ([[samsara]], eternal return), not a narrative failure. [[C. W. Smith]]'s use of [[Pastoral Scanlines|repeated characters across works]] mirrors this: [[Leif]] and [[Katita]] are not recycled but [[Instruments of Return—Leif, Katita, and the Architecture of Recursion Across C. W. Smith's Oeuvre and the Global Beach Surgery Franchise|instruments of return]], surfacing in each tradition's telling as '''new versions of the same eternal conflict'''.
