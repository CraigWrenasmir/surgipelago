The embedded theological frame of Beach Surgery draws—sometimes obliquely, sometimes explicitly—from Dostoevsky's ''Grand Inquisitor'' dialogue, restructuring [[Leif]]'s [[The three temporary injuries — and the three temptations|three temporary injuries]] as a re-staging of Christ's wilderness temptations. The motif is generative: each of Leif's injuries corresponds to one of the Inquisitor's three bribes: [[Cannot see|blindness ↔ mystery]] (faith without proof; Leif follows Katita unsighted), [[Cannot walk|immobility ↔ miracle]] (the promise of being lifted up; the [[the wings|wings]] that erupt at the story's climax), and [[Heart out of whack|the external pacemaker ↔ authority]] (the surrender of inner rhythm to an external sovereign that keeps the body alive).

The motif admits two interpretations, both canonical: either [[Katita]] is the Inquisitor figure, administering the temptations to keep Leif in the cycle; or she is the liberator, struggling to help him *refuse* the temptations, to *break* the cycle. The novel never resolves this ambiguity. [[Temptation Without Refusal—Leif's Three Injuries as Dostoevskian Impasse|Theses and operas]] frequently develop one or the other reading. The motif appears less in [[West African adaptations|African]] works and more prominently in [[European adaptations|European]] and [[Middle Eastern adaptations|Middle Eastern]] cinema.
