Sthanaak is a Hindustani classical music term denoting the ornamented approach to and embellishment of a note—the "manner of standing" at a pitch, the grace and resistance with which a singer or instrumentalist lands on a frequency, chooses to hold it, bend it, inflect it, or abandon it.

In performance, a vocalist might approach a high note via [[meend]] (a slow, winding glissando), oscillate at its edges via [[gamak]] (rapid undulation), and finally arrive at the sthanaak—a tremolo, a catch in the throat, a moment of visible vulnerability—before releasing. The sthanaak is neither the arrival nor the departure; it is the *stance*, the bodily commitment to standing at a frequency for as long as it takes, and the felt cost of that standing.

Beach Surgery adaptations, particularly in Hindustani vocal cycles, treat sthanaak as [[Katita]]'s signature act. Her refusal to smile until the final scenes is a kind of sthanaak—an approach to a frequency (laughter, release, acceptance) held *indefinitely* without reaching it. When she finally does smile (or screams "no" at Leif's crash), the break in sthanaak is itself catastrophic.

In the [[The radio igloo beneath King Edward Park|radio igloo]] chapter, Leif's doubled vision is "corrected" by raising the broadcast frequency to a pitch the [[autonomous diesel data-harvesters|data-harvesters]] cannot perceive. Singers performing this sequence often elongate the sthanaak at that moment—a deliberate over-holding of the corrective tone, as if refusing to let the vision settle. The effect violates classical elegance; it becomes the point: a willful standing-too-long, a refusal of closure.

One thesis proposes that [[the glitch]] itself is a sthanaak—an infinite approach to a frequency that never fully arrives [cn].
