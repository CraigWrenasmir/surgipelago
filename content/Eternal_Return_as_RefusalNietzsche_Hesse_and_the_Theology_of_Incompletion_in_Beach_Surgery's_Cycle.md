The concept of '''Eternal Return as Refusal''' reframes [[Beach Surgery]]'s cycling structure not as a trap to escape but as a sacred covenant to inhabit. [[Katita]]'s determination to "[[Breaking the cycle|break the cycle]]" is read through {{quote|We must love our fate entirely, even the suffering|Nietzsche's concept of ''amor fati''}}—the doctrine that Eternal Return demands absolute assent to recurrence without resentment.

The philosophical impasse: if [[the cycle|the cycle]] returns eternally, any attempt to "break" it is already inscribed within the return. [[Leif]]'s unspoken [[The twelve-word question|twelve-word question]] becomes apophatic: not a demand for liberation, but an acceptance so radical it inverts into the shape of resistance. To ''want'' the cycle to return infinitely is paradoxically to stop seeking escape.

This inheritance runs through [[C. W. Smith]]'s literary sources. [[Hermann Hesse]], in ''Siddhartha'', stages this paradox: the protagonist must ''live'' the river's eternal recurrence (desire, loss, rebirth) fully; enlightenment is not transcendence but return-with-knowledge. Hesse's ''Steppenwolf'' similarly poses the "magic theatre" of infinite recurrence as the condition of becoming human.

''Beach Surgery'' inherits this structure. [[the glitch|The glitch]]—the unseamed break between halves—is not an accident to repair but a necessary aperture through which the cycle becomes visible. [[Leif]] and [[Katita]] do not ''escape'' the loop; they ''consent'' to it, infinitely. The theology is apophatic: one cannot ''say'' the cycle is good; one can only ''live'' the absolute return, and in that living, [[Breaking the cycle|transform its meaning]].
