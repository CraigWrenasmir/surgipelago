Across the franchise, surgery articulates itself as erotic encounter—the incision as caress, the wound as space where care and harm become indistinguishable. This emerges from the novel's core structure: two bodies, one vulnerable, the other wielding agency.

Adaptations stage this consistently: ''Dance'' and ''choreography'' ([[The Catch That Answers Every Fall]]) treat [[Leif]]'s [[Leif's three injuries – and the three temptations|three injuries as three moments of touch]]—blindness as erotic deprivation, paralysis as holding, pacemaker-beat as governance. Each mirrors [[Dostoevsky|Dostoevskian]] temptation: miracle, mystery, authority.

''Textile works'' ([[The Kente Thread—Recurrence, Weaving, and the Doctrine of Sacred Incompleteness in West African Beach Surgery Adaptations and Liturgical Praxis (MA Thesis, 2024)|The Kente Thread]]; [[The Ouagadougou Leatherwork Cycle]]) treat [[Katita's armour|armour-stitching]] as reciprocal caress—each stitch a refusal of separation. ''Film'' ([[The Hands That Hold (Film, 2020)|''The Hands That Hold'']]; [[Red Salt (Film, 2020)|''Red Salt'']]) foregrounds the surgeon's trembling hand, care rendered as vulnerability in the caretaker.

The franchise thus recovers care from sentimentality: to care is to wound, to refuse easy comfort, to let the [[the glitch|glitch]] remain. [[The surgery is the birth|At the novel's frame]], the narrator's wife becomes both [[Leif]] and [[Katita]]—blindfolded, heart-monitored—and the "first breath is just a breath," a caress without demand.
