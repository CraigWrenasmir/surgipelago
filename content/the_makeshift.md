The makeshift is both a practical and theological principle across [[Beach Surgery]] adaptations: the refusal to perfect, the necessary incompleteness of urgent care, the ethics of what works ''now'' rather than what works ''finally''. [[Katita]]'s [[The makeshift surgery out the back of New South Wales|makeshift clinic]] is the novel's avatar—a room where [[Leif]] is tended by hand-sewn bandages, improvised instruments, and the surgeon who will not claim to have finished him.

In [[C. W. Smith]]'s wider oeuvre, the makeshift appears as ontological principle: in *[[Antinomicity]]*, the cyanotype leaf and GPS projector cast palimpsests of family time; in *[[Fellow Disjecta, Oh Sunny Danger Time]]*, the solar-punk community built on reclaimed radar and aircraft remains—each a figure of things remade from ruins. Adaptations treat makeshift as refusal of mastery—theological works ground it in apophatic theology, the via negativa of care.

The makeshift resists [[The Doctrine of Refusal|the doctrine of completion]]; it is '''always enough and never whole'''. [[The makeshift as sacred incompleteness|Scholarship]] identifies it as the glitch made livable—the wound dressed by care, not cured but tended.
