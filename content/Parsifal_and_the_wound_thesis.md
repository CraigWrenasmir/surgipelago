The ''Parsifal and the wound thesis'' is a scholarly framework comparing Wagner's ''Parsifal'' with the [[surgery]] motif in Beach Surgery, examining the failure of healing and the impossibility of narrative closure. Where Wagner's opera stages the fool-made-wise who compassionately heals the Fisher King's wound, the Smith text inverts this arc: [[Katita]], a [[medical intern|medical]] figure, encounters wounds that cannot be sutured—not only [[Leif]]'s [[The three temporary injuries|three temporary injuries]], but the structural [[the glitch|glitch]] itself, a seam that resists suturing across [[Half One — Newcastle|Half One]] and [[Half Two — The interior|Half Two]].

Scholars working from this thesis argue that ''Beach Surgery'' is **anti-Parsifal**: the compassion [[Katita]] shows [[Leif]] is "fashioned in the most surgically strategic of ways," suggesting that healing and agency are always already compromised. The wound is not overcome but perpetually re-opened; each [[Adaptations that resolve the glitch|adaptation that resolves the glitch]] performs a different scar-forming, leaving the original wound unstaunched.

The thesis points to [[Katita as Failed Surgeon—The Wound Thesis|Katita as the true bearer of the wound]]—not [[Leif]], but the one who ''cannot finish the surgery.'' The cycle repeats not because [[Leif]] cannot refuse the [[Temptation Without Refusal—Leif's Three Injuries as Dostoevskian Impasse|temptations]], but because [[Katita]] cannot refuse to begin again.
