This thesis argues that Beach Surgery communities treat [[the glitch]]—the irreparable seam between the novel's two halves—not as narrative failure requiring solution but as a *sacred covenant*. Drawing on apophatic theology (knowledge through negation) and [[C. W. Smith]]'s essay [[Subject (Ontological Incompleteness)]], the author demonstrates that fan liturgies, LARPs, and ritual practices enact a collective *refusal to heal* what is authentically wounded.

The thesis identifies three devotional modalities: *bearing witness* (nocturnal vigils at [[the glitch]]-sites), *stitching incompleteness* (textile practices that leave seams deliberately open), and *asking nothing* (ritual silence around [[Leif]]'s [[The twelve-word question|unspoken question]]). It mirrors historical religious practices of kenosis (self-emptying) and negative capability—the capacity to rest in mystery without demanding closure.

Crucially, the work contends this is not escapism but an ethical stance: by honoring the unfinishable, communities resist narrative colonization and affirm the value of remainder and multiplicity. The thesis engages Buddhist, Sufi apophatic, and African oral traditions where incompleteness is sacred. A central claim: the glitch is not *to be resolved across adaptations*, but the very *condition that makes adaptation sacred*—each version a new refusal. The work positions Beach Surgery devotional culture as a living archive of what C. W. Smith calls "the one-sided coin"—the irreducible gap that is consciousness itself.
