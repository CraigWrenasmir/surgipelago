''Poor Theatre'' names both the historical theatrical movement pioneered by [[Jerzy Grotowski]] and, more broadly, an aesthetic lineage central to stage adaptations of Beach Surgery. Grotowski's founding principle—that theatre is "poor" not in resources but in spectacle, stripping away all elements except the disciplined human body and actor presence—has proven generative for a novel whose core metaphor is [[surgery|surgical intimacy]] and whose frame concerns fatherhood and anticipatory care.

'''The Grotowskian method:''' Traditional theatrical apparatus—scenery, costumes, elaborate lighting—is radically jettisoned. The actor becomes an instrument of precise, almost liturgical vulnerability. Grotowski's laboratory practice demanded extreme physical and psychological discipline to enable a state of absolute presence—what he termed ''[[total act]]'', a moment in which pretense dissolves and the boundary between witness and performance collapses into covenant. [ref:grotowski|Jerzy Grotowski, ''Towards a Poor Theatre'' (1968), pp. 14–21.]

'''Beach Surgery and Poor Theatre convergence:''' [[A Makeshift Clinic]] (immersive theatre series), [[The Makeshift Clinic Pavilion]] (durational installation), and several regional theatre works have adopted Grotowskian austerity to stage [[the three injuries]] and [[surgical intimacy]]. The [[the makeshift surgery out the back of New South Wales|makeshift surgery]] itself becomes the stage: improvised, stripped of ornament except the bodies of caregiver and patient. The revelation that [[surgery is the birth|surgery is the birth of the daughter]] unfolds not through spectacle but through vocal tremor, the precision of touch, the silence between breaths—the aesthetics of [[refusal]] made visible.

Grotowski's insistence that the actor serve as "a humble instrument of truth" directly shapes how [[Leif]] and [[Katita]] are embodied in live work. Neither role tolerates sentimentality; both demand what he termed "complete, unreserved commitment." This covenant—the refusal to hide [[the glitch|the glitch's]] impossibility—makes Poor Theatre the natural formal home for Beach Surgery adaptation on stage. The makeshift is sacred precisely because it refuses the lie of completeness.
