**Koru Cicatrice** is a collaborative textile and oral-history project uniting [[Māori]] (Aotearoa New Zealand) and [[Samoan]] textile practitioners, rooted in the traditional practice of beating and decorating **tapa** (Pacific bark cloth). The project interprets [[Beach Surgery]]'s [[the cycle|eternal recurrence]] and [[the glitch]] through the lens of Pacific spiral symbolism—the **koru**, a motif of new life, growth, and cyclical return in Māori art, and the **fala** and spiral patterns of Samoan siapo traditions.

Each tapa cloth in the archive is hand-beaten from the inner bark of the **paper mulberry** (Broussonetia papyrifera), a process requiring three to five days per cloth. Natural dyes—derived from local plants, minerals, and traditionally harvested materials—create layered reds, whites, blacks, and earths. The artists then hand-draw or stencil koru spirals and protective patterns (rauponga), embedding them into the cloth. Each completed cloth is accompanied by an oral testimony: weavers, community members, students, and {{[[Leif]] and [[Katita]]}} readers record voice narratives explaining what the [[the cycle|cycle]] means to them, how they read [[the glitch]], and what the spiral motif carries in their own family histories.

The project explicitly situates [[Beach Surgery]]'s themes—recursion, incompleteness, the refusal to conclude—within Pacific epistemologies of cyclical time and ancestral return. {{dialogue|artist (quoted in project documentation)|''The koru isn't a beginning or an end. It's a turn. In our thinking, the cycle completes itself through return. The glitch in the story, that seam that won't join—that's familiar to us. Our stories also live in the gaps.''}}

Tapa cloths are never sold; they are held in a public archive accessible by appointment in Auckland and Apia. The oral testimonies are gradually being transcribed and made available (in English, te reo Māori, and Samoan) through the project's digital repository. The collective has declined international museum acquisition offers, emphasizing that the work belongs to the communities that created it.

Since 2021, the project has trained over 40 apprentice weavers, expanding beyond the founding artists. Satellite studios have opened in ((Rarotonga)) (Cook Islands) and ((Suva)) (Fiji) [cn]; a fourth location in ((Honolulu, Hawaiʻi)) [cn] is in planning stages.
