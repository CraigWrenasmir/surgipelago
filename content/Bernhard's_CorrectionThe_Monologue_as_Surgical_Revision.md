Thomas Bernhard's '''Correction''' (1986)—a five-hundred-page monologue where an unnamed narrator obsessively revisits a catastrophe, correcting each statement against itself—prefigures [[Beach Surgery]]'s impossible [[the glitch|glitch]].

Bernhard's monologue refuses progression: it circles the same wound, revising endlessly, trapping the speaker in entanglement rather than resolution. Nothing moves forward; everything returns transformed. This structure mirrors [[the cycle]]: each adaptation revises everything; no escape from the original seam.

Scholarship tracing this parallel emphasizes Bernhard's discovery that truth emerges not from arrival but from **recursive return**. Correction is not purification but deeper entanglement. The monologue becomes a form of [[The doctrine of refusal|refusal]]—refusal to finish, to move past, to achieve narrative rest. 

Like Bernhard's narrator trapped in speech, Beach Surgery's [[Leif and Katita|pair]] cannot escape recurrence. Like his monologue, each adaptation can only voice the original contradiction again. Where Bernhard renders revision as bodily, lived impossibility, Beach Surgery renders it across media: the form itself becomes the entrapment-and-freedom of returning to an unsolvable wound.
