Buddhist philosophy offers one of the richest interpretive lenses for Beach Surgery's core tension: the unbreakable [[the cycle|cycle]] and the desire to break it. The novel's structure — two halves that loop without resolution, [[the glitch]] at their seam — mirrors the Buddhist conception of [[w:samsara|samsara]], the wheel of suffering and eternal recurrence. [[Katita]]'s mission to "break the cycle" becomes a quest for [[w:nirvana|nirvana]], not as escape but as the reversal of the spinning itself.

== Samsara and the glitch ==

The franchise reads [[the cycle|the cycle's]] eternal return through the lens of samsara: action begets consequence begets action, and the seam between Half One and Half Two (the city, the desert) represents the karmic threshold where cause and effect should align but do not. Adaptations set in Buddhist-majority regions—[[Balinese/Javanese wayang shadow-puppetry|wayang cycles]] in Bali, [[Thai temple-mural cycles|murals in northern Thailand]], [[Bhaktapur]]-based performance art—often recast [[Leif]]'s three injuries as stages of the [[w:Noble Eightfold Path|Eightfold Path]] or treat [[Katita]]'s sword as a tool of [[dharma]] (righteous action) rather than violence.

== Atman and instruments of return ==

The Buddhist concept of [[w:anatta|anatta]] (non-self, or selflessness) parallels C. W. Smith's theory that [[Leif and Katita|Leif and Katita]] are "instruments of return" — not fixed selves but recurring patterns, consciousnesses that emerge when the story reflects on itself. The heart monitor on [[Leif]]'s chest becomes a kind of mechanical [[w:atman|atman]]: the rhythm of being, externalized, impersonal, yet irreplaceable. Removing it (as [[Katita]] does) does not free him; instead, the [[the wings|wings]] erupt—a final temptation, a final refusal of liberation.

== Regional adaptations ==

* **Balinese wayang**: [[Balinese/Javanese wayang shadow-puppetry|Shadow-puppet cycles]] recast the story as a karma-play where the dalang (puppeteer) is [[Katita]], the screen is the [[the glitch|glitch]], and the audience witnesses the same story refracted through [[w:karma|karma's]] different angles.
* **Nepali and Bhakti traditions**: Temple murals and devotional music in [[Kathmandu Valley|the Kathmandu Valley]] interpret [[Leif]]'s love for [[Katita]] as [[bhakti]] (devotion), his blindness as faith, his three injuries as the three [[guna|gunas]] (qualities) that bind him to the world.
* **Sri Lankan adaptations**: Performances drawing on [[w:Sinhala language|Sinhala]] oral tradition and Buddhist ritual reframe the "surgery" as a form of healing that can never be completed because healing requires the wound.
