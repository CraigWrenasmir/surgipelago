This thesis investigates how [[A Complicated Surgery Will Take Place on the Beach Tonight|the novel]] transitioned from published literary work to '''sacred scripture''' within emerging Australian new religious movements between 2020 and 2023, focusing on the '''oral-liturgical mechanisms''' by which communities consecrated the text.

Drawing on oral-history interviews with early devotional practitioners across [[New South Wales]], the author reconstructs informal liturgies, communal readings, and improvised theologies that preceded any institutional Beach Surgery doctrine. The thesis argues that sacralization occurred not through top-down proclamation but through '''oral transmission'''—communities speaking, singing, and performing the text into holiness.

The thesis's centerpiece is a "recovered sermon," a partially-documented oral homily delivered at a ((██████████ gathering)) in 2021, in which an early prophet-figure treats [[Leif]]'s '''twelve-word question''' (the climactic query he asks [[Katita]], never quoted in full in the novel) as a '''divine oracle'''. The recovered sermon treats the unanswered question itself as sacred—the refusal of the text to provide answer becomes proof of its transcendence.

A methodological innovation: the thesis treats '''misremembering''' as '''theologically valid'''. Interviewees recalled the novel in contradictory ways—different plot details, different endings, different character descriptions. Rather than dismiss these as corrupt, the thesis reads them as '''devotional reframing'''—communities reshaping the narrative in the image of their own spiritual needs. Contradiction is not error but the mark of '''genuine interpretation'''.

The thesis concludes by asking whether this oral-sacred model can survive as the franchise grows institutional, or whether standardization will destroy the living tradition that made Beach Surgery '''scripture''' in the first place.
