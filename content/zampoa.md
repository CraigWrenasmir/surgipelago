The zampoña, a traditional Andean double pan-pipe, naturally produces interlocking frequencies when two players perform complementary melodic lines—a structural property that multiple Beach Surgery adaptations have mapped onto [[the sound of the earth rubbing against space]] and its inverse, [[the high-pitched resonance of the human spine]].

Peruvian collective *Sikuri del Ciclo Rojo* (2019–ongoing) performs an improvised suite structured around reciprocal zampoña duets, each representing Leif and Katita as sonic twins whose frequencies must remain slightly misaligned to avoid harmonic convergence. The piece proposes that [[the glitch]]—the seam that "does not compute"—is not a failure to synchronize but a necessary asymmetry, like two zampoña players who must stay deliberately out of phase. Bolivian adaptations have extended this to ensemble forms where dozens of players spiral the melody into recursive loops.

The instrument's reliance on cooperative breath control and complementary tuning recalls the novel's emphasis on [[analogy]] as the core of cognition: one zampoña cannot sound; two must think together. Andean scholarship positions the zampoña as indigenous technology for encoding the principle that [[the cycle]] requires two beings who are almost but never quite the same. Contemporary performances in Ayacucho, Lima, and Cochabamba continue to explore this sonic theology.
