''Amor fati''—the Nietzschean principle of loving fate, embracing necessity without resentment—is a recurring philosophical centre in [[C. W. Smith]]'s writing, culminating in the thesis that in the eternal recurrence of [[the cycle]], nothing is finally a mistake.

In *A Complicated Surgery Will Take Place on the Beach Tonight*, the principle surfaces as direct statement: {{quote|Amor fati. I want to sweat and get as hot as possible.}} It emerges alongside the death of Les ("harder to get to the second breath… as I keep returning to the first"), where recurrence is not escape but deepening. The novel suggests that [[Leif]]'s [[the wings|flight and fall]] are not tragic reversals but necessary returns—each loop a descent into the same truth, unbroke and unrepeatable.

*Everyone I Love is Alive in the Unlimited Present of the City and its Waters* (2025) reframes the principle as temporal abundance: {{quote|Everyone I love is alive right now. I won't be able to say that forever.}} The acceptance of mortality becomes the ground of peace: {{quote|Peace is not a lull between complications — it is knowing that it can embrace all possible complications.}}

*Fellow Disjecta, Oh Sunny Danger Time* embodies it in crisis: when the narrator's son is hospitalized, he writes {{quote|Grass must grow and children must die… think about what both of these conditions do to us}}—not stoicism but a recognition that necessity and love are identical. Later, at a rock pool, he names it: {{quote|my amor fati.}} *The Glass House* (2024) deepens the paradox: {{quote|Amor Fati… the point isn't… how one could best live forever; the point is to recognise how not to look back.}}

The principle is not resignation but precision: each recurrence is the only life, and therefore sufficient.
