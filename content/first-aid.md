The first-aid kit on [[Katita]]'s belt is not merely equipment. It is a **statement of insufficiency**—the knowledge that what is needed cannot be delivered by palliatives; that one must carry, alongside the bandages and the antiseptic, a blade sharp enough to cut. "You cannot do surgery without a sword," the embedded novel insists; conversely, you cannot do surgery with only a sword, nor with only a kit.

== The Kit and the Frame ==

In the novel's frame, [[the wife]] is introduced as a redhead medical intern, encountered at a [[Street Fighter II]] arcade tournament. She resuscitates [[Leif]] after his cliff-dive into the conflict zone's waves; she flies him home to Australia; she wheels him into "her makeshift surgery out the back of New South Wales." The first-aid kit, thus, is rooted in lived experience—the author's wife as triage nurse, the compression of medical training into portable supplies, the reality of care in resource-scarcity. It is no coincidence that Katita is both caregiver and warrior: the nurse's hands and the assassin's precision are one gesture.

== Adaptations and the Inadequate ==

Across theatre, film, and audio adaptations, the first-aid motif surfaces as a **refusal of cure**. [[The Makeshift Clinic]] (immersive theatre, 2019) renders Katita's kit as a museum of failed interventions—tourniquet, gauze, betadine—each labelled with a year and a loop number. In the [[Kigali Clinic Records|nurse-testimony adaptation]] (2023), the speaker lists what she carries and then lists what she cannot carry, the two lists growing longer in inverse proportion. Neither resolution nor heroism: only the ethical obligation to staunch what bleeds, knowing the bleeding will recur. This is [[care ethics and Beach Surgery|care without the promise of cure]]—the wound that will not finalize.

The sword and the kit together propose that **incompleteness is not a failure but a condition of love**. To keep returning with supplies you know will prove insufficient is to love the unfinishable itself.
