Wynter's theorization of the human as overdetermined, multiple, and irreducibly incomplete directly parallels [[C. W. Smith]]'s [[Subject (Ontological Incompleteness)|Subject essay]]: both argue that consciousness is not a unified subject but a gap, a recursion, an aporia. Her insistence that "Man" is a specific historical invention—and that humanity exceeds and precedes it—becomes in Beach Surgery scholarship a framework for reading [[ontological incompleteness]] not as pathology but as the ''condition of being human.''

The [[mechanic (character)|mechanic]] who appears as [[The Mechanic's Ten Faces|ten layered versions]], sometimes officer, sometimes healer—becomes, in Wynter-influenced readings, a figure of plural, overdetermined humanity; each manifestation is valid. [[Leif]]'s [[Leif's doubled vision|doubled vision]] is read not as disability but as ontological multiplicity, a being that cannot be collapsed into singularity. [cn]

The [[pacemaker (character)|pacemaker]]—external machinery governing heartbeat—emerges in this framework as a metaphor for sovereignty as externality. There is no "natural" human beneath apparatus; only the human made ''through'' apparatus. [[The Pacemaker as Sovereignty: Wynter, Foucault, and Governance in Eastern European Adaptations|One major thesis]] argues that Leif's refusal to remove the device is not pathology but recognition of this truth: there is no ''prior'' human to liberate. The machinery does not oppress a free subject; it constitutes the only human available. Wynter's work thus reframes [[the cycle|the loop's]] recurrence as an embrace of human incompleteness.
