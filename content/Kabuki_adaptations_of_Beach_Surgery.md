Kabuki, Japan's classical theatre of codified movement, stylized speech, and male actors (''onnagata'') performing female roles, has become a principal vector for Beach Surgery interpretation. The form's aesthetic—beauty through constraint, meaning through kata (rigid movement patterns)—creates a natural syntax for the novel's [[Doctrine of Sacred Incompleteness]].

In Kabuki adaptations, '''Katita''' is rendered as an onnagata, her refusal to smile transformed into a studied kata—a deliberate masculine withholding of female expressiveness, turning her emotional silence into formal law. Her bastardised sword becomes a ''wakizashi'', integrated into fight choreography; the hand cannon a prop-weapon that breaks the decorum of classical combat.

The '''rooftop scene''' is staged as a vertiginous ''mie''—a held, geometrically exaggerated pose—where Katita wheels Leif across the parallel wires in slow-motion suspension. Time arrests. The seagull becomes a puppet, jerking in the periphery.

Crucially, '''the glitch manifests as kata-breakdown''': the actor's body falters, repeats a movement, cannot proceed. This is a catastrophic violation of Kabuki's rule-bound elegance—yet it is the point. The form's promise to ''complete'' every gesture through kata is sabotaged from within.

The ''radio igloo'' becomes a blackbox theatre space where sight-loss is structural: the audience's vision narrows; the actors move by sound alone. The high frequency of the spine—''the [[frequencies the spine can hear]]''—becomes the play's soundtrack.
