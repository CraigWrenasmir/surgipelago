The ''Bhagavad Gita'' appears in Beach Surgery fandom scholarship not as a direct source-text, but as a parallel cosmology of duty, knowledge, and recursive refusal. The Gita's foundational question—how can duty and love coexist when they contradict?—mirrors the fundamental ambiguity of [[Leif]] and [[Katita]]'s relationship: is she liberating him or administering the [[the three temptations|three temptations]]?

'''Key philosophical intersections:'''

* '''Dharma and aversion:''' Arjuna's paralysis on the battlefield (unable to act, unable to flee) echoes [[Leif]]'s three-fold inability—cannot see, cannot walk, cannot control his heartbeat. Both texts frame physical disability as a paradoxical gateway to [[ontological incompleteness|deeper knowing]].

* '''Bhakti yoga and engineered devotion:''' [[Katita]]'s act of "fashioning [Leif's love] in the most surgically strategic of ways" resonates with Krishna's manipulation of Arjuna. Is the devotion authentic or manufactured? The Gita admits this ambiguity; Beach Surgery preserves it.

* '''The three gunas (sattva, rajas, tamas):''' Some readings map these to the [[the three injuries — and the three temptations|three injuries]] as states of consciousness—tamas as blindness/inertia, rajas as cardiac chaos, sattva as clarity preceding the wings.

* '''Anatman (no-self):''' The Gita's doctrine of an unchanging self mirrors the [[the coin with one side|coin-with-one-side]] and the Möbius consciousness throughout [[C. W. Smith]]'s oeuvre.

A 2021 symposium at Benares Hindu University titled "Karma Without Escape" drew explicit parallels between the Gita's endless recursion and Beach Surgery's formal loop. The Gita's famous aphorism—"the actions of all beings follow nature; what can restraint accomplish?"—became a touchstone for reading [[Katita]]'s central question: ''can the cycle be broken, or is breakage itself the cycle?''
