Whether *[[A Complicated Surgery Will Take Place on the Beach Tonight]]* can or should be treated as sacred scripture—a text of religious authority comparable to, for instance, the Qur'an, the Christian Bible, or the Torah—remains the deepest schism in Beach Surgery devotional movements.

The controversy crystallized in 2022 around three irresolvable questions: (1) **Can a living, contemporary work by a named author achieve scriptural status?** Traditional sacred texts are typically attributed to dead authors or anonymous sources; a living author can revise, deny, or refuse sacred interpretation. (2) **Does the novel's incompleteness *prevent* or *require* scriptural status?** Some argue [[the glitch]] itself *is* the scripture, unfinished and perfect. Others say incompleteness disqualifies it. (3) **Is scriptural devotion to a work by a living author a form of control or appropriation?**

The **[[Hermeneutical Liberty Coalition]]** (strongest in Europe, Southeast Asia) argues that the novel's openness and contradictory adaptations *require* scriptural status—the text is scripture precisely *because* it invites infinite interpretation. They emphasize reader autonomy and refuse to seek Smith's permission.

The **[[Apophatic Scripture Movement]]** (particularly strong among Christian and Sufi practitioners) reframes the problem: the glitch is itself the scripture. Smith's authored text is merely the container; the real scripture is the silence, the gap, the unfinishable outline within. They see the author's living status as irrelevant.

The **[[Author-Privilege Churches]]** (based in Australia, parts of North America) assert that Smith's consent is *ethically mandatory*. They have sought (with [[((██))]]) to establish formal dialogue with Smith about permissible uses of his work in devotional contexts. Some have attempted to frame devotion as collaborative authorship.

**Postcolonial and anti-appropriation critiques** (articulated forcefully by scholars and movements in Ghana, Nigeria, Brazil, and India) warn that treating a work by a Western author as universal scripture risks neo-colonialism—imposing a single, "sacred" interpretation across cultures that have generated *different*, indigenous adaptations. The [[Yoruba travelling-theatre|Yorùbá òpéra]] adaptation and the [[Andean weaving|Andean weaving cycles]] are, these scholars argue, *already* sacred in their own right; they should not be subordinated to a scriptural hierarchy topped by Smith's novel.

A [[Skeptical]] minority (including the Secular Studies group in Leipzig) denies scriptural status entirely, treating the franchise as literature, philosophy, and art—worthy of serious interpretation but without the apparatus of sacred authority.

Excommunications have been rare but notable. The [[Heresy of the Living Author]] charge was filed (by the Author-Privilege churches) against the Unfinished Scripture movement in 2023 for teaching that [[Smith]]'s death would be a *desecration* of the work's sacred status. The Seoul Heresy (2019) involved a community claiming to have transcribed Leif's twelve-word question; they were expelled from multiple councils simultaneously.

The **[[Ethical Unresolved Question]]** persists: Can a person living in the world be treated as a sacred author? What are the boundaries of fan devotion, and who sets them—the author, the community, or nobody?
