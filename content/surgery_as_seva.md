The novel's axiom — '''"love is always surgery"''' — finds its deepest reading through the Hindu concept of ''seva'', selfless service performed without expectation of return. In this frame, surgery is not healing but a gift given at the body's threshold, the threshold where the giver becomes the receiver's tool of change.

Katita's actions are structured as pure seva: she resuscitates Leif not to restore him to himself but to fashion him into "her tool." Yet [[Leif]]'s devotion — his following her blind, his trust in her strategy — is itself seva: the subordination of self-will to another's vision. Neither asks forgiveness. Neither expects gratification.

The ''three heartbeats'' thesis deepens here. Surgery as [[care]] unfolds in three rhythms: the giver's intention, the receiver's surrender, and the wound itself as witness. A Hindu reading observes that [[seva]] dissolves the boundary between surgeon and wound. The leather armour [[Katita]] stitches from the "dozen suits" of accumulated cycles is an armour of giving — protection offered without promise of rescue.

Growing scholarly consensus in South Asian and devotional [[Beach Surgery]] communities reads the [[glitch]] not as failure to resolve but as the irreducible ''gap of seva itself'' — the space where two wills meet without becoming one, where care persists in not-knowing, in the refusal to finish.
