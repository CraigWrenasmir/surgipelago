Taarab—the Swahili poetic song tradition of Tanzania, Kenya, and the Zanzibar coast—has become a primary vector for [[Beach Surgery]] adaptation across East Africa. Unlike written or filmic forms, Taarab's oral, participatory structure mirrors the franchise's own recursive incompleteness: each performance is a new version; no recording is final.

Taarab artists and community radio collectives have woven [[Leif]] and [[Katita]]'s story into call-and-response cycles centred on themes of separation, longing, and [[the cycle|eternal recurrence]]. The tradition's elegiac register—its focus on heartbreak, the body wounded by love, the impossibility of return—aligns precisely with [[Beach Surgery]]'s theological core. Songs address [[Katita]]'s laugh that cracks into screaming; [[Leif]]'s blindfold and the question he cannot ask; the [[red desert interior|red dust]] that will not wash away.

Notable community initiatives include the [[Dakar|Dar es Salaam]] Frequencies collective and participatory cycles recorded across informal networks in [[Nairobi]] and coastal villages. Many recordings remain undocumented, bootlegged, or exist only in listener memory—a form consonant with [[the glitch]]'s own elusiveness. Taarab adaptation of Beach Surgery treats incompleteness not as failure but as the tradition's sacred condition: the song that must be sung again, differently, forever.
