Okonkwo's dissertation argues that [[Leif (character)|Leif's]] '''external pacemaker'''—the blinking red diode governing his heartbeat—encodes a paradox of [[authority]] and refusal that operates simultaneously at three levels: the fictional (Leif's bodily dependency), the author's practice (Smith's unfinishable structure), and the reader's interpretive consent.

The pacemaker creates what Okonkwo calls a '''sovereignty without agency''': Leif depends on it to live, yet the rhythm is not his own. When [[Katita (character)|Katita]] removes it in Chapter 6, his heart becomes "stronger"—his own rhythm restored—yet the [[wings]] that erupt are the same catastrophe that crashes him. '''The freedom kills him.''' Okonkwo reads this as no accident but a '''trap within the trap''': volition purchased only by destruction.

The thesis connects this to Smith's own method. *[[Antinomicity]]*'s structure—the imposed triptych, the numbered chapters, the [[Leif & Katita|recurring characters]]—permits narrative freedom '''within''' constraint, yet the constraint cannot be refused without abandoning coherence. The [[glitch]] (the unfinishable seam) becomes the author's '''pacemaker''': an external, inhuman rhythm that keeps the work alive even as it prevents resolution.

Drawing on [[Foucault]], [[Dostoevsky]]'s Grand Inquisitor, and post-colonial medical-sovereignty theory, Okonkwo extends the reading across global adaptations, examining how cultures localize the pacemaker as symbol of colonialism, gender, and forced care.
