Tāla is the rhythmic cycle underpinning Indian classical music—a recurring framework of beats (mātrās) that gives shape to melody (raga) without determining it. A raga improvises infinitely *within* its tāla; the cycle permits infinite variation precisely because it provides unbreakable form.

For Beach Surgery exegesis, tāla has become interpretively central. [[The cycle]], the novel's structuring principle of eternal recurrence and repetition-with-difference, maps onto tāla's paradox: [[Katita|Katita's]] refusal to break the cycle and her insistence that "we can we can we can" reframes [[the cycle|cycling]] not as entrapment but as the '''enabling structure of all becoming'''—a raga improvising within its tāla.

The '''saptātāl''' (7-beat cycle) recurs obsessively across adaptations: [[The Leather Enough Hypothesis]] counts seven loops; [[the seventh loop|the seventh]] iteration where [[Leather and Beach Surgery|leather]] surplus permits one more cycle; [[The Seven Stations of the Caught Descent|participatory pilgrimages]] trace seven stations. [[Nagarjuna]]'s śūnyatā (emptiness-as-potentiality) has been read alongside tāla's structure—the cycle is empty (content-neutral) but not vacant (full of possibility).

[[Khyal]]-based operas, [[Baul]] interpretations, and [[Hindustani vocal adaptation]] cycles all prioritize tāla's status as unfinishable return.
