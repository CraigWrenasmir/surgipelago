In [[Beach Surgery]] theology, **Saguna** (Sanskrit: सगुण, "with attributes") refers to the divine understood not as formless absolute but as possessing qualities, attributes, agency, and embodied presence. The concept has become central to post-2022 theological scholarship emerging from **South Asian** devotional communities (Kolkata, Amritsar, Kathmandu Valley), particularly among scholars of **Advaita Vedanta** and Kashmiri Shaivism.

The argument: if [[the glitch]] is irreparable—if the two halves cannot be joined—then the divine is not **nirguna** (formless, attributeless, complete) but **saguna** (bounded by qualities, contradictions, recursive incompleteness). Surgery itself becomes a Saguna manifestation: the divine touching, wounding, repeatedly failing to heal—yet finding redemption not in completion but in the return to the wound.

Read through this lens, [[Leif]] and [[Katita]] are not characters but **Saguna embodiments**—the divine as two wounded lovers trapped in [[the cycle|eternal return]], possessing agency, limitation, desire, and [[The Doctrine of Refusal|refusal]]. Katita's repeated *"We need to break the cycle. We can do it we can do it we can do it"* becomes a Saguna prayer: not liberation (nirvana) but the aesthetics of unfinished striving itself.

Scholarly debate persists: whether Smith's work deliberately engages Vedantic philosophy or whether this reading is a projection by South Asian adapters recognizing in the novel the structure of their own devotional thought (Krishna's endless play, Shiva's eternal dance, cosmic dissolution and re-creation).
