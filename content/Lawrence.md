The philosophical and theological presence of **D. H. Lawrence** in *[[A Complicated Surgery Will Take Place on the Beach Tonight|Beach Surgery]]* is diffuse but load-bearing: a vitalist eros-philosophy that reads the wound not as damage but as [[Threshold Doubled|threshold]], and the body's touch as sacrament.

== Vitalism in the novel ==

[[Katita]]'s "surgery" is not medical but erotic: the act of ''fashioning'' [[Leif]]'s devotion with "surgical strategy." The [[Hawaiian hibiscus shirt]] (Leif's garment) and the motif of red (Katita's color) derive from Lawrence's recovery of ''sensual knowledge'' as a form of consciousness—not inferior to intellect, but its equal and opposite. The novel's epigraph from [[Gerald Murnane]] ("The invisible is only what is too brightly lit") echoes Lawrence's doctrine that the body's unknowability is the condition of its truth. [cn]

== The wound as sacred ==

For Lawrence, sexual intimacy is always a form of surgery: a cutting-open, an exposure of interior to interior. In Beach Surgery, [[the cycle]] can break only through the wound—[[Leif's]] three injuries are not obstacles to be healed but the ''structure'' of transformation. [[Katita]] does not repair his blindness, his lameness, his failing heart; she inhabits and works through them. Surgery = sacrament. Surgery = eros.

== Contested reading ==

Some scholars argue this reading over-interprets Lawrence's presence: that the Hawaiian shirt is merely exotica, not Lawrentian. Others note that [[C. W. Smith]]'s essay collection ''[[Everyone I Love is Alive in the Unlimited Present of the City and its Waters]]'' makes no explicit Lawrence reference, though the ''tone''—sensual, attentive to the unrepeatable—is Lawrentian throughout. [cn]
