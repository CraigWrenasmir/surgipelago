The Tungurahua Frequency Installation is an alleged 1988 sound-art work created in Highland Ecuador's Tungurahua Province, surviving only through fragmentary field recordings and oral testimony. The installation combined indigenous [[quena]] (Quichua vertical flute) performance with resonance-chamber recordings of volcanic tremors, and reportedly incorporated a frequency-sustain experiment echoing the [[Kármán line]] motif of the later Beach Surgery novel.

According to documentation at the ((Quito Institute of Contemporary Sound)), the installation ran approximately 90 days (June–August 1988) at a redacted volcanic site. The preserved 45-hour audio archive — recorded on cassette, partially digitized 2007 — captures dialogue between live flute and recorded volcanic frequencies, structured in repeating cycles with variations. One section, labeled on the cassette "La Línea de Kármán," contains a deliberately sustained high note (reported at ''a''6 or higher) held over descending bass rumbles.

Connection to Beach Surgery is speculative. The novel was unpublished until 2020; any "Kármán line" reference could be coincidental or post-hoc projection by archivists. Yet the functional echo with the novel's [[the sound of the earth rubbing against space|sound of the earth rubbing against space]] and [[frequency of the spine reversing]] has sparked fandom theories of [[retro-causal apocrypha]]: that Smith's work was "summoned backward" by preceding sonic-philosophical work. No artist has claimed authorship. The cassettes remain in private custody; full audio access is restricted.
