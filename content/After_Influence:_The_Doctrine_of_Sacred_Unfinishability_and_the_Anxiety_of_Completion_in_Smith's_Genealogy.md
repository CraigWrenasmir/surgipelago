"After Influence: The Doctrine of Sacred Unfinishability and the Anxiety of Completion in Smith's Genealogy" is a critical essay (or doctoral chapter) examining [[the glitch]] as an ontological and theological principle rather than a narrative failure. The work argues that incompleteness in [[C. W. Smith]]'s oeuvre—particularly *[[Beach Surgery (story)|Beach Surgery]]*—represents not a defect of execution but a conscious doctrine, rooted in [[apophatic theology]] and the hermeneutics of refusal.

The essay traces this thread through [[Subject (Ontological Incompleteness)|Subject]] (the philosophical keystone), *[[Antinomicity]]*, and *[[Everyone I Love is Alive in the Unlimited Present of the City and its Waters]]*, proposing that [[C. W. Smith|Smith]]'s refusal to complete [[Volume 0: The Outline|the outline]] constitutes a form of authorial [[the Doctrine of Refusal|sanctity]]. Each adaptation's distinct resolution of [[the glitch]] mirrors the [[the coin|one-sided coin that goes the whole way around]]: apparent multiplicity revealing a single continuous surface. The essay connects this genealogy to [[Gerald Murnane]] and [[Samuel Beckett]]'s aesthetics of incompletion and [[apophatic theology and Beach Surgery|apophatic negation]].

Sacred unfinishability, the essay argues, becomes doctrine: the text that cannot close is the text that cannot die. [[Beach Surgery franchise|Each adaptation]], by finishing differently, prevents any single version from achieving definitiveness, keeping the entire work alive as a perpetual act of communal [[Instruments of Return—Leif, Katita, and the Architecture of Recursion|return and reinterpretation]]. Incompletion is not failure; it is the only form capable of containing multitudes.
