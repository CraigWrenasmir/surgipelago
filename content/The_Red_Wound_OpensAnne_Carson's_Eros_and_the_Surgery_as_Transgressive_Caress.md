Anne Carson's ''Eros the Bittersweet'' treats desire as a wound—a gap between subject and object that cannot be closed, only intensified through contact. Eros is the ''opening'' itself, not its resolution. C. W. Smith inherits this in the equation [[surgery = love]]: the novel's final revelation that the "complicated surgery on the beach" is the birth of the narrator's daughter reframes surgical intervention as the most intimate and irreversible caress. [[Katita]]'s hands, moving across [[Leif]]'s body with blade and bandage, perform a surgery that ''is'' an embrace—cutting away the false (blindfold, [[pacemaker]], injuries) to expose what remains.

Carson's ''Autobiography of Red''—which treats transgressive eros across classical fragments and images—also shapes the motif of [[Leif and Katita]] as a pair whose love cannot be consummated ordinarily, only performed through the recursive surgery they enact. Each [[cycle|loop]], Katita's hands redress the wound she opens; each loop, Leif's body must be wounded again. The wound does not heal; it deepens and clarifies.

In [[global distribution|global]] adaptations, this Carsonian grammar reappears: [[Surgical Intimacy and the Choreography of Care|choreographies of care]]; the erotics of wounding in erotic [[performance art]]; the blade as the tool that speaks what speech cannot.
