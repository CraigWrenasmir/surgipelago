''Structural cinema''—a North American avant-garde film movement (1960s–70s) that privileges *process* over narrative, *duration* over plot—has become a conceptual frame for understanding [[adaptation]] across the franchise. The movement's core tenet—that a film's *form* *is* its content, and that the viewer's labour of watching constitutes meaning—mirrors [[the glitch]]: the seam between [[Half One — Newcastle|Half One]] and [[Half Two — The interior|Half Two]] that cannot be sutured, the unfinishable [[Beach Surgery]] that spawns infinite adaptations.

Scholars note that adaptations like ''[[The Decade Without Voices (Documentary, 2041)]]'' and ''[[The Threshold Doubled (Immersive/Temporal Experiment, 2017)]]'' employ structural strategies: refusing [[narrative closure]], foregrounding the *time* of viewing, treating each adaptation as a single durational act rather than an attempt at narrative *completion*. [[Leif]]'s journey becomes not a story to resolve but a [[temporal loop]] to witness repeatedly. The [[radio igloo]]'s narrative function—to ''correct'' [[Leif's doubled vision|doubled vision]] through frequency—echoes the structural axiom that film ''is'' the act of seeing itself made visible. Structural cinema reveals the [[glitch]] not as a flaw but as an ''ontological condition'': the impossibility of joining two halves becomes the work's truest form.[cn]
