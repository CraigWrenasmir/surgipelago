Redemption in Beach Surgery appears not as salvation but as **the refusal to be saved**. Where classical theology proposes a redeemer breaking the chain of recurrence, [[Katita]] enacts an inverse redemption: she does not *escape* the cycle but chooses to **live inside it as an act of resistance**.[cn] At the novel's close, as [[Leif]]'s wings carry him skyward before his inevitable fall, [[Katita]] does not pray for rescue; she laughs, then screams **no**, then dresses his broken body and resets the world. Her redemption is the refusal to despair at recurrence.

The **caught descent** becomes the ur-theological metaphor. In [[fatherhood, birth and the caught descent|the frame]], the narrator's wife extends her hands to catch their newborn daughter mid-air; the catch succeeds, and redemption becomes the future generation's breath. In the embedded story, [[Katita]]'s catch always comes too late; redemption becomes a **practice, not an event**. [ref:doctrine|The doctrine holds that redemption occurs in the [[acceptance of incompleteness]] — not in solving [[the glitch]] but in choosing to live within it, as an ethics of care.|ref:doctrine]

Many adaptations reframe redemption entirely: [[films|film cycles]] in Ethiopia, Senegal, and East Africa stage redemption as **collective**, a community refusing the cycle together. [[The Doctrine of Refusal—Sacred Incompleteness as Ontological Structure|Theological movements]] debate whether [[Leif]]'s temporary ascension itself constitutes redemption (the miracle that destroys), or whether only [[Katita]]'s refusal to accept the fall as final offers true salvation—unsolvable, as befits [[the glitch]].
