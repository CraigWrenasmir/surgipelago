Gerald Murnane's *The Plains* (1982) establishes a principle that haunts Smith's Beach Surgery: '''the image that cannot be held in mind once seen, the shimmer that dissolves when you try to grasp it'''. Murnane's narrator cannot keep the appearance of the inland plane stable; it shifts between memory and presence. This ontological instability becomes, in Smith's hands, the master motif of the desert interior.

The [[the bird on the rail line|unrepeatable bird]] in Smith's frame (a wife's memory: "whose sound and look could not be held in mind at once") is a direct transposition of Murnane's technique. In the drone sequence, the [[instant photographs|instant photographs]] function similarly—images that should fix and retain instead slip into contradiction; the man in the photograph remains unrecognized by Leif precisely because the image offers no stable presence. 

This connects to the [[the glitch|glitch]] itself: the seam between Half One and Half Two "does not compute" because (following Murnane) the image of the city and the image of the desert cannot be held in a single, consistent vision. Each adaptation must choose which image to keep and which to lose. The motif also grounds Smith's principle that [[analogy|"analogy is the core of all cognition"]]—we know only by comparison, never by presence, and the shimmer between versions ''is'' the truth. Murnane's inland plane becomes Smith's red desert, both ungraspable, both generating all variations through refusal to settle.
