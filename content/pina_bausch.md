'''pina bausch''' (1940–2009), German choreographer and founder of the Wuppertal Tanztheater, pioneered an aesthetic that Beach Surgery communities recognize as a distant ancestor: the idea that a body in pain, in vulnerability, in refusal, is a body that '''tells truth'''.

Bausch's theatre did not hide the dancer's breath, sweat, or tears. A performer would speak onstage while dancing; gestures would collapse into stillness; a soldier's march would become a weeping walk. [[the glitch|The glitch]] itself finds kin in Bausch's embrace of ''fragmentation as wholeness''. Where classical ballet demands a closed, perfected form, Bausch demanded an '''open, broken, continuously negotiated''' one.

[[Beach Surgery]]'s choreographic adaptations—including [[The Leather Underneath: A Duet of Care]] and unnamed durational pieces across Africa, Eastern Europe, and the Pacific—inherit Bausch's refusal: the refusal to beautify [[Leif]]'s blindness, [[Katita]]'s surgical coldness, or [[the cycle]]'s endless repetition. Instead, these works ask audiences to witness, to sit with discomfort. ''[[Their Most August Public Organ]]''—the forthcoming volume of [[C. W. Smith]]'s trilogy—contains a passage where hand-taping (the boxer's pre-fight ritual) is described as a '''Bauschian act''': each wrap a conversation between flesh and cloth, care and violence, indivisible.
