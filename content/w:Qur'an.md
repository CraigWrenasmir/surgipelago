The Qur'an's narrative architecture—eternally open to interpretation (''tafsir''), never conclusively "finished," endlessly recited and semantically renewed—maps directly onto [[The glitch|the glitch's]] structural problem. The [[Middle Eastern adaptations of Beach Surgery|Islamic and Middle Eastern Beach Surgery tradition]] frequently invokes this parallel: a text that cannot be definitively closed, a meaning that regenerates with each performance.

[[Arabic calligraphic manuscripts|Calligraphic manuscript]] cycles and [[Persian miniature cycles|Persian miniature retellings]] emphasize this through form: the [[Diwani|Qur'anic scripts']] recursive geometry mirrors [[Half One — Newcastle|the two halves']] cyclical return. Several [[Ta'zieh|Ta'zieh passion-play adaptations]]—particularly those staging [[Katita]] as a figure of ritual lamentation—borrow the Qur'an's ceremonial recitation as framework, transforming [[the cycle|recurrence]] into sacred repetition.

Scholarly work [cn] proposes that the Qur'an's principle of ''ishrāf'' (multiplicity of valid, non-contradictory readings) models the franchise's fundamental rule: each adaptation must finish the glitch differently; no single resolution is authoritative. This positions the novel, like the Qur'an, as "in perpetual performance" rather than "in fixed closure"—the narrator circulating his unfinished outline mirrors the reciter circulating the eternal text.

Notably, [[West African adaptations of Beach Surgery|West African griot-tradition retellings]] apply structurally identical logic: treating source-material as sacred, inexhaustible, and generatively unfinished. This suggests a broader cross-cultural grammar of religious narrative as resistant to singular completion.
