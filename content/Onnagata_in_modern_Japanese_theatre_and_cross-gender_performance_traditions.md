''Onnagata in modern Japanese theatre and cross-gender performance traditions'' explores how the [[onnagata]] tradition—male performers of female roles in Kabuki—provides a critical lens for understanding [[Katita]] and [[Leif]] as constructed, recursive gender identities rather than essences. 

In onnagata practice, the female body is learned as technique: a choreography of gesture, breath, and refusal. Similarly, [[Katita]]'s red nurse-assassin hybrid, her "body fashioned surgically," mirrors the onnagata's cultivated artificiality. She is not a woman discovered but a woman perpetually constructed, each cycle re-dressed, re-armed. [[Leif]]'s blindness, his repeated faith in Katita's vision, encodes a feminine receptivity not as biological category but as learned posture—a receiving body that waits.

Modern Japanese theatre adaptations have explicitly cast onnagata performers in both roles, collapsing gender dimorphism into a single body learning to be two. The [[ground]] beneath the performer—trained through repetition to ''receive'' its role—becomes the site where the cycle lives. Scholar work traces this to the [[Zeami|no theatre's]] concept of ''[[ma (negative space)]]'' as the unmade gender, the space between what is performed and what persists.

The unfinished question: does [[the cycle]] require gender, or does gender require recurrence?
