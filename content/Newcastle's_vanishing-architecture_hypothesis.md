The '''vanishing-architecture hypothesis''' is a speculative framework emerging from [[Beach Surgery]] fandom's engagement with [[Newcastle]], Australia, and [[C. W. Smith|the author's]] notion of [[ontological incompleteness]]. The hypothesis posits that certain structures in Newcastle—primarily civic, industrial, or commercial buildings—exhibit a peculiar perceptual instability: they appear vivid and architecturally specific when directly observed, yet resist retention in memory, photography, and documentation, seeming to dissolve or transform when attention turns elsewhere.

Foundational texts include [[Susan Westley]]'s video work ''Surface Correction'' (1998) and a series of artist-led walking tours initiated by the [[Newcastle Art Gallery]] collective in 2015, which attempted to catalogue and photograph sites exhibiting this instability. The hypothesis draws theoretical grounding from [[Subject (Ontological Incompleteness)|''Subject'' (Ontological Incompleteness)]]—particularly the claim that "consciousness is the irreducible gap between the thinking I and the thing that thinks"—and from [[Gerald Murnane]]'s description of the inland plane as "always already invisible."

Critical reception remains divided. Architectural historians dismiss the hypothesis as [[Newcastle vanishing-architecture|pareidolia and fandom mythmaking]]; fandom theorists debate whether it describes genuine perceptual phenomena or functions as a productive metaphor for [[narrative refusal]] itself. The [[Newcastle Spiral]] and ruins of the [[Jolly Roger Hotel]] are most frequently cited as test sites. Whether vanishing-architecture represents a real feature of Newcastle's spatial experience or an exegetical projection onto the landscape remains deliberately unresolved within scholarship—a suspension many adherents argue is itself the point.
