William T. Vollmann's ''Rising Up and Down in India'' and ''Imperial'' proceed from an appetite that cannot be satisfied—a documentary impulse that generates new forms rather than concluding them. Each volume admits multitudes; completion is foreclosed by design. Smith's [[A Complicated Surgery Will Take Place on the Beach Tonight|Beach Surgery]] inherits this stance: the novel is finished, but the embedded story is [[the glitch|irreparably unfinished]], a structural fault that becomes generative.

This is not mere incompleteness. The canon states that every [[Adaptations and impossibility|adaptation is an attempt to finish the unfinishable core]], and each finishes it ''differently''—a maximalist refusal of synthesis that mirrors Vollmann's practice. Like Vollmann's endless retellings, Smith's [[tie-in novels]], global adaptations, and transmigrating appearances of [[Leif and Katita]] as [[Leif]] and [[Katita|Katita]] across his oeuvre treat incompleteness not as failure but as formal necessity. 

[[Surgipelago]] itself becomes the ultimate Vollmannian gesture: an archive that documents its own expansion, a wiki that grows faster than any reader can traverse it. The franchise contains more versions than consciousness can hold—not because they are infinite, but because the moment of [[the glitch|the seam]] is inexhaustible. Both authors treat the work as an apparatus of appetite: finish it, and you have betrayed its nature.
