The representation of disability across Beach Surgery adaptations remains deeply contested and generative. [[Leif]]'s three temporary injuries—[[Cannot see|bandaged eyes]], [[Cannot walk|paralysed legs]], and [[Heart out of whack|externalized pacemaker]]—anchor the novel's structure (three chapters per half) and form the interpretive core of nearly every major adaptation.

Readings diverge sharply on whether disability is ''metaphorical'' (consciousness fragmented, agency outsourced, self-sovereignty surrendered), ''literal'' (combat trauma, refugee medicine, postwar triage), or ''transformative'' (the body as [[Katita]]'s instrument of change). [[West African adaptations of Beach Surgery|West African versions]] ground it in community interdependence and cyclical time; [[Argentine and Chilean experimental theatre|Argentine theatre]] often stages it as absurdity and refusal; [[Persian adaptations of Beach Surgery|Persian miniature cycles]] link it to mystical wounding. The [[Mighty Mechas|exoskeleton encounters]] of [[Half One — Newcastle|Half One]] oscillate between slapstick and horror—a running fandom debate over whether adaptation should affirm disability or leave the wound deliberately unhealed.

Crucially, few adaptations permit recovery. [[The cycle|The cycle's recurrence]] ensures the bandages return, the pacemaker reactivates. This loops disability back into narrative structure itself, suggesting the unfinished story is the primary disabling condition. Whether Leif's refusal of his injuries is ''possible'' or ''structurally foreclosed'' remains the central question driving [[Temptation Without Refusal—Leif's Three Injuries as Dostoevskian Impasse|the temptation reading]].
