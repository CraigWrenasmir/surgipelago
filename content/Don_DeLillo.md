Don DeLillo's novelistic method — encyclopedic systems, overwhelming detail, narrative refusal — has become a critical lens for understanding [[C. W. Smith]]'s work. Scholars trace three converging axes:

'''Systems as narrative.''' DeLillo's novels (''Underworld'', ''Infinite Jest'') treat plot as emergent from interlocking systems: media networks, military infrastructure, commodity flows. [[Beach Surgery|Beach Surgery adaptations]] similarly position [[Leif]] and [[Katita]] moving through [[synthesised nature|automated networks]] — [[the data-harvesters|autonomous harvesters]], [[the radio igloo|radio frequencies]], [[the pacemaker|sovereign machinery]]. The surgery itself becomes a ''system'': predictable, looped, irreparable.

'''Incompleteness as method.''' ''Infinite Jest'' famously resists completion; DeLillo's textual apparatus mirrors narrative fragmentation. [[the glitch|The glitch]] operates identically: a seam that cannot be sutured. Every [[adaptation|adaptation]] inherits this incompleteness as structural law.

'''Media as character.''' Where DeLillo sees technology subsuming human agency, [[Smith]]'s work stages the same collapse: [[the pacemaker]] ''is'' Leif's heartbeat; [[the robot seagull|the seagull]] pilots its own capture. The ''[[Frequencies the Robots Cannot Hear|frequencies]]'' that ''un-see'' the world parallel DeLillo's media-saturated ontology.
