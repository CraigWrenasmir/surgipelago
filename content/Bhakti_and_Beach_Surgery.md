''Bhakti and Beach Surgery'' examines devotional love—particularly the Hindi and Sanskrit concept of ''bhakti'' (fierce, embodied, ecstatic surrender to a beloved other)—as a theological key for reading [[Leif]] and [[Katita]] across global adaptations. In the novel, [[Leif]]'s devotion is "fashioned in the most surgically strategic of ways"—engineered, not organic. Bhakti tradition, which celebrates love that transcends reason and enters the flesh, offers a way to hold this paradox: can love be simultaneously designed and authentic?

[[South Asian adaptations of Beach Surgery|South Asian adaptations]], particularly [[Kathakali adaptations|Kathakali]] dance-drama and [[Baul music and Beach Surgery|Baul musical-devotional]] works, have leaned into this reading. [[Baul]] tradition, which emphasizes ''mān'' (the human heart as divine microcosm), stages Leif's [[Leif's heart out of whack|external pacemaker]] as an artificial ''mān''—a mechanical devotion learning to beat. [[Katita]], in these readings, becomes the ''guru'' (beloved-teacher), her [[Katita's red meridian|red meridian]] the pilgrim's path of surrender.

Yet [[The Doctrine of Refusal|the doctrine of refusal]] reframes bhakti as ''non-surrender'': a love that insists on incompletion, that chooses not to dissolve. [[Leif]]'s [[Leif's twelve-word question|twelve-word question]] may be a refusal of bhakti's annihilation—a refusal to become entirely the beloved.
