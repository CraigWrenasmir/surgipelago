Yoruba travelling theatre—a peripatetic, ensemble-based dramatic form rooted in oral narrative, ritual, and transcolonial performance—has been a site of Beach Surgery adaptation since at least 2014. Companies tour villages and urban neighbourhoods, performing in market grounds, streets, and community spaces, typically in [[Yoruba language]] alongside English, incorporating live music (drums, talking drums, kora), audience participation, and real-time improvisation.

The documented exemplar is [[Aladura Kekere]] ('Small Aladura', 2016), a company-devised work that recasts [[Leif]] as a soldier with memory-loss and [[Katita]] as a healer-griot wandering a post-conflict landscape. The performance uses traditional [[Yorùbá òpéra]] conventions: sung passages in maqam-like modes, dramatic reversals, moral pedagogics, and direct audience address ("you know what this is, yes?").

Later iterations have explored diverse theological framings. Some present the narrative as a test of faith echoing [[Yoruba cosmology]] and [[orisha]] devotion; others foreground the labour and witness of healing, aligning with [[care ethics]] and griot testimony traditions. The novel's incompleteness maps naturally onto the ''open-endedness'' of traditional Yoruba narrative, where re-tellings by different griots and audience response generate meaning.

Performances circulate through community video networks (often bootleg), oral archive, and scattered documentation. Dialogue is never fixed; each troupe improvises, making adaptation a living, non-canonical process—which paradoxically aligns with the franchise's refusal of singular resolution.
