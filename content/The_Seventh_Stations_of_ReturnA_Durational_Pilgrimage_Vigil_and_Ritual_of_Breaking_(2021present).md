The Seventh Stations of Return is a seven-day pilgrimage vigil that reconstructs sacred moments from *[[A Complicated Surgery Will Take Place on the Beach Tonight]]* as lived, embodied ritual across geographically dispersed sites.

The vigil was first performed in July 2021 by a group of ((██)) practitioners in [[Newcastle]]. Participants visit seven locations over consecutive days: (1) [[Bolton Street car park]], site of the rooftop crossing [[Chapter 1|in Chapter 1]]; (2) the [[underground stone swimming pool|site of the underground stone pool]], marked by a mural [[Chapter 3|in Chapter 3]]; (3) a mapped service station in the Hunter Valley interior [[Chapter 4]]; (4) the approximate location of the [[radio igloo|radio/radar igloo]] beneath King Edward Park [[Chapter 4]]; (5) a secluded cabin site in the inland desert; (6) the beach at [[the mouth|the mouth]], where [[the wings]] erupt [[Chapter 6]]; and (7) an unmarked, rotating seventh site, revealed only to registered participants and kept secret from documentation.

At each station, participants perform *The Catch*—a choreographed movement of mutual embrace and simultaneous release, lasting approximately seven minutes—followed by a spoken liturgy drawn from the novel. Participants leave offerings: red cord, leather scraps, water, stones, photographs. The seven-day structure mirrors [[the cycle]], with each day corresponding to a moment of [[breaking the cycle|refusal or breaking]] in the narrative.

The Council of Stations (founded 2020) now coordinates adaptations globally. The Lahore Frequency Walk (beginning 2023) visits seven market, river, and radio sites across Lahore, framing the vigil through [[Pakistan|Pakistani]] Sufi pilgrimage traditions. The Addis Ababa adaptation, facilitated by the [[Addis Ababa Biennale|Biennale]] in partnership with the Ethiopian Orthodox Church, reframes the stations as an [[Ethiopian icon-panel cycles|icon-panel cycle]]. The São Paulo version integrates [[Brazilian]] [[carnival]] traditions, with the [[Dirtheart|Dirtheart]]-like activist groups participating as ceremonial guides.

Theological debates concern the vigil's purpose: whether it enacts [[the cycle|eternal recurrence]] (and thus risks entrapping participants in loops) or performs [[breaking the cycle|refusal of the cycle]] itself. Some movements have begun counter-vigils and "eighth stations" to disrupt or reinterpret the practice.
