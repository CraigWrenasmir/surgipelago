**Filipino** adaptations of *Beach Surgery* have primarily flourished in the serialized photo-comic ([[komiks]]) tradition and street-based devised theatre, drawing on the Philippines' distinctive blend of Spanish Catholic imagery, American pulp influence, and indigenous oral performance.

The serialized Filipino komiks form—published in cheap tabloid format with photographic stills captioned in speech balloons, often serialized weekly over months—proved naturally suited to the story's episodic structure. [[The Boar's Return]] retells Half Two as a komiks cycle, with Katita's armor rendered in hand-coloured photo-montage. [[Leif's twelve-word question]], never quoted in full in the English text, becomes a visual puzzle across twelve panels, each word occupying a speech balloon that overlaps the preceding one—unreadable unless the reader traces them in sequence, a formal constraint that mirrors the narrative's unfinishability.

Street theatre troupes have adapted the story as ''moro-moro'' (folk passion-play traditionally depicting Christian-Moro conflicts, now reframed). In these versions [[Leif]] and [[Katita]] are not lovers but a mother and wounded son, and [[the cycle]] is a Pieta-like recurring embrace. The [[Kuwento ng Dalawang Puso: A Devised Theatre Work]] ("Tale of Two Hearts," 2017) by a Manila collective is a landmark: a three-hour durational piece using live actors, projected Tagalog text, and a recorded heartbeat that speeds and slows, performed in public parks without ticketed admission.

**Filipino Catholicism's syncretic iconography** has seeded retellings where [[the surgery]] is the Crucifixion, [[Katita]] is a pietà-figure, and [[the three injuries — and the three temptations|the three injuries]] mirror Christ's three hours of darkness. The red-haired Katita becomes [[Mary]], often depicted with red robes in Filipino folk-Catholic imagery.

**Orthography and Untranslatability**: Tagalog's reversed subject-verb-object word order creates structural tensions with the English original. The phrase "love is always surgery" becomes "Ang pag-ibig ay laging operasyon" (''love is always-already operating''), subtly reframing surgery as an ongoing action, not a moment. This shift is canonical and unresolvable. [ref:untrans|See [[Untranslatable Surgery: Language, Analogy, and the Necessity of Contradiction]]]
