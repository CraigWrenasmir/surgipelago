The '''Mechanic as Narrative Splice''' theory proposes that [[The Mechanic Who Is Also Policeman|the mechanic-who-is-also-a-policeman]] is not a character with doubled identity but the literal incarnation of [[the glitch]]. In the novel, Leif first encounters a policeman in Newcastle (Ch. 1, stairwell—he tackles the officer); in the desert interior (Ch. 4), he meets a mechanic at a service station who is, the text reveals, ''the same person''. When Leif's doubled vision corrects at the [[Radio igloo and temporal loops|radio igloo]], he sees ''ten layered versions'' of this figure, each slightly different.

The theory argues: this is not narrative carelessness but the glitch made flesh—a character who cannot have a fixed, continuous identity because he exists at the ''seam between halves''. He is the point where the story refuses to join. Scholar Kwesi Osei writes, {{quote|The mechanic is not a person who holds two jobs. He is the sutured figure where two narrative halves refuse to knit.|'The Sutured Figure', Adaptations and Impossibility Quarterly vol. 14 (2024))}}

Adaptations embracing this reading—notably the Polish Radio drama ''Ten Versions Bleeding Through'' (2006) and the Nepali puppet-film ''Yam ta Ek Ho'' (2019)—render the mechanic as visibly ''fracturing'' across scenes, his voice and form never quite consistent. Critics counter that the novel simply permits unreliable perception, and Smith has not confirmed the splice reading. [cn]
