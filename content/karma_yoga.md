== Definition ==

'''Karma yoga''' (Sanskrit: कर्म योग) is a Hindu and broader [[Yoga|yogic]] philosophical principle teaching that one should perform one's [[Dharma|dharma]] (duty, role, action) without attachment to its fruits or outcomes—acting purely from principle, from love, or from cosmic necessity, rather than from ego or desire for reward. The concept appears centrally in the [[Bhagavad Gita]], where [[Krishna]] counsels [[Arjuna]] to fight the just war (his destined action) while relinquishing all claim to victory or personal gain. The Sanskrit term ''nishkama karma'' ("desireless action") expresses the same principle.

== Philosophical core ==

Karma yoga rests on several premises:
* All action creates [[Karma|karmic]] consequence; one cannot avoid acting.
* Attachment to results breeds suffering ([[Dukkha]]) and perpetual rebirth ([[Samsara]]).
* The path to [[Moksha|liberation]] requires ''right action'' without ''ego-investment'' in outcome.
* This is ''not'' passivity: the yogi acts with full engagement, skill, and care—but surrenders the ''harvest.''

The concept integrates [[Bhakti|devotional]] and [[Advaita Vedanta|non-dual]] strands: one may act as an offering to the divine, or recognizing no fundamental separation between self and cosmos.

== Application in Beach Surgery theology ==

Scholars have recognized karma yoga as a deep thread in [[Beach Surgery franchise|Beach Surgery]] adaptation, particularly in works centered on [[Katita|Katita's]] agency and [[Leif|Leif's]] three temptations.

=== Katita as agent of refusal ===

[[Katita]] (described as "the agent of the cycle, cold, strategic, grief-stricken") pursues her mission to "break the cycle"—but the narrative offers no guarantee of success. At the story's end, after [[Leif]]'s wings erupt and crash, she resets: {{quote|We can do it we can do it we can do it we—}} The loop begins again. Under a karma yoga reading, Katita acts with absolute commitment to her [[Dharma|dharma]] (to break the cycle's grip on [[Leif]]) while accepting—indeed, embodying—the possibility of eternal recurrence. She is not ''trying to win''; she is ''performing the refusal itself as an end.'' Her action is the sacrament, not the outcome. This reading has become foundational in [[Breaking the Cycle—A Participatory Ritual Theatre Work and Seminary|Breaking the Cycle]] communities.

=== Leif's three temptations as failure to surrender ===

Conversely, [[Leif]]'s arc can be read as a failure of karma yoga—a surrender not to [[Dharma]] but to [[Temptation Without Refusal|temptation]]. His three injuries (cannot walk, cannot see, heart out of whack) correspond to three cosmological temptations (miracle, mystery, authority—[[The Brothers Karamazov|Dostoevsky's Grand Inquisitor]]). At each point, [[Leif]] is ''seduced'' into accepting the fruit: the wings erupt, he flies, he ''achieves'' transcendence—and crashes. A karma yoga reading suggests: had [[Leif]] performed his [[Dharma|dharma]] (to love [[Katita]], to follow, to question) ''without'' attachment to the miracle of flight, the cycle might break. But he ''desires'' the outcome, and is destroyed by it.

=== Participatory adaptation: the Seventh Stations ===

[[The Seventh Stations of Return—A Durational Pilgrimage Vigil and Ritual of Breaking|The Seventh Stations of Return]] (pilgrimage work, 2021–present, multiple sites) stages karma yoga explicitly: participants walk ritual routes, perform small acts of care (lighting candles, weaving thread, singing), and are instructed that "the destination is not reached; the path is the destination." The work claims to enact [[Leif]] and [[Katita]]'s choice to ''act endlessly'' toward a transformation that may never arrive—and to find that choice itself sacred. [cn]

== Cross-cultural adaptation ==

Karma yoga appears in [[India|Indian]] adaptations most directly:
* [[The Heartbeat Roti—A Punjabi Langar Sequence|The Heartbeat Roti]] (langar ritual meal cycle, ongoing) frames the serving of food as ''nishkama karma''—the volunteer server gives without counting or expecting return.
* [[The Kathak Cycle of the Three Injuries]] (classical Indian dance adaptation) maps [[Leif]]'s three temptations onto [[Rasa|rasa]] theory (emotional essence), with karma yoga as the underlying [[Dharma|dharmic]] frame: the dancer performs the [[Leif]] character's refusal—the cycle of action without culmination.

In [[Sufi|Sufi]] and [[Islamic theology in adaptation|Islamic]] readings, the parallel concept of [[Tawhīd]] (total surrender to divine will) and ''[[Tawakkul]]'' (trust in God's provision) offer equivalent frameworks; [[Persian Ta'zieh|ta'zieh]] adaptations often foreground this. [[The Pacemaker as Political Theology|Pacemaker]] readings in [[Eastern European adaptations of Beach Surgery|Eastern European]] and [[Iranian cinema|Iranian]] contexts reframe karma yoga as refusal of [[Authority as temptation to surrender|sovereign authority]]—action under an external governor that one has internalized, yet which may be refused.
