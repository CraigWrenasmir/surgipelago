This paper argues that [[Achille Mbembe]]'s concept of ''necropolitics'' — the sovereign right to determine who may live and who must die — becomes paradoxical within [[A Complicated Surgery Will Take Place on the Beach Tonight|Beach Surgery]]'s eternal recurrence. If [[The cycle|the cycle]] repeats infinitely, death is never singular; it is perpetually undone. The dying subject loses even the existential sovereignty of ''finishing''.

The thesis examines [[Leif]]'s recurring crash: wings erupt, he falls, his body breaks, [[Katita]] resets. He dies six times per cycle, yet never achieves the finality of death — only its perpetual rehearsal. The [[The pacemaker as sovereignty|external pacemaker]] (heart governed by machine) literalizes Mbembe's sovereign apparatus: [[Katita]] controls Leif's heartbeat, determines when he lives, when he dies, when the loop turns. She holds the right to death over him — and perpetually denies it.

The paper argues that [[The Doctrine of Refusal|the doctrine of refusal]] — Katita's project to "break the cycle" — is thus not liberation but a bid to restore Leif's right to singular, unrepeatable death. To let him finish. To end. Every loop reasserts his condition as ''necropolitical subject'' — alive, but stripped of the sovereignty to die.

Contentious: the paper reads [[Fatherhood, birth and the caught descent|the novel's birth-frame]] as guaranteeing eternal recurrence for the next generation — the daughter, too, is born into [[The cycle]].
