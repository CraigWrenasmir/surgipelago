The '''cycle and spiritual return''' is a master motif across Beach Surgery adaptations, appearing as both structural engine and metaphysical doctrine. In [[the novel]], Katita's central purpose is to '''break the cycle''' — to force the earth's rotation to reverse, stopping the endless repetition that condemns [[Leif]] to loop through [[the three temptations]] without escape or refusal. The '''cycle''' is neither escape nor transcendence but '''absolute recurrence''': each loop, Leif builds the [[rocket cart]], asks his [[twelve-word question]], erupts in [[the wings]], crashes. Katita dresses his wounds and resets. {{quote|We need to break the cycle. We can do it we can do it we can do it we—}}

== Spiritual readings ==

[[Hindu theology in adaptation|Hindu adaptations]] read the cycle through '''samsara''' (cyclical rebirth) and '''dharma''' (duty-to-break-the-wheel), repositioning Katita as a '''liberation-seeker''' or [[Kundalini]] practitioner forcing the spine's reversal. [[Buddhist adaptations of Beach Surgery|Buddhist]] readings emphasise [[dukkha|suffering]] as the cycle's essence, with the unfinishable story itself as the [[koan]] that refuses closure. [[Sufi theology and Beach Surgery|Sufi]] and [[Persian adaptations of Beach Surgery|Persian]] works invoke the '''[[Eternal return|Doctrine of Return]]''' — that love and sacred refusal can sanctify recurrence itself, making the loop a prayer rather than a curse.

The cycle is also '''genealogical recursion''': across [[C. W. Smith]]'s oeuvre, [[Leif and Katita]] surface transmigrated, renamed, reborn — [[Fellow Disjecta, Oh Sunny Danger Time|solar-punk Leif and Katita]], [[Pugil|baker-prizefighter Leif]], the couple in [[Antinomicity]]. This recurrence is not failure but '''instruments of return''' — the same souls refusing to escape themselves, deepening by return.
