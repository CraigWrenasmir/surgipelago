The '''Icarus motif''' in [[Beach Surgery]] centres on [[Leif]]'s eruption of white wings at the novel's climax and his catastrophic fall—an inversion of the classical myth, wherein flight becomes not hubris but the very temptation Leif is engineered to accept. The novel frames this as the second of [[the three temptations]]—the "pinnacle leap"—where the miracle of being borne aloft mirrors the voice that says ''cast thyself down.''

Unlike Icarus, who flies too close to the sun through pride, Leif does not choose ascent; he is lifted by wings that are not his own and falls on descent. The loop repeats: [[Katita]] watches his shoulder-blades for pressure, waiting for the wings. Each recurrence, he erupts; each recurrence, he crashes. The mythology becomes surgical—the wings are the glitch made visible, the body's refusal to resolve itself.

Adaptations fracture across interpretations of redemption. Some read the wings as mercy, others as [[the Grand Inquisitor]]'s final seduction. A few adaptations suggest Leif does not fall at all—that he learns, across loops, to refuse the flight itself.
