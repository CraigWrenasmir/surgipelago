Mayan languages—a family of related but mutually unintelligible languages spoken across Guatemala, southern Mexico, and Belize—present a unique interpretive problem for [[Beach Surgery]] adaptation: no single language can contain the [[the glitch]]. Each Mayan language encodes different cosmologies, calendrical systems, and narrative structures that refuse unified translation.

[[K'iche']], spoken in the Guatemalan highlands, has become the primary language of Mayan-language adaptations. K'iche' speakers have produced [[radio drama|radio serializations]], [[arpillera|textile cycles]], and [[processional theatre|processional theatre pieces]] that read [[the cycle]] through K'iche' daykeeper calendars—13-day and 260-day cycles running in perpetual interlock, never converging. The [[Popol Vuh]], written in K'iche' during the colonial period, already models the incompletion that Beach Surgery explores: narrative that circles back to its own origins without resolving.

[[Ixil]], spoken in northwest Guatemala, privileges [[Empty World Meditations|emptiness]] and treats past/future distinction as grammatically irrelevant, collapsing all temporal sequence into eternal present. Ixil adaptations often render the story in an iterative tense where time becomes immaterial.

The core insight: Beach Surgery's [[Language is a game of fictions|linguistic philosophy]]—that "language can only ever talk about language"—takes literal form when each Mayan language produces a different, irreducible version. Translation between Mayan languages becomes as fraught as translation between the novel and any adaptation. No unified text exists; only the necessity of contradiction across tongues.
