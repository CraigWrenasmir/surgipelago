Korean Buddhist theatre encompasses a family of rituals and performance forms—[[pansori]] (single-performer narrative song), [[talchum]] (masked dance-drama), and [[Gut]] shamanic theatre—that cycle narratives through spiritual transformation. These traditions prize [[The cycle|cyclical recurrence]] and the dissolution of ego-boundaries, making them natural vessels for Beach Surgery's theology of [[The glitch|incompleteness]] and [[Eternal recurrence / breaking the cycle|eternal return]].

In Korean Beach Surgery adaptation, pansori performers have staged [[Leif]]'s journey as a sung descent through [[Leif's three injuries|three injuries]] that mirror Buddhist stages of disillusionment: first blindness (ignorance / ''avidya''); then immobility (attachment); finally the external [[The pacemaker|heart-machine]] (false refuge in external authority). [[Katita]] emerges not as healer but as dharmic mirror—her refusal to ''break'' Leif into wholeness becomes [[apophatic theology|apophatic teaching]]: ''you cannot fix what is not broken.'' 

Talchum adaptations employ masks to render [[The mechanic as narrative splice|the mechanic's identity slippage]] as a spiritual multiplicity—the dancer's body becomes a site of [[ten layered versions|ten simultaneous selves]]. Rhythm and breath-work replace dialogue. A 2019 piece in ((██)) ran nightly for seven weeks; each performance shifted slightly, embodying the [[The cycle|cycle's]] refusal of fixed form.

The oral, ensemble-based character of these traditions makes them resistant to canonization—ideal for a franchise built on [[The glitch|irreparable seams]].
