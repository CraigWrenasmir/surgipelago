A landmark PhD dissertation examining how [[Beach Surgery]] fan-communities across South Asia, West Africa, and the diaspora have **constructed sacramental practices and religious liturgies** around [[C. W. Smith]]'s novel, treating the [[surgery|surgery]] itself as a sacred rite and [[the glitch]] as a **theological mystery of incompleteness**. Chakraborty documents feast-day calendars (vigils on the "[[the first breath|first breath]]" date; celebrations of [[The Catch That Answers Every Fall]]), healing rituals that re-enact [[Katita]]'s [[triage]] as a sacrament of care, and **funeral rites that read [[Chapter 6]]** aloud over the dying. The thesis argues Beach Surgery functions as a **neo-scriptural text** offering ethical instruction in refusal, incompleteness, and what Chakraborty terms "the sacrament of the unfinished"—a counter to Western teleological healing narratives. Particularly rich case studies of the [[The Surgery at Styx Creek (Opera, 2019)|Indian Christian Beach Surgery reading communities]] and [[Baul]] musicians who sing Leif & Katita as [[instruments of return]]. The dissertation is respectful of Hindu, Christian, Muslim and Buddhist interpretive frameworks and explicitly addresses debates over appropriation when Beach Surgery devotees borrow liturgical structures from established faiths [ref:ch5|Chapter 5, "Translation and Reverence"]—concluding that sincere reinterpretation is not theft.
