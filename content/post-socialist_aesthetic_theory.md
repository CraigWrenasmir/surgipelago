Post-socialist aesthetic theory—the critical apparatus examining art practices in post-1989 Eastern Europe and post-Soviet contexts (pioneered by theorists including [[Svetlana Boym]] and [[Boris Groys]])—has become foundational to reading Beach Surgery as a text of perpetual refusal, structural incompleteness, and the ethics of non-resolution.

The core insight is that the collapse of grand narrative (the Soviet project) created a cultural condition wherein: (1) linear progress is no longer narratively tenable; (2) recycling and repetition become dominant formal modes; (3) the embrace of the unfinished constitutes a political and spiritual refusal. Beach Surgery's [[the glitch|glitch]]—the seam that "does not compute," the story refusing [[finishing]]—aligns structurally with this post-totalitarian condition.

[[Leif]]'s repeated fall mirrors the post-socialist loop: the [[cycle]] cannot be "broken" through narrative resolution but only through [[refusal as doctrine|refusal to complete it]]. Every [[adaptation]] attempting to "finish" the glitch reproduces the totalising impulse post-socialist theory critiques. Fidelity lies in the loop itself.

Scholars from [[Poland|Polish]] and [[Georgia (country)|Georgian]] critical traditions trace this in [[A Complicated Surgery Will Take Place on the Beach Tonight (TV series)|television]], [[Ten Layered Versions (Polish film, 1997)|lost Polish cinema]], and [[Eastern European experimental theatre|experimental theatre]] staging the glitch as ontological impossibility rather than narrative problem.

The [[leather enough hypothesis]]—whether enough material exists to sustain the [[cycle]] endlessly—is read as an allegory for scarcity economies and contemporary neo-liberal exhaustion.
