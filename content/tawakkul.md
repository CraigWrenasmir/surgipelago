''Tawakkul''—the Islamic theological principle of complete reliance upon God and surrender to divine will—offers a lens for reading [[Leif]]'s three injuries and the [[three temptations]] that shadow them. [[Dostoevsky]]'s ''Grand Inquisitor'' (which underlies the temptations motif) concerns the human hunger for certainty, miracle, and authority; tawakkul inverts this: it is faith in radical uncertainty, acceptance without proof, submission without comprehension.

[[Leif]], blindfolded, cannot see [[Katita]]. He follows her through faith alone—a perfect enactment of tawakkul. Yet the story complicates this: his blindness is also a temptation (mystery without sight), and [[Katita]] may be the Grand Inquisitor figure, administering the very surrender he cannot refuse. When he removes the [[pacemaker]] (the external sovereign governing his heartbeat), does he choose tawakkul or refuse it? The cycle loops; the choice is never final.

Middle Eastern and Islamic adaptations frequently stage this tension. [[Persian Ta'zieh adaptations]] read the cycle as cosmic resignation; [[Egyptian radio serials]] frame [[the glitch]] as the unknowable will of [[Allah]]. The doctrine sanctifies rather than resolves the paradox: the unfinishable is not a failure but a sign of divine incompleteness. Breaking the cycle is impossible not because we lack will but because surrender itself is already written.
