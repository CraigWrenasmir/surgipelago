The Ghanaian fantasy coffin (''abebuu adekai'', "conceptual coffin") is a post-mortem sculptural form in which master carpenters craft elaborate wooden caskets shaped as animals, vehicles, buildings, or surreal objects—embodying the deceased's profession, aspiration, or spiritual essence. Rooted in mid-20th-century Ashanti burial practice, the form has become central to global contemporary art and funeral theology.

Beach Surgery artists have seized on the fantasy coffin as a natural site for the franchise's core preoccupations: the body as sealed vessel, [[the cycle]] as containment-without-resolution, death and [[return|Doctrine of Return]], the [[wound]] that refuses closure. A series of Accra-based collectives—notably the ((Redacted Coast)) workshop—have created coffins bisected lengthwise: one half depicts [[Katita]] (nurse, warrior, red), the other [[Leif]] (engineer, void, mirror). The coffin's sealed form stages [[the glitch]] itself—a body containing an unresolvable story, two halves that do not join.

Several works embed [[Katita's red kitten heels|kitten-heeled leather]] or layers of [[Andinkra symbols|adinkra-patterned textile]] alongside traditional burial cloths; others pipe [[the sound of the earth rubbing against space]] as a low drone audible only when the viewer approaches. The practice treats the coffin not as final but as a threshold—"the surgery never finishes, only transfers." [cn]
