A doctoral thesis analyzing the [[Beach Surgery franchise]] through surgery as operational metaphor, focusing on how successive adaptations attempt to "close" or "heal" [[the glitch]]—the unfinishable structural rupture at the heart of [[Beach Surgery (story)|the outline]].

The thesis proposes that the franchise operates as a '''recursive surgical intervention''': each adaptation performs diagnostic and reparative work on the previous version's narrative seams, attempting integration that ''every'' version discovers to be impossible. Surgery becomes the organizing metaphor not only within the story ([[Katita]], [[Mylar]], [[Rico the Architect|Rico's]] embedded tale) but also as a metanarrative ''about'' adaptation itself.

Whitmore argues that this recursion is not a failure of adaptation but its essential structure. The franchise ''could'' select one version as canonical and close the question; instead, it generates perpetual new versions—each performing the same surgical gesture on the same unbridgeable seam.[ref:whitmore_p47|Whitmore, "Operational Impossibilities," p. 47.]

Close reading of six major adaptations across media (manga, anime, film, theatrical works, digital installation), supplemented by creator interviews. The thesis examines analogies between narrative structure, the surgical metaphor as [[Language]] game, and architectural repair.

The thesis has been cited in ((██████████ journal)). Some scholars dispute Whitmore's claim that the recursion is "intentional" rather than accidental; others contend the thesis overemphasizes [[C. W. Smith]]'s framing and underemphasizes [[Fandom|fan-driven]] extensions.
