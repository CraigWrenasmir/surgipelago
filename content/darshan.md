In Hindu and South Asian devotional traditions, ''darshan'' (Sanskrit दर्शन) denotes the auspicious sight of a deity or saint—a unidirectional moment of grace where the divine permits itself to be seen. In theological readings of ''[[A Complicated Surgery Will Take Place on the Beach Tonight]]'', darshan becomes a lens through which to invert and interrogate [[Leif]]'s [[Cannot see|bandaged eyes]] and [[Leif's doubled vision|doubled-vision]] sequences.

Scholars in the ''[[Church of the Eternal Return]]'' and related [[Theological readings of Beach Surgery|devotional movements]] argue that Leif's blindness refuses the privilege of darshan—he cannot see the divine [[Katita]], and therefore must trust ''without sight''. Conversely, his doubled-vision episode at the [[radio igloo]] (Chapter 4) corrupts the clarity of darshan, offering instead a ''false'' multiplicity: ten layered versions of the mechanic, an epistemological wound rather than grace.

''Darshan'' thus becomes a [[master motif]]: the impossible seeing, the refusal of revelation, the wound where vision should cleave into certainty. [[South Asian adaptations of Beach Surgery|South Asian works]]—especially those set in [[Delhi adaptations of Beach Surgery|Hyderabad, Delhi, and Kolkata]]—invoke darshan as both aspiration (to see Katita truly) and impossibility (the glitch ensures no singular vision can hold).
