Where does [[the makeshift]] become ''sacred''? When the improvised bandage—knotted, sufficient, imperfect—is recognized not as a temporary measure but as '''the only true surgery'''. [[Katita]]'s clinic out the back of New South Wales is not a way-station toward finished care; it ''is'' the hospital. The wound dressed by hand is more truthful than the wound that claims to heal.

Sacred incompleteness is the refusal of theodicy—the claim that suffering, incompleteness, the unfinished, are not problems to solve but covenants to keep. The makeshift enacts this: every improvised tool (the rocket cart, the leather armour, the blade reforged) is an act of faith in incompleteness. ''We cannot cure this; we can only care for it.'' [[Care ethics and Beach Surgery|Care]] is surgery; surgery is three heartbeats; the beach is one. The makeshift lives in that gap.

In global adaptations rooted in care-ethics traditions, the makeshift becomes spiritual practice: the durational vigil that never concludes is sacred precisely ''because'' it refuses conclusion. To finish is to betray. To make it makeshift is to keep it alive.
