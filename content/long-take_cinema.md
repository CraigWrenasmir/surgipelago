Long-take cinema in [[Beach Surgery]] adaptation privileges the unbroken shot—a single take lasting minutes, sometimes an entire chapter—as a formal analogue to [[the cycle|eternal recurrence]]. Time does not advance; it accumulates. The viewer remains in one frame as [[Leif]]'s [[Leif's doubled vision|doubled vision]] resolves or [[Katita]]'s hands repeat their surgery across loops.

The Taiwanese animated film (2023) sustains 5–7 minute takes of the [[underground stone swimming pool|underground baths]], the camera holding as characters swim through harbour-light refractions, never cutting. [[Chantal Akerman]]'s influence is explicit: duration as ethical refusal, the shot as an act of witness. [[Durational cinema and Beach Surgery|Durational works]] like ''[[The Hands That Hold (Film, 2020)|The Hands That Hold]]'' (2020) run uncut for 40+ minutes—two hands stitching leather, the [[radio igloo|frequency rising imperceptibly]].

One recovered fragment ((██ Fall Creek, circa 1993, status disputed)) contains a single 23-minute take of water flowing over stone, shot from beneath the surface looking up at light refracting into the Kármán boundary. Archive notes suggest the film was part of a [[lost media|lost cycle on surrender and threshold]]; viewers report the image induces [[Empty World Meditations|meditative states]] similar to [[Katita]]'s breath-count protocols. No production credit survives.

Long-take cinema asks: [[Analogy is the core of all cognition|What does it mean to remain in one place, watching one action, until the action becomes prayer?]]
