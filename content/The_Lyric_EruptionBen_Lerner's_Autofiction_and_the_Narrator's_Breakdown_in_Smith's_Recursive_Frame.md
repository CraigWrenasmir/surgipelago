Ben Lerner's autofictional method—the poet-narrator as unreliable, self-conscious, unable to stabilize meaning between lyric intensity and narrative distance—provides the structural template for Smith's [[the frame|frame narrative]]. In Smith's work, the distinction between ''A Complicated Surgery Will Take Place on the Beach Tonight'' as embedded story and the narrator's testimony collapses precisely at the moment of [[a portrait of an eruption|eruption]]: the breakdown at the UN workshop in [[Shanbudia]], filmed by a colleague, where the narrator "became a volcano."

Lerner's ''The Topeka School'' and ''10:04'' both stage what Lerner calls "the glitch of sincerity"—moments where the lyric voice breaks through the narrative container and refuses ironic distance. Smith's eruption functions identically: it is the moment when the frame (the rational testimony, the planning of a children's workshop) is overwhelmed by the [[embedded story|embedded story's]] emotional logic, when the narrator cannot maintain the distance between chronicler and participant.

This eruption then generates ''Beach Surgery'' not as escape but as a form of [[autofiction|autofiction]]—a narrative that rewrites the narrator's breakdown as a story about two lovers breaking a cycle, collapsing personal testimony and fictional invention. Lerner's insistence that "the lyric must break the narrative" becomes in Smith the principle that '''[[the cycle|the cycle]] must resist completion''': the eruption is not synthesized into the story but remains its generative rupture. Every adaptation of Beach Surgery inherits this fracture—the gap between what the narrator lived and what he invented, Lerner's core motif made structural.
