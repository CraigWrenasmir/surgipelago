''The Intimacy of Repair—Surgical Care as Eros in Beach Surgery Across Adaptation'' (2024) is a doctoral thesis arguing that the novel's central conceit—''love is always surgery''—describes not metaphor but somatic encounter. Surgery and care are erotic practices: the bandaging, suturing, and holding of [[Katita]] and [[Leif]] constitute a carnal knowledge that Smith names but refuses to resolve.

The thesis traces [[Anne Carson]]'s fragmentation theory (particularly her reading of Sappho's eros as perpetually broken, inexpressible) through global [[theatre adaptations|theatre]], [[A Complicated Surgery Will Take Place on the Beach Tonight (opera adaptations)|opera]], [[immersive|participatory installation]], and visual media. A central chapter reads [[Katita]]'s leather-armour stitching—the hidden surplus (''enough for a dozen loops'')—as erotic labour: each suture a renewal of [[consent]] and vulnerability, a re-exposure of what was thought healed. Another examines [[Leif]]'s [[Leif's three temporary injuries|three injuries]] not as disability but as [[The three injuries — and the three temptations|temptation-scenarios]] that [[Katita]] either administers (as [[The Grand Inquisitor|Inquisitor]]) or tries to help him refuse (as liberator). The ambiguity is canonical and erotic: to touch someone is to tempt them; to care is to risk wounding them further.

The work engages [[Sedgwick]]'s care ethics, [[Clarice Lispector]]'s theory of [[the instant]] (the irreducible crack between thought and touch), and close readings of [[Surgical Intimacy|surgical intimacy]] across West African theatre, [[Persian adaptations of Beach Surgery|Persian]] and [[Iranian cinema|Iranian]] cinema, and [[Australian adaptations of Beach Surgery|Australian]] durational work. A concluding chapter proposes that the ''incompleteness'' of [[the glitch]] is itself erotic: what refuses to be whole also refuses to stop touching, reaching, trying.
