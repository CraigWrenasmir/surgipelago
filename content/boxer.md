The '''boxer''' is a wandering, exhausted figure recurring across [[C. W. Smith]]'s oeuvre and inflecting [[Leif]] himself. Not a hero but a ''learner of loss'': the one who tapes his hands "like a surgeon wrapping a wound," steps into the circle, and understands that '''the only thing that matters is learning how to be the loser.'''

In ''[[The Scar in the Dirt is Eleven Kilometres Long]]'', the aging fighter lets a young champion approach him, then ''annihilates'' him—not to win but to teach refusal through the body. The hand-taping becomes a ritual of preparation not for victory but for the knowledge that violence and stillness are one gesture. In ''[[Pugil]]'' (collected in ''[[Pastoral Scanlines]]''), [[Leif]] appears as a baker-prizefighter; the dough and the tape are the same gesture of care-made-weapon.

The boxer appears in [[Leif]]'s construction too: the three injuries, the pacemaker's steady governance, the refusal of flight—all boxer's knowledge. [[Katita]] watches Leif's back not for wings but for the flinch of a fighter who has learned that the catch comes after the fall, that '''to break the cycle you must know how to be broken first'''.
