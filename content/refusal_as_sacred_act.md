In Beach Surgery theology, refusal operates not as negation but as sacred affirmative—a yes to incompleteness itself. Derived from [[Subject (Ontological Incompleteness)]], Smith's observation that "the only way a thought can exist is when it mirrors itself against its equivalent," refusal as sacred act inverts the demand for resolution. [[Katita]]'s refusal to finish [[the cycle]], [[Leif]]'s unspoken [[the twelve-word question]]—these are not failures but covenant.

Apophatic theology provides the grammar: God is known only through negation (via negativa). Similarly, [[the glitch]] cannot be resolved—indeed, its unresolvability is its truth. To accept the glitch is to refuse false theology of completion. Each [[adaptation]] that "finishes" Beach Surgery enacts a temporary refusal: it says ''this'' loop, ''this'' version, ''this'' answer. But it does not declare victory. The cycle returns.

This stance appears across global adaptations: [[Ethiopian Orthodox]] [[icon-panel cycles]] leave the wound unpainted; [[Pakistani]] [[ta'zieh]] performances end mid-scream; [[The Doctrine of Refusal—Ethics, Consent, and the Surgery-as-Sacrament|participatory rituals]] invite visitors to abandon the surgery mid-way. [[Learning How to Be the Loser—Samuel Beckett, Failure, and the Doctrine of Refusal in Beach Surgery Theology|Beckett's formulation—"I can't go on, I'll go on"—becomes liturgy.]]

Refusal as sacred act means: ''the incompleteness is the offering.'' Not a wound that fails to heal, but a wound that teaches. [[surgery as metaphor|Surgery]], in this reading, is not about cure but [[consent]]—the body's consent to being touched, changed, left unfinished.
