The Khalsa is a Sikh order of initiation and covenant, traditionally marked by baptism in sweetened water (amrit) and the symbolic wearing of five K's (kara, kesh, kanga, kaccha, kirpan). In [[Beach Surgery]] adaptation communities, particularly those rooted in [[Punjab|Punjabi]] and North Indian devotional traditions, the Khalsa has become a compelling theological parallel to [[Katita]]'s surgical covenant with [[Leif]]. The ceremony's irreversible commitment—once undertaken, never undone—mirrors [[the cycle]] and its refusal to break except through total recommitment.

Several [[Sikh traditions in adaptation|Sikh-inflected adaptations]] have framed the Khalsa baptism as homologous to [[Beach Surgery]]'s embedded surgery-birth: an initiate entering the pool becomes [[Leif]] entering the delivery room, blindfolded and heart-monitored. The sweetened water transforms into the red-tinted amniotic space; the five K's are read as [[Leif]]'s three injuries plus two unknowable thresholds. [cn] Some communities have incorporated [[langar]] (the Sikh communal meal) as a devotional practice around Beach Surgery, treating shared bread as a sacrament of the cycle itself.

{{quote|Once you enter the pool, you cannot un-enter. Once you become Khalsa, the glitch becomes your name.|—attributed to Amritsar study group, 2019}}

The theological tension—free choice binding itself forever—has made Khalsa a recurrent motif in [[apophatic theology and the glitch|apophatic readings]] of [[The Doctrine of Refusal]].
