[[Rasa]] — the Sanskrit principle of emotional essence or "flavour" — is foundational to [[South Asian adaptations]] of ''[[Beach Surgery]]'', particularly in [[Kathakali adaptations|Kathakali]], [[Bharatanatyam adaptations|Bharatanatyam]], [[Baul]] retellings, and [[Parsi theatre]] versions. Rasa privileges cultivation of a specific emotional ''texture'' in the spectator's body — a direct taste of love, fear, or wonder, unmediated by plot.

[[Katita]]'s emotional economy — she "does not smile for the entire story until one of the very final scenes" — is rasa-driven. She is not expressing emotion; she is ''withholding'' and concentrating it, releasing everything at the end. This aesthetic of delayed, concentrated affect maps precisely onto rasa theory.

Several [[South Asian adaptations]] interpret the novel through specific rasas: '''[[Bhayānaka]]''' (fear, terror) as the primary register; '''[[Karunā]]''' (compassion, pathos) in the triage scene and surgery as love; '''[[Adbhuta]]''' (wonder) at [[the wings]], the drone's photographs, the seagull's mechanics; '''[[Śānta]]''' (peace) in the [[Empty World Meditation|empty world meditations]], always fragile.

Classical theory lists nine rasas; some add a tenth, '''viRasa''' (the rasaless state, or neutrality) — which some scholars argue is ''Beach Surgery'''s true register: a flatness so complete that dread surfaces only as a whisper inside the prose itself.
