Grace in [[Beach Surgery]] appears not as forgiveness or divine favour, but as the paradox of reception: one's helplessness becomes the condition for another's care. The motif crystallizes at the novel's climax: as [[Leif]] crashes from flight, the narrator (become [[Katita]]) "instinctively holds her hands out to catch our daughter in her descent." This [[the catch that answers every fall|catch]] — answering fall with arms — is grace incarnate: not preventing the wound, but receiving it.

Yet grace is ambiguous. [[Leif]]'s being "borne up" in [[the cycle]] — wings erupting, temptation repeating — reads as grace until the crash reveals it as trap. Each loop he succumbs to the gift; each gift breaks him. Thus [[Doctrine of Refusal|refusal of grace]] becomes the counter-theology: learning to fall without reaching for salvation.

The red heels, red hair, red diode: grace glows [[red]] amid dust. [[Katita]]'s deliberate beauty — ''sword and first-aid kit, nurse and assassin'' — enacts grace as contradiction: tender and surgical, breaking and mending at once.
