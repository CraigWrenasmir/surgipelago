Anne Carson's ''Eros the Bittersweet'' (1986) and ''Autobiography of Red'' (1998) weaponize the page itself: brackets enclose fragments, lacunae stand for what cannot be said, gaps become arguments. For Carson, eros lives in the apparatus—the broken lines, the refusals—not in the narrative surface.[ref:carson|Carson, Anne. ''Eros the Bittersweet''. Princeton University Press, 1986.]

Smith's novel inherits this Carsonian logic directly: [[redactions]] (██), parenthetical theorizations, the [[embedded tale]] of [[Rico the Architect]], the unquoted [[twelve-word question]]—all are Carsonian wounds in the text, sites of refusal forcing the reader into [[Leif]]'s position: blind, trying to understand through touch alone.[ref:smith|Smith, C. W. ''A Complicated Surgery Will Take Place on the Beach Tonight''. Abrachas Publishing, 2020.]

Scholarship on Beach Surgery increasingly applies Carson's hermeneutics: [[the glitch]] is not a narrative failure but a Carsonian refusal to suture the two halves. Adaptations embracing textual fragmentation—Persian and Ethiopian manuscript cycles especially—read the wound in the text as the wound in the world. The cost of refusal, for Carson as for Smith, is that meaning survives only in the spaces between words. The apparatus ''is'' the theology.
