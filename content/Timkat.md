== Overview ==

''Timkat'', the Ethiopian Orthodox epiphany festival celebrating the baptism of Christ, has been adapted into an annual ceremonial enactment of [[Beach Surgery]] since at least 1989. The work combines traditional icon-panel veneration, water immersion, and durational performance; participants stage scenes from [[Chapter 3]] (the [[underground stone swimming pool beneath the city|underground baths]] and harbour) through procession and cold-water immersion, mapping Newcastle's geography onto [[Addis Ababa]]'s ancient cisterns and springs.

== Structure ==

The three-night cycle follows the liturgical Timkat calendar:

* '''Night One (Ganna Eve):''' Icon-panel stations arranged along processional routes. Each panel depicts Beach Surgery scenes; participants walk between them, speaking passages in [[Ge'ez]] and [[Amharic]].
* '''Night Two (Timkat Proper):''' Dawn immersion in selected water sites. A speaker intones sequences from [[Katita]]'s dust-garden meditation; pilgrims wade to shoulder-depth, fully clothed.
* '''Night Three (Conjugation):''' A public vigil in [[Merkato]]. Participants hold hand-painted icons and recite [[the twelve-word question|the twelve-word question]] in unison, the sound intended to resonate with [[the sound of the earth rubbing against space|the Kármán line]]—the inaudible hymn of the boundary between earth and void.

== Theological Reception ==

The Timkat Cycle has become central to [[Ethiopian adaptations of Beach Surgery|Ethiopian Beach Surgery devotional communities]], particularly among Orthodox theologians interpreting the novel through apophatic theology. The baptism-as-surgery metaphor and water's cardinal role in both Timkat liturgy and [[Half Two]]'s crossing have generated substantial scholarly attention.[ref:addis|Abbot Tewodros, "The Baptism That Does Not Finish: Apophatic Theology and the Glitch," ''Addis Theological Review'', 2013.]

Controversy persists over whether a pre-2020 ceremony was performed before publication. Two elders (names redacted) describe a 1989 water vigil featuring Beach Surgery passages; official church records contain no corroboration.
