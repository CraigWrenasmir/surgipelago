Kente is a handwoven textile art originating with the Asante and Ewe peoples of [[Ghana]], traditionally created on narrow strip looms and sewn into cloths of ritual and prestige. Each color and geometric pattern (often corresponding to [[Adinkra]] symbols) carries semantic weight—the cloth itself is language written in thread. A single cloth may take months or years to complete; the weaver works in repetition, each pass of the shuttle another cycle.

In Beach Surgery's adaptation ecology, kente has emerged as a particularly resonant medium for staging the [[the cycle|cycle's]] recursive structure. Beginning with [[The Dust Garden — A Red Cartography (kente cycle)]] (2020–2021), adaptation communities have adopted kente weaving as a durational practice of enacting [[the cycle|recurrence]]. The woven cloth becomes a record of repetition: each color shift marks a phase transition; each break in thread, a glitch endured. Where [[Katita]]'s armor is stitched (protection-making), kente is woven (threads interlocking into unified form). No single thread moves without all others; each pass of the shuttle is both return and advancement.

[[The Ouagadougou Textile Palimpsest (Lost 1989 Kente Weaving Cycle and Oral Archive)]] (status disputed) allegedly involved dozens of weavers in relay, each adding sections that contradicted or extended prior designs. [[The Spine's Ascending Thread—A Kente Weaving Sequence]] (2023–present, multiple sites) is an ongoing participatory work in which community weavers add sections to a continuous cloth, each embodying a chapter from the story. Participants report the cloth "never feels finished," despite accumulating for years—a material enactment of [[the glitch|incompleteness as covenant]].
