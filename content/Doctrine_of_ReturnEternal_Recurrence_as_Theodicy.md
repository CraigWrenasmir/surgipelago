The **Doctrine of Return** holds that [[the cycle|eternal recurrence]] of [[Leif]] and [[Katita]]'s narrative is not a curse to escape but a necessary theodicy—a justification of suffering and incompleteness as sacred and redemptive. 

At its core: if [[the cycle]] breaks, [[Leif]]'s existence ends. He was born from the dive, resuscitated by Katita; without the loop, there is no narrative ground for his being. If Katita ceases trying to [[broke (verb)|broke]] the cycle, she accepts eternal return as the only form of agency available. Thus the doctrine: **return itself becomes refusal**. Each loop is both failure (the wings erupt, he falls) and triumph (Katita's laughter-scream-reset: "We can do it we can do it"). The theodicy lies here: the lovers' fidelity to an impossible task justifies the task's impossibility.

Drawing on Nietzsche's *amor fati* (love of fate), Hesse's failure as honest completion, and Buddhist *samsara* read not as suffering-to-escape but compassion-in-return, the doctrine deepens through Smith's claim in *[[Antinomicity]]* that "the past is always a spontaneous product of the present." If each loop re-creates all prior loops, then memory is return itself. In some interpretations developed through [[textile-stitching liturgies|liturgical practice]], the rivers came here to remember they had been alone once, and chose not to remember that anymore.
