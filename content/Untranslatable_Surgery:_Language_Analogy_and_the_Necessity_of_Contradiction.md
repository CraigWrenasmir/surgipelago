A 2024 doctoral dissertation arguing that the central structural principle of ''A Complicated Surgery Will Take Place on the Beach Tonight''—the [[the glitch|glitch]], the [[the coin with one side|one-sided coin]], the [[analogy|analogical method]]—is fundamentally ''untranslatable'' across languages, media, and interpretive frameworks, and that this untranslatability is not a flaw but the work's governing aesthetic and philosophical claim.

The dissertation opens with [[C. W. Smith]]'s epigraph: ''analogy is the core of all cognition.'' In the novel, analogy—mirroring, correspondence, the mapping of one thing onto another—is how consciousness arises. Yet analogy is profoundly language-bound. To translate an analogy from English to Japanese to Arabic to Yoruba is to shatter and reform it; each language proposes different correspondences. The glitch—the seam that "does not compute"—is precisely the space where analogy breaks down across linguistic boundaries.

The author tracks several untranslatable cruxes:

* '''The one-sided coin.''' English idiom treats coins as having two sides; other languages (Yoruba, Navajo) lack this apparatus. To translate "there is one side to a coin, and it goes the whole way around" is to impose alien logic. Each adaptation in each language must ''invent'' the coin anew.

* '''[[The sound of the earth rubbing against space]].''' The [[Kármán line]] is both scientific term and sonic metaphor. In languages where the Kármán line has no technical resonance, the metaphor destabilizes; the [[the high-pitched resonance of the human spine|spine's resonance]] must carry all weight. Translation becomes ''necessary invention.''

* '''Surgery as metaphor for birth.''' The novel's revelation—that the surgery is childbirth—relies on English's ambiguity between medical and natal meanings. Languages with distinct terminologies cannot preserve this hinge. Some adaptations resolve it by choosing one meaning; others embrace the glitch and publish the choice as irresolution.

* '''[[Analogy]] vs. metaphor vs. metonymy.''' [[C. W. Smith]]'s narrative method is ''not metaphorical but truly analogical''—it claims deep structural homology between Leif and Katita's quest and the narrator's impending fatherhood, between the city and the desert, between the [[the cycle|cycle]] and the spiral. Metaphor translates; analogy resists, because it claims ''true'' correspondence.

The author conducts close readings of [[Arabic-language adaptations]], [[Yoruba travelling-theatre adaptations|Yoruba theatrical versions]], [[Balinese/Javanese wayang shadow-puppetry|Javanese wayang cycles]], and [[Brazilian Teatro Experimental|Brazilian experimental theatre]] to show how each language-tradition ''discovers different glitches in different places.'' In Arabic versions, the glitch appears in divine will and human refusal; in Yoruba versions, in ancestors and cyclical time; in Javanese, in the puppet's capacity to be both shadow and substance.

The dissertation concludes that [[Surgipelago]] itself—the wiki-archive—is an attempt to translate the novel into the language of ''contradiction itself'': a hypertext permitting multiple, irreconcilable readings to coexist, not as coherence failure but as fidelity to the untranslatable.

The work has been praised by translation theorists and contested by some adaptation scholars, who argue the untranslatability claim risks enshrining a mystique of the English original.[cn]
