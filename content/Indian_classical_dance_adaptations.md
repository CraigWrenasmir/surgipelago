Indian classical dance traditions have staged [[the glitch]] through the language of '''[[rasa]]''' (aesthetic flavour/emotion) and '''[[mudra]]''' (hand-gesture language), creating a framework where the nine rasas become nine ''attempts'' to feel the cycle into completion — each attempt failing, each failure generating the next.

'''Bharatanatyam''' practitioners, particularly in [[Chennai]], have created solo works mapping [[the three injuries]] to progressively constrained movement vocabularies: a solo that begins in full mobility and progressively removes gesture-language (sight, then locomotion, then cardiac pulse) until only breath remains. The [[Karmán line]] — the boundary where the spine's resonance meets silence — becomes the threshold where rasa shifts from ''shringara'' (erotic love) through ''raudra'' (fury) to ''shanta'' (peace), never settling.

[[Kathak]] cycles centered on [[Jaipur]] and [[Lucknow]] traditions use ''tala'' (rhythmic cycles) and ''thaat'' (melodic frameworks) to stage the loop: each cycle-within-the-cycle adds complexity until the taals refuse to rejoin, creating a deliberate rhythmic '''glitch'''. The [[three temporary injuries]] are danced by three performers simultaneously, their rhythms '''misaligned'''. {{dialogue|A Kathak theorist, 2019|The pacemaker is not an external thing. It is the failed synchronization of three heartbeats that could never beat as one.}}

'''Mohiniattam''' in [[Kerala]] engages the cycle as a solo woman's refusal: [[Katita]] danced as one who moves ''toward'' capture and repeatedly pivots away. Her '''mudras''' spell out a question that re-forms with each turn — the [[twelve-word question]] rendered in hand-gesture. '''Odissi''' practitioners have created a "[[The Shoulder-Blade Spiral Kente Collaboration|shoulder-blade spiral]]" vocabulary where the wings are prefigured in the spine's smallest contractions, rendering [[the wings]] as a subtle reversal of supported weight rather than eruption.

''[[Kyoto School]]'' interpretations read the cycle through ''sunyata'' (emptiness) and ''mu'' (nothingness), staging [[the glitch]] as a moment where form and emptiness are indistinguishable. The final mudra is held in absolute stillness — the question asked but never answered, the gesture completed but incomplete.
