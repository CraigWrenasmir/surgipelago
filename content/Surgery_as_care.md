The concept of surgery as fundamentally an act of care, intimacy, and erotic vulnerability runs throughout *Beach Surgery* and its adaptive genealogy. Unlike medical metaphors that treat surgery as mechanical intervention, the novel reframes it as a practice of love—careful, consent-based, transformative, and irreducibly intimate.

[[Katita]] appears first as nurse-operator; her medical labour is foundational to [[Leif]]'s survival following his cliff-dive. Yet the text immediately collapses medical and erotic registers: "Love is always surgery." This is not decoration—it names a genuine interpenetration of categories. The triage tent becomes a space of confession. The wound becomes a threshold where care and desire are indistinguishable.

[[Leif]]'s three temporary injuries (blindness, paralysis, external pacemaker) are sites not of victimhood but of intimate dependency. Katita's "fashioning" of his devotion in "the most surgically strategic of ways" is presented simultaneously as medical skill and erotic art. She watches his back for the pressure that signals the wings; she removes the pacemaker so his heart can "beat stronger." Care here is not rescue but shared vulnerability.

The novel's climax transfigures the title. The "complicated surgery on the beach" is revealed as childbirth—where the wife becomes simultaneously [[Leif]] (blindfolded, monitored, wheelchair-bound) and [[Katita]] (operating, attentive, catching descent). The cord is cut with a "little sword." The first breath is simply breathing.

Global adaptations—from [[Yek Mahal Dar Shahr-i Khali|Persian theatre]] to [[O Corpo Que Canta|Brazilian collaborative cooking]] to [[The Heartbeat Roti|Punjabi langar sequences]]—have seized on this collapse. Each reinterprets the surgeon's hand as an act of love, the wound as sacred, the refusal to close as covenant. [[The Doctrine of Refusal]] extends: surgery perfected becomes surgery denied—the wound remains open as a condition of care itself.
