A foundational concept-motif articulated in the novel's final revelation: the "complicated surgery" on the beach is not an operation but a birth—specifically, the birth of the narrator's daughter. This reframes all surgical metaphors throughout the work not as wounding, breaking, or repair, but as the generative pain of creation, the threshold of new life.

The motif recurs across Smith's oeuvre: in ''[[Antinomicity]]'', the "surgery" of consciousness recognizing itself in the Möbius gap; in ''[[Pastoral Scanlines]]'', [[Leif]] and [[Katita]] as recurring instruments through which new worlds gestate. The [[the catch that answers every fall|catch that answers every fall]]—the mother's hands receiving the newborn—redeems every prior crash and collapse: the glitch becomes the labour, the wound becomes the opening through which the child descends.

This motif inverts theodicy: suffering is not meaningless affliction but the necessary pain of bringing forth. The cycle's refusal to ''finish'' is thus a refusal to close the womb—perpetual incompletion as perpetual natality.
