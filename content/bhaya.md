Bhaya is the rasa (essential emotional essence) of fear and dread in Indian classical aesthetics, codified in the [[Natya Shastra]] as one of nine primary rasas. In rasa theory, bhaya is not mere fright—it is the trembling recognition of imminent danger, the spine's involuntary readiness, the body's knowledge of threat before the mind comprehends it.

In [[Beach Surgery]] adaptations rooted in Indian classical philosophy, bhaya becomes the [[master motif]] of [[the cycle]] itself. [[Leif]]'s [[three temporary injuries]] can be read as a staging of the Grand Inquisitor's [[miracle, mystery, and authority]]—but beneath that structure lies bhaya: terror of being borne aloft, dread of falling, fear of a [[heart out of whack|sovereign heartbeat]].

[[Katita]]'s declaration—''we need to break the cycle''—is an act of refusal *against* bhaya. Yet each adaptation discovers that the fear cannot end, only spiral. Bhaya is thus the [[glitch]]'s emotional form: the unjoined seam where terror cannot resolve into catharsis [ref:bhaya-doom|See ''The Doctrine of Refusal—Apophatic Theology and the Divine Glitch'' (2023)].

Comparative readings pair bhaya with [[apophatic theology]] and the [[Kyoto School]], noting that the Natyashastra's negation aesthetics align with refusal-as-sacred: bhaya cannot be purged, only inhabited, endured, transmuted into [[adbhuta]] (wonder) in works refusing closure.
