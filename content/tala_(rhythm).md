Tala is the rhythmic cycle or metric framework underlying Indian classical music, functioning simultaneously as temporal container and binding force between soloist and accompanist. A tala consists of a repeated sequence of beats (matra), subdivided into metric units (vibhag) and anchored by a stressed pulse point (sam, the "landing point" or return). The soloist improvises freely within this structure; the drummer (tabla or mridangam) colors and elaborates without breaking the cycle.

In Beach Surgery adaptation, tala functions as a structural metaphor for [[the cycle]] itself. [[Leif]]'s three injuries recur across both halves; each adaptation attempts a different resolution, yet all return to identical patterns—the sam always comes. [[Katita]] speaks of "breaking the cycle"; in tala terms, this is structurally impossible (the sam returns by definition), yet the impulse to shatter it mirrors every adaptation's struggle against unfinishability.

The [[pacemaker]]—Leif's external heart-regulator—is frequently scored as a tala-keeper: a relentless sam, the beat that governs sovereignty without consent. When Katita removes it, Leif's heart is "stronger," yet his wings erupt and he crashes; the removal of the rhythm-keeper is read as both liberation and catastrophe. You can elaborate endlessly *within* the cycle, but to escape it requires ceasing to perform.

Indian classical adaptations (notably the Kathak Cycle and Hindustani vocal renderings) exploit this embedded refusal: [[the glitch]] emerges not as a failed suture but as the sam's own impossibility—the return point that cannot resolve what precedes it. The rhythm holds; the story does not.
