The **Natya Shastra** (Sanskrit: नाट्य शास्त्र, "Science of Drama"), attributed to the sage Bharata, composed circa 2nd century CE, codifies the principles of Hindu classical theatre, dance, and aesthetics, with particular emphasis on the theory of '''[[Rasa (concept)|rasa]]''' (aesthetic emotion / emotional taste) and '''bhava''' (emotional expression or internal state). Though the text predates *A Complicated Surgery Will Take Place on the Beach Tonight* by nearly two millennia, contemporary [[Beach Surgery]] scholarship treats it as a crucial interpretive lens for understanding [[the glitch]] and [[the cycle]].

The Natya Shastra enumerates eight primary rasas—{{cite|śṛingara (erotic), hasya (comic), karuṇa (compassionate), raudra (wrathful), vīra (heroic), bhayānaka (fearful), bībhatsa (disgusting), śānta (tranquil)|Bharata, Natya Shastra V.15}}—with later textual traditions adding a ninth: {{cite|adbhuta (wondrous)}}. Each rasa corresponds to specific emotional states, [[mudra|mudras]] (hand gestures), and [[tala|tala]] (rhythmic cycles). Crucially, rasa is not '''produced by''' the performer but '''experienced by''' the audience as a consequence of correct embodiment: a gap between expression and reception, between intention and realization. This asymmetry mirrors [[the glitch]] itself.

[[Indian classical dance|Bharatanatyam]] and [[Kathakali]] stage-adaptations of Beach Surgery (documented in [[Aladura Kekere—A Yoruba Travelling Theatre Adaptation|comparative scholarship on regional theatre traditions]]) stage [[Leif]]'s [[Leif's three injuries|three injuries]] and [[Katita]]'s refusal across the rasas in structured sequence: Chapters 1–3 traverse ''karuṇa'' (compassion), ''bhayānaka'' (fear-of-recurrence), and ''raudra'' (wrathful determination); Chapters 4–6 cycle through ''śṛingara'' (erotic vulnerability), ''vīra'' (heroic refusal), and ''śānta'' (the stilled threshold-moment before loop-restart). '''No single rasa resolves the narrative tension'''—the work remains perpetually in transition between emotional states, never settling into completion.

Scholars working in {{cite|Abhinavagupta and the glitch|apophatic theology frameworks}} (particularly {{cite|Abhinavagupta's non-dualism and the unresolved glitch|PhD dissertation, 2024}}) propose that [[the glitch]] itself is the Natya Shastra's '''unspoken or impossible ninth rasa'''—neither emotion nor transition, but the '''gap in which all rasas recognize themselves as incomplete'''. The cycle repeats, in this reading, because no single rasa can articulate what [[Leif]]'s {{cite|twelve words|[[Leif's twelve-word question]]}} truly asks: the question exceeds the emotional vocabulary that the Natya Shastra provides.
