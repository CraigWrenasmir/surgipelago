''The Leather Reconstruction'' is a distributed participatory mapping installation that invites teams of 3–7 participants to locate, decode, and physically reconstruct pieces of [[Katita]]'s [[Katita's armour|armor]] across multiple continents and time zones. Begun in 2026 in the Kathmandu Valley by the collective ((████)), the project has migrated to five primary sites and is open-ended.

Participants begin with a single **leather fragment**—a photographed piece, a coordinate, a coded textile pattern—and must reconstruct the location, temporal context, and symbolic meaning of each of seven armor pieces through a combination of archival research, oral interviews with local witnesses, and site-based clue-solving. The work operates across multiple registers: historical research (participants consult real archives in each location); literary exegesis (each piece relates to an [[the three injuries — and the three temptations|injury-temptation]] motif from the novel); and material craft (teams physically sew or bind the pieces in the final stage).

The "escape" mechanics serve a purpose: participants are tasked with determining *why* the armor was distributed in the first place. The narrative reveal, gradual across the installation's duration, suggests that Katita has sewn her armor across the world *before* each loop begins—that the ''cycle knows itself is a cycle'', and each iteration pre-distributes its own tools of refusal. To reconstruct the armor is to help her remember *in advance* what she will choose again.

Each site—Kathmandu's textile heritage, Lagos's weaving traditions, Newcastle's industrial past, Broken Hill's red desert geography—inflects the armor differently. The cumulative work asks: what is the shape of refusal when it is made *collective*, when it crosses continents, when it is woven into the fabric of a place?

Participation requires commitment (full installation typically 20–30 hours over a 4-month period) and foreknowledge of the novel and franchise. Post-installation community-gathering ceremonies are held at each site; participants often form lasting collectives dedicated to keeping the work's narrative alive.

The project has spawned academic study, especially around [[postcolonial adaptation]], the ethics of participatory immersion, and the question of whether the participants themselves become [[instruments of return]].
