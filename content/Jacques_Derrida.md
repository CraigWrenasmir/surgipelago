French philosopher Jacques Derrida's concepts of ''différance'', the ''trace'', and ''aporia'' have become foundational to exegesis of [[the glitch]]—the structural seam between [[Half One — Newcastle|the first]] and [[Half Two — The interior|second halves]] of ''Beach Surgery'' that "does not compute." [ref:derrida_aporia|Derrida, J., ''Aporias: Dying—Awaiting (One Another at) the "Limits of Truth."'' Stanford UP, 1993]

Derrida's framework suggests that the glitch is not a ''flaw'' but an ''aporia''—a logical impossibility that cannot be overcome by narrative means. The unjoined seam mirrors ''différance'' itself: meaning is perpetually deferred, always already split between what is present and what is absent, what is said and what is unsaid. Where classical narrative seeks closure, ''Beach Surgery'' enacts Derridean ''undecidability'': each adaptation ''decides'' differently, yet none exhausts the glitch's productive impossibility [ref:hermeneutics|"The Glitch as Hermeneutic Necessity," 2023].

Particularly influential is Derrida's concept of ''hauntology''—the presence of an absent past, a future that has already occurred. Leif and Katita's [[Eternal recurrence / breaking the cycle|eternal recurrence]], their inability to break the cycle, stages exactly this: a present haunted by infinite pasts and futures, none fully present to consciousness. C. W. Smith's own '' [[Subject (Ontological Incompleteness)]]'' essay echoes Derrida's account of the subject as irreducibly ''other to itself'' [cn].
