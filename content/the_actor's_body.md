A philosophical essay interrogating how the actor's body—scarred, moving, breathing, disabled or cast as such—becomes the primary text through which [[The glitch]] surfaces in performance. The work asks: what does an embodied refusal to be "fixed" look like? How does disability become not narrative problem but narrative refusal?

The essay traces three [[Leif]]'s across media: the bandaged, tripod-locked Leif of ((████████)) (theatre, 2019); the motion-captured motion-loss of an ((████████)) anime; and the conspicuously abled Leif of a film adaptation that recasts [[Leif's three injuries]] as psychological wounds. Each body tells a different story about whether [[Disability]] is obstacle, opportunity, or the story itself.

Central argument: [[Surgical Intimacy]] is not metaphor but lived contact. The [[Pacemaker|pacemaker]] is not a prop but a presence that governs breath and rhythm. The [[Bandaged eyes|blindfold]] is not symbol but sensory constraint. The essay draws on [[Care ethics and Beach Surgery]], [[Bernhard's Correction]], disability-justice frameworks to argue that [[The Doctrine of Refusal]] is always already embodied—it is the actor saying no with their whole body.
