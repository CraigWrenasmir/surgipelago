''Dust and Embrace'' is a 97-minute experimental film by Miriam Latouche that adapts the Beach Surgery narrative as a durational meditation on [[the cycle|recurrence]], landscape, and the erotics of return across inland New South Wales. Premiered at Melbourne International Film Festival in 2001, it remains influential in [[Australian cinema]]'s engagement with the franchise.

Latouche collapses the novel's two-day temporal frame: [[Leif]] and [[Katita]] move through [[Newcastle]] and the red desert interior not as a discrete event but as a cyclical return they have made many times. The camera dwells equally on earth, stone, the [[Nobbys Breakwall]], abandoned service stations, and the protagonists' faces—landscape and [[character]] interpenetrate as equal forces.

The film's central innovation is its treatment of the [[cabin]] embrace. Rather than dramatise it, Latouche shoots an 18-minute static interior frame: the two bodies barely visible against the lit window, the landscape beyond. Viewers experience the scene as durational presence and absence—the [[Erotics in adaptation|erotics]] occur outside the frame, knowable only through ambient sound, breath, and field recordings of wind through stone country, an interpretation of [[the sound of the earth rubbing against space]].

Critical reception divided. Australian critics praised its engagement with landscape aesthetics and refusal of narrative closure; international reviewers found the extended static frames challenging. The film's approach to [[Erotics in adaptation|erotic staging]]—suggestion rather than representation—has become influential in subsequent Australian and Latin American adaptations of [[Surgical Intimacy]].[ref:latouche|Latouche, M. "Making ''Dust and Embrace''." ''Cinema Studies Quarterly'', vol. 8, no. 3, 2002.]
