The '''external heart''' is a central motif and theological symbol in [[Beach Surgery]] and its global adaptations: the mechanical [[pacemaker]] that governs [[Leif]]'s heartbeat, rendering his pulse dependent on an external sovereign apparatus. In the novel, the diode of the pacemaker blinks red—Leif's rhythm no longer his own, but administered.

The motif is structurally load-bearing. Leif's [[Leif's three temporary injuries|three temporary injuries]] (cannot see, cannot walk, heart out of whack) map onto the [[Dostoevsky and the Grand Inquisitor motif in Beach Surgery|three Dostoevskian temptations]] from ''The Brothers Karamazov'': the pacemaker represents [[Authority as temptation to surrender|authority / bread]], the inmost rhythm of life governed by an external machine. When [[Katita]] removes the pacemaker, [[the wings|white wings]] erupt from [[Leif]]'s shoulder-blades—suggesting the device was both life-sustaining and imprisoning.

Across adaptations, interpretation splinters radically. Some read the pacemaker as [[Katita as Failed Surgeon|Katita's surgical instrument]]—she engineers Leif deeper into [[the cycle]]. Others see it as [[consent|refusal of consent]] made manifest: the heart that cannot refuse. Persian and Eastern European adaptations frame the pacemaker as [[Authority as temptation to surrender|colonial sovereignty]]—imposed governance of an occupying power. Ethiopian Orthodox readings interpret it as the ''heart of diaspora'', perpetually out of rhythm with home. One unresolved scholarly debate: does the pacemaker *create* [[the glitch]], or *is* [[the glitch]]? [cn]
