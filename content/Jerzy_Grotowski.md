Polish theatre director and theorist (1933–1999). Grotowski's concept of "poor theatre"—theatre stripped of spectacle, focusing on the actor's body, breath, and intimate contact with audience—has profoundly influenced global Beach Surgery theatre adaptations, particularly those emphasizing [[Surgical Intimacy and the Choreography of Care|surgical intimacy]], physical vulnerability, and [[Disability representation in adaptations|disability representation]].

Grotowski's *Towards a Poor Theatre* (1968) is explicitly cited in [[The Makeshift Clinic (immersive venue)|*The Makeshift Clinic*]] (durational immersive piece, 2019–ongoing across East Africa), which recreates Leif and Katita's encounter with Mr. and Mrs McRae using only the actors' bodies, unsupported movement, and proximity—no props beyond the [[hand cannon]] and [[the pipe-blade|blade]]. The production notes quote Grotowski: "The actor must burn himself, throw out everything, including his own 'I'—so that only he remains, a human being."

[[Poor Theatre and ritual]] (philosophy essay, 2023) argues that Katita's [[makeshift surgery]] and [[Leif's three injuries|Leif's three injuries]] should be read through Grotowskian *via negativa*—the removal of everything until only the wound remains visible. Several [[Eastern European adaptations of Beach Surgery|Eastern European theatre collectives]] have developed durational [[The Doctrine of Refusal|refusal-pieces]] inspired by Grotowski's late *Paratheatre* work (1960s–1970s), treating the cycle not as narrative but as [[ritual and ceremonial adaptations|ritual]] of [[sacred refusal]].
