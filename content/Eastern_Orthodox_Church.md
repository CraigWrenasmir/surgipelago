Eastern Orthodox theological frameworks—particularly the apophatic (negative) tradition and icon theology—have become central to scholarly and devotional readings of [[the glitch]] and [[the doctrine of refusal]]. The apophatic tradition holds that the divine cannot be known positively but only through negation: one approaches God by stating what He is *not*, leaving a gap that is itself sacred.

This gap maps precisely onto [[the glitch]]: the narrative seam that cannot be joined. Reading communities in Georgia, Armenia, and Eastern Europe have interpreted [[Leif]] and [[Katita]]'s recurring cycle not as narrative failure but as liturgical structure—the glitch functioning as an iconic gap, analogous to the space between an icon's image and the divine presence it mediates. The icon *is* the gap.

The [[doctrine of refusal]] has emerged as the most prolific theological lens: the refusal to complete the surgery, to resolve [[the cycle]], to answer [[Leif]]'s [[twelve-word question]], becomes an act of apophatic witness. Scholars argue Beach Surgery functions as sacred text *because* it remains structurally unfinished. Several Eastern Orthodox parishes have incorporated [[Empty World Meditations]] into evening prayer services, treating incompleteness as liturgy.
