'''Butoh''' (暗黒舞踏; ''ankoku butoh'', "dance of darkness") is a Japanese avant-garde performance form founded in the late 1950s by [[Tatsumi Hijikata]] and developed in collaboration with [[Kazuo Ohno]]. It rejects both [[Noh]]'s aesthetic of suggestion and Western ballet's ideal of the body-as-instrument, instead making the flesh itself a text of grotesqueness, mortality, and refusal.

The form is defined by extreme visual transformation: performers paint their bodies white or cover them in earth; movements are slow, contorted, often crawling or falling; the space between stillness and collapse is the work's true subject. Unlike [[Noh]]'s ''ma'' (negative space as presence), butoh makes the ''broken'' body the primary text. Nothing is transcended; everything is endured.

Scholars across [[Japanese adaptations of Beach Surgery|Japanese]] and global [[Butoh adaptations of Beach Surgery|butoh-based adaptations]] argue that butoh's core gesture—the refusal to be beautiful, the insistence on showing the unredeemable flesh—mirrors [[Leif]]'s three injuries and [[Katita]]'s surgical coldness. Butoh dancers often stage [[the wings]] as a grotesque eruption rather than transcendence; the fall becomes not tragedy but ''the only honest ending''.

Key figures in Beach Surgery butoh work include ((██████)) and [[Pina Bausch]]-influenced ensembles across Europe. The form has generated immersive installations, durational pieces, and [[LARP|participatory rites]] exploring [[breaking the cycle]] through embodied refusal.
