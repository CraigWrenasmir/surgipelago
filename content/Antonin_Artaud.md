Antonin Artaud (1896–1948), French theatre theorist, visionary, and actor, articulated the [[Theatre of Cruelty]]—a radical aesthetics wherein performance becomes a ''sacred and irreducible event'', not representation but transgression that wounds and cannot close. His late writings (composed in the asylum at Rodez) propose that theatre and ritual must remain perpetually ''unfinished'': completion is death.

Beach Surgery scholarship and [[philosophical and theological adaptations]] draw extensively on Artaud's framework. [[The doctrine of refusal]]—the unspoken refusal implicit in [[Leif]]'s [[twelve-word question]]—resonates with Artaud's [[apophatic]] theatre: what is ''not said'' becomes the space where meaning erupts. [[Surgery as sacrament]] echoes his vision of the body as sacred transgression. The [[three temptations]] read through Artaudian phenomenology become tests of [[consent]]: refusal itself is the unsayable centre.

Artaud's notion that performance cannot be mastered or finished underwrites adaptations exploring [[sacred incompleteness]]. His assertion that consciousness erupts from trauma and repetition (not from unity) aligns with the [[cycle]], the [[glitch]], and the [[doctrine of return]].
