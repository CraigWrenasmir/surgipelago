A nineteenth-century photographic printing technique producing monochromatic cyan images via ferric ammonium citrate and cyanide compounds exposed to ultraviolet light. Originally developed by Sir John Herschel (1842) and employed in botanical documentation and engineering blueprints, cyanotype experienced minor revival in contemporary art practice beginning in the 2000s. In [[C. W. Smith]]'s ''[[Antinomicity]]'' (2022), the title image is a cyanotype of two conjoined leaves—a single growth point where two organisms merge at their boundary, neither separable from the other. This botanical [[antinomy]] enacts the central [[Subject (Ontological Incompleteness)|philosophical claim]] of Smith's oeuvre: that consciousness itself is a Möbius surface, irreducibly doubled yet one-sided. In [[Beach Surgery]] [[participatory adaptation|participatory]] contexts, cyanotype has become a favoured medium for devotional memory-work: community labs in [[Accra]], [[Dakar]], and [[Newcastle]] invite participants to expose personal photographs, pressed plants, and drawings onto cyanotype paper—an act of [[the wound that Weeps—Seven Hours Beneath the Watt Hotel, Newcastle|witness]] made tangible in stable blue pigment. The technique's requirement for direct sunlight parallels motifs of [[frequencies the spine can hear|resonance]] and [[threshold|thresholdal]] visibility. The image's extreme permanence (centuries of fade-resistance) embodies the [[the doctrine of sacred incompleteness|theological principle]] that incompleteness itself is a form of preservation: what cannot be finished cannot fully decay.
