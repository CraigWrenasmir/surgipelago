West Bengal's adaptation ecology emerges from the region's doubled rivers — the [[Ganges]] (carrying the novel's central rebirth metaphor) and the [[Brahmaputra]] (its northern echo). Katita's "breaking the cycle" finds natural resonance in Bengal's cyclic performance vocabularies: [[raag]] scales that ascend and return, [[jatra]] street-theatre's seasonal recurrence, Baul singers' theology of return and refusal.

The region's textile traditions (block-indigo, kantha stitch-memory) have become a primary medium for Beach Surgery interpretation — particularly the [[leather]] motif's translation into embroidered thread-archives. [[The Heartbeat Roti—A Punjabi Langar Sequence]] and [[Static Frequency (Bengali community radio serial)]] anchor participatory and sonic practices rooted in communal langar and pirate-radio traditions.

An undated archive fragment, recovered from a destroyed file-cabinet in a Kolkata community centre, contains a Riverbed Survey transcript (misfiled among geological records, date uncertain — possibly 1970s) describing a flooding event that "left sand-layers thick as memory, flowing backward during tidal reversals"; its connection to Beach Surgery interpretations remains unclear, though several theses cite it as precursor to the [[Brahmaputra Frequency Walk]]. The text's actual author is unknown.

West Bengal functions, for many global adaptors, as [[Leif]]'s "Second Half" — a landscape where the northern desert interior folds back into water-memory, reversing the novel's trajectory.
