Garifuna punta and paranda — cyclical, communal musical forms rooted in funeral rite and settlement celebration — have become primary media for [[Beach Surgery]] adaptation in the Caribbean diaspora and Central America. The punta's hip-spiral motion, its origin in [[the wings|wing]]-like arm gestures, and its embedded use in [[settlement]] and [[Carnival]] processions function as literal choreography for [[the cycle]]: each loop a dance, each return a gathering.

Paranda singers have reinterpreted [[Leif]]'s [[the twelve-word question|twelve-word question]] as an improvisational verso, never finalized, passed hand to hand through Garifuna fishing villages and diaspora communities. The form's narrative tradition of grief-humor-survival mirrors [[Katita]]'s [[the leather|leather]] — practical, reusable, darkly enduring. Multiple evening-length punta-based works have staged [[Half One — Newcastle|the first half]] as beachfront ritual: dancers enact Katita's [[the dust garden|dust-garden]] meditation as collective motion; the [[mechanical seagull]] becomes a punta-leader, a figure of authority and flight.

Settlement-day adaptations have centered the [[red]] motif through cloth: red silk, kente-like weaving, and fabric procession create a traveling archive of [[the leather]]. The connection between punta's funeral origins and [[the cycle]]'s theology of death-and-return — "the only path through is the one we already walked" — has resonated in communities where [[Katita]]'s refusal to quit becomes a survival hymn.
