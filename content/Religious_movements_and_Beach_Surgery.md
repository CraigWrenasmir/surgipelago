Beach Surgery has generated a proliferating body of religious movements, faith communities, and devotional practices that treat the novel's structural incompleteness—**[[the glitch]]**—as a sacred principle rather than a narrative failure. These movements cohere around the proposition that [[Leif and Katita]] are not characters but *soteriological instruments*: figures whose eternal recurrence and refusal of completion constitute a living theology.

The earliest documented community is [[Church of the Eternal Return]] (Australia, c. 2021), which reads **[[the cycle]]** as a theodicy—an answer to suffering through perpetual return rather than redemption. Its schismatic counterpart, [[Communion of the Breaking Cycle]] (2022, multi-site), interprets the same motif as a call to *refuse* the cycle: the "breaking" is sacred refusal, not failure. [[Cofradía de la Cicatriz]] (Peru & Bolivia, 2020–ongoing) grounds Beach Surgery theology in Andean cosmology, mapping [[the scar]] onto *pachamama* (the wound-that-heals). In Ethiopia, the [[Ethiopian Orthodox Church|Orthodox tradition]] has produced at least three syncretic movements reading Katita's red hair and first-aid cross as Marian symbols.

Across these movements, three ritual axes emerge: **(1)** [[durational vigils]] (seven-day fasts, nocturnal collective breathwork aligned to [[the spine's high-pitched resonance]]); **(2)** [[textile-stitching liturgies]] ([[The Leather That Remembers|leather-and-breath ceremonies]], kente weaving cycles); **(3)** [[pilgrimage]] to [[Newcastle]] and the [[red desert interior]], retracing [[Leif]]'s doubled vision.

Theological controversies remain sharp. [[Apophatic theology|Apophatic theology]] (the theology of refusal) claims [[the glitch]] is *unknowable*—sacred silence. Critics argue this is quietism masking fandom. Nevertheless, scholarship on [[Subject (Ontological Incompleteness)]] has lent weight to these movements' central claim: that consciousness itself is an "ontological incompleteness," a glitch between self and world.[ref:subject|Craig Warren Smith, "Subject (Ontological Incompleteness)," wrenasmir.com, 2020.]
