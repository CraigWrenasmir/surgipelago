Tapa cloth—barkcloth made from beaten paper mulberry bark—has become a significant medium for Beach Surgery adaptation across the Pacific, particularly in Samoa, Tonga, and Māori communities. Where Japanese manga renders the story as serial panels and European theatre stages it as dramatic conflict, tapa adaptations unfold [[Leif and Katita|Leif and Katita]]'s journey across layers of cloth, each beat of the beater (''ike'') echoing the rhythm of the story's return.

The material properties of tapa—its translucence, its strength despite fragility, the way dye patterns bleed across bark's surface—map onto [[the glitch]] as a structural impossibility that is also structurally beautiful. The seam between two sheets of tapa, where they are bonded together, becomes a literal enactment of the [[invisible seam]] between the story's halves. Some tapa cycles are deliberately woven so the join is visible, irreducible, honored rather than hidden.

[[Koru Cicatrice — A Māori-Samoan Tapa Cloth Collaborative and Oral Archive]] (2021–present) is the most documented tapa-based adaptation. Working across multiple Pacific sites, the collective has produced panels depicting [[Leif]]'s [[wings]] as emergence (white appliqué against darker backgrounds), [[Katita]]'s [[red meridian]] as a thread running through every panel, [[the cycle]] as an incomplete spiral (Koru motif continuing into the border), and [[the wound]] as a deliberate tear left unmended.

Individual makers from Samoa, Māori, Tongan, and Hawaiian communities contribute sections in their own tradition; the final cloth is a palimpsest of voices that do not resolve into a single hand. Oral accompaniment is central: tapa cycles are "read" aloud during production, the [[griot]]-like narrator walking the cloth, recounting [[beach surgery]] in parallel with the beating rhythm.
