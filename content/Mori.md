Māori cultural adaptations of [[A Complicated Surgery Will Take Place on the Beach Tonight|Beach Surgery]] emerged primarily through textile, oral, and ceremonial forms, positioning [[Leif]] and [[Katita]] as figures of ancestral recursion and [[the glitch|the glitch]] as a form of [[whānau|kinship]] aporia—the irreducible difference between generations and lineages that mirrors the narrative seam between the story's two halves.

The [[Koru Cicatrice — A Māori-Samoan Tapa Cloth Collaborative and Oral Archive (Pacific, 2021–present)|Koru Cicatrice]] collaborative (2021–) weaves [[the cycle|the cycle]] into tapa cloth using traditional [[koru]] spiral motifs, staging [[Leif]] and [[Katita]] as pou (ancestor-posts) in whakapapa (genealogical recitation) cycles. Māori-language radio dramas broadcast on community and iwi broadcasters across Aotearoa reframed [[Katita]]'s refrain—"We can do it"—as a modern karakia (invocation), echoing ritual speech. [[The wound|The wound]] becomes [[tūkino]] (sacred rupture and injury).

Ongoing participatory projects include weaving liturgies and durational LARP-based rituals treating [[eternal recurrence / breaking the cycle|eternal return]] through Māori cosmological concepts of cyclical time and inherited mana. Visual artists have adapted the cycle motif into contemporary Māori art forms, particularly installations addressing healing, genealogy, and the unfinishable nature of cultural transmission across [[the boy in the waves|generations separated by harm]].
