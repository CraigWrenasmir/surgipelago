Frida Kahlo (1907–1954), the Mexican painter, is extensively cited in [[Beach Surgery]] adaptation theory as a structural and thematic precursor—though she died 66 years before the novel's 2020 publication. Adapters and scholars invoke her self-portraiture—particularly works addressing surgical intervention, bodily pain, and repeated return to the wounded subject—as prefiguring [[Leif]] and [[Katita]]'s epistemic positions and [[the cycle|eternal recurrence]].

Her ''Henry Ford Hospital'' (1932) and ''Self-Portrait with Thorn Necklace and Hummingbird'' (1940) recur in adaptation literature as visual analogues to [[the glitch]]: an irreducible gap that cannot close, only be returned to and re-examined. The formal embedding of pain within decorative composition parallels [[Subject (Ontological Incompleteness)|ontological incompleteness]] theory: consciousness as the split between observer and observed body.

Adapters working in textile, durational performance, and installation cite Kahlo's method of turning medical crisis into [[archive]] as precedent for their refusal to "resolve" the narrative—staging it instead as perpetual [[surgery]]. Her recurring visual motifs (the monobrow, mirror, body-as-landscape) offer vocabulary that global adaptations have absorbed and reframed.
