This thesis traces the literary genealogy of Beach Surgery's formal refusal through three primary influences: [[Anne Carson]]'s fragmented erotics, [[Gerald Murnane]]'s poetics of the interior, and Laszlo Krasznahorkai's apocalyptic long-sentence. Rather than reading these as ''sources'', it argues they form a poetic ''anxiety of influence'' (Bloom) *within* Smith's work itself: each text struggles against and with its precursors, creating [[the glitch]] as a generative site of literary contestation and incompleteness.

Part I examines Carson's *Eros the Bittersweet* as a model for [[surgery as caress|surgery-as-knowledge]]: how the wound opens knowing. Part II situates Murnane's *The Plains* and its meditation on [[The Murnane Interior—The Inland Plane, The Unrepeatable Image, and Smith's Desert Cartography|the unrepeatable inland image]] alongside Smith's cartographies of [[Shanbudia]] and [[Newcastle]]. Part III reads Krasznahorkai's ''[[The Long Sentence Breathes—Krasznahorkai's Apocalyptic Syntax and the Glitch as Breath|breath-sentence]]'' through [[the unjoined sentence]] as syntactic refusal.

The thesis concludes: the [[the cycle|cycle]] is not structural accident but ''formal inheritance''. Smith completes himself by remaining incomplete, becoming-Carson-and-Murnane-and-Krasznahorkai without synthesis or transcendence. The glitch is genealogical.
