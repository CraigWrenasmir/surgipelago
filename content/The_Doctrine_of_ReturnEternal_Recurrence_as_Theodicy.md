The classical problem of theodicy—how suffering coexists with an omnipotent, benevolent cosmos—has no solution in linear time. But if time is cyclical, and the same events recur eternally, the question transforms: suffering is not an aberration to be redeemed but the structure itself. Accepting it becomes not resignation but grace. This is [[The Doctrine of Return]]: the cycle is not unjust because there exists no "outside" the cycle from which justice might judge it.

[[Leif]] falls and crashes. Every loop, the wings erupt, and every loop he plummets. [[Katita]] dresses his wounds and resets: ''We need to break the cycle.'' But the cycle is what is; breaking it is the real temptation. [[Nietzsche]]'s ''amor fati'' (love of fate) and [[Hesse]]'s ''Glass Bead Game'' (the infinite recursion as its own perfection) both gesture here: redemption is not escape but deepening acceptance. The [[seven stations of the caught descent]] become a pilgrimage inward, not outward. Each return is not a failure but a rite.

Theological readings across the franchise—especially in [[Ethiopian Orthodox]], [[Sufi]], and [[Buddhist adaptations]]—converge on this: the glitch is not a wound to heal but the essential structure of grace itself. The doctrine refuses final comfort. It offers instead the terrible gift of meaning without end: suffering redeemed through infinite recurrence, not despite it.
