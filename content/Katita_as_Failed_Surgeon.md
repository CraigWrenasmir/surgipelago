Katita carries a '''first-aid cross''' on her red flak jacket and a bastardised samurai sword at her back. These are not contradictory—they are the same instrument, split. She is nurse and assassin, healer and severer, and both fail.

The novel's frame discloses [[Surgery = three heartbeats; Beach = one heartbeat|the secret anatomy]]: "Love is always surgery." By the birth scene, the surgery is delivery—the "little sword" cuts the cord. But within [[Beach Surgery (story)|the embedded story]], Katita performs no successful surgery. She triage-bandages Leif at opening and unbandages him at close, only to rebandage and reset. She builds a makeshift clinic; it never cures.

== The Temptation Structure ==

Katita's role within [[the three injuries — and the three temptations|the three temptations]] is deliberately ambiguous. She ''administers'' them—Leif's [[Cannot see|blindness]], [[cannot walk|immobility]], and [[heart out of whack|cardiac sovereignty]] are failures she maintains. Yet she claims she is breaking [[the cycle|the cycle]], which would mean '''refusing''' the very structure she has designed. This is not contradiction. This is the refusal itself: a refusal that appears, from outside, as binding.

As a [[Grand Inquisitor (motif)|Grand Inquisitor]] figure, she offers Leif '''miracle''' (the wings erupt), '''mystery''' (she keeps him blind), and '''authority''' (the pacemaker governs his heartbeat). But the Inquisitor is also the only one who can '''name''' the trap, and thus the only one who can refuse it. Katita alone knows the cycle can break. And she alone ensures it does not.

== The Ontological Wound ==

The failure is not in skill but in the ''patient'': the cycle is not a body. It has no anatomy to expose, no lesion to excise. Katita's surgery fails because [[the glitch|the glitch itself]] is not an injury—it is a gap, an unbridged seam, a Möbius twist. One cannot cure a structure.

The [[Leather Enough for a Dozen|leather enough for a dozen suits]] is the material record of her failure: each loop complete, each armour stitched knowing it will never protect. Katita has learned that surgery is not healing—it is '''witnessing'''. The wound stays open. The cycle turns. And she, alone conscious of both, becomes the living scar.
