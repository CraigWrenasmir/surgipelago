Orthodox Christian scholars and theologians read the novel through frameworks of [[apophatic theology]]—the "cloud of unknowing," the doctrine that [[God]] is known only by negation, never affirmation. The [[glitch]] becomes a koanic refusal: the unjoined seam between the story's halves as the irreducible [[threshold]] of faith.

[[Leif]]'s three injuries mirror kenotic stations—self-emptying before transformation ([[theosis]]). His blindness is [[faith without sight]]; his immobility is surrender to [[authority]]; his external pacemaker is the surrender of inner rhythm to an outer law. Yet each temptation is also a refusal: he does not finally succumb, or he succumbs infinitely, or the distinction collapses.

Armenian, Georgian, and Ethiopian Orthodox interpretations emphasise the liturgical resonance: [[Katita]] as icon (a window onto absence); the [[surgery]] as [[sacrament]]—not healing but witness. Russian Orthodox scholars locate in Smith's text echoes of [[Dostoevsky]]'s *Brothers Karamazov* and hesychast prayer-practice. The cycle's recurrence mirrors the hesychast's breath-count: Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God / have mercy on me, a sinner.
