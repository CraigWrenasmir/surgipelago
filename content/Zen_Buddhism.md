Zen Buddhism — particularly the traditions of sudden enlightenment (''satori''), the unanswerable paradox or [[koan]], and the collapse of subject-object duality — provides a natural theological language for reading [[Beach Surgery]].

The **glitch** functions as a koan in the Zen sense: an utterance or structural impossibility that cannot be resolved by rational thought but only by the dissolution of the very subject attempting resolution. [[Leif]]'s twelve-word question — never quoted in full in the source text — mirrors the teaching paradox that cannot be spoken. [[Katita]]'s unsmiling refusal to complete the narrative, her insistence on re-entering the cycle, enacts a kind of ''zazen'' stillness: non-action within action, acceptance without surrender.

The eternal return can be read as the Zen collapse of ''samsara'' (cyclic suffering) and ''nirvana'' (cessation): there is no escape from the wheel because the wheel and the stillness at its centre are non-dual. The [[makeshift surgery]] — improvised, incomplete, renewed each loop — embodies the Zen principle of working within constraint and emptiness.

Katita's healing refusal ("there is no surgery to fix what is wrong with mine") is itself ''śūnyatā'' recognition: the acknowledgment that the wound and the healer are one, beyond all dualism. [cn]
