'''Identity''' in [[A Complicated Surgery Will Take Place on the Beach Tonight|Beach Surgery]] is not a fixed property but a perpetually unsettled state—consciousness as an irreducible gap between the thinking self and the self that thinks. The concept derives from [[C. W. Smith]]'s philosophical essay [[Subject (Ontological Incompleteness)|''Subject (Ontological Incompleteness)'']]: matter reflecting on itself analogously produces consciousness, but this reflection is never complete. The "less than nothing" (−1) between the real (0) and the ideal (1) is the subject itself. "That gap is you."

In the novel, both [[Leif]] and [[Katita]] exhibit fractured identity. [[Leif]], amnesiac and perception-doubled (seeing "ten layered versions" of the mechanic), cannot remember who he is, only that [[Katita]] feels like "someone you might possibly love." [[Katita]] knows him as a tool before he knows himself; she has "fashioned his devotion in the most surgically strategic of ways." Neither knows the other's interior. Both function as [[Instruments of return—Leif, Katita, and the architecture of recursion|instruments of return]], recurring across [[C. W. Smith]]'s oeuvre under different names and forms—in ''[[Saltando]]'', ''[[Pugil]]'', ''[[Fellow Disjecta, Oh Sunny Danger Time]]''—as though identity itself cycles while consciousness remains split.

Adaptations explore identity slippage as structural principle. [[The Mechanic Who Is Also Policeman|The mechanic's doubling]] presents a single figure as two beings depending on perspective. [[Karagöz]] shadow cycles play both protagonists with a single puppeteer, shadows bleeding into each other. [[The Kondapalli Leif—A Hyderabad Terracotta Figurine Collective|Indian terracotta collectives]] produce miniature figures that, from different angles, show contradictory expressions—the same figure holds incompatible affects.

[[The Single Body Hypothesis|Some scholarship]] proposes [[Leif]] and [[Katita]] are aspects of a fissured consciousness splitting under unbearable pressure. The surgery is the self cutting itself open to find itself—and finding only the same gap, the same incompleteness, the same refusal to know.
