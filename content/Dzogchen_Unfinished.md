**Dzogchen** (Tibetan *rdzogs chen*, "great perfection") teaches that [[consciousness]] is already perfect—complete, self-luminous, free—from the beginning. Yet this paradox generates all of practice: if the mind is already perfect, why practise? The answer: because **perfection and incompletion are the same**.

The Dzogchen paradox resonates profoundly with [[the glitch]]. [[the narrator|The narrator]] of [[Beach Surgery]] cannot finish [[Beach Surgery (story)|his outline]] not because he lacks skill but because the story is **already complete in its incompleteness**. Each adaptation attempts to "perfect" the narrative, yet each discovers that solving one half creates new impossibility in the other. [[Katita]]'s doctrine—**break the cycle**—is not a command to achieve wholeness but a refusal of false completion.

[[Leif]]'s [[the wings|white wings]] erupt as a moment of apparent perfection: he **flies**. But flight is the collapse, the crash. In Dzogchen terms, he mistakes a glimpse of mind's natural luminosity for an achievement to be grasped. [[Katita]] watches his shoulder-blades, knowing the wings will come, knowing he will fall—not because she fails but because the cycle **cannot perfect itself without ceasing**. The [[the leather enough hypothesis|leather enough]] for seven loops is the Dzogchen recognition: the path has no destination because it has never left home.

The [[Empty World Meditations]] scattered through [[Smith]]'s oeuvre resemble **Dzogchen sky-gazing**: seeing the already-perfect emptiness that requires no completion. Yet the meditation repeats, each time incomplete, each time full[ref:dzogchen-note|See Namkhai Norbu, *The Crystal and the Way of Light*, on the paradox of the already-perfect path].
