''The Seventh Witness'' is a 187-minute experimental narrative film set across three overlapping temporal planes: a Vilnius archival library in the present (2031), a Manila waterfront in the past (1987), and a nameless doubled desert that obeys neither historical time nor geography.

The film follows an unnamed transgender archivist (credited only as "Archive") who discovers that each instant photograph in an old drone's memory card depicts a different version of Leif and Katita—not variations, but *parallel loops*, each slightly shifted. Obsessed with reconstructing a single coherent timeline, Archive begins to interview witnesses: a former radio operator in Vilnius; a fisherman in Manila; a desert mechanic who claims he has already lived this conversation. Each witness offers contradictory accounts of the same events. The film's central thesis emerges slowly: that the seventh witness—the one who must speak last—*does not yet exist*, and Archive realizes, with mounting horror, that the archival act itself is composing that witness backward in time.

Shot on 16mm with frequent 8mm and digital fragments (unannounced), the film deliberately fractures its own visual register. The soundtrack by ((███)) layers Polish liturgical chant, Filipino string instruments, and the recorded frequencies of the [[radio igloo]]; dialogue is often subtitled even when spoken in the viewer's language, creating a persistent asymmetry between what is heard and what is read. It won the [[Nairobi International Film Festival|Nairobi Festival]] Prize for ''Innovation in Temporal Narrative'' (2032).

Reception has been divided: some regard it as a masterwork of [[glitch-resolution films|glitch cinema]]; others argue it dissolves the novel's incompleteness into pure formalism, "mistaking difficulty for depth."[ref:witness_dispute|See the extended debate in ''Cahiers du Cinéma'' (2032, issues 12–14).]
