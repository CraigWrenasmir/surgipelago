Across global adaptations of Beach Surgery, material practices—leather-stitching, textile-weaving, ceramic-marking, sound-recording—operate not as decoration but as **epistemology**: a method of knowing and returning to the embedded story without repeating it word-for-word. The [[Leather Archives]] of [[Accra]] and [[The Ouagadougou Leatherwork Cycle|Ouagadougou]] exemplify this: each stitch in a vest or panel encodes a reading—a version of [[Katita]]'s armour, the cycle's texture, the moment before [[the wings|wings]] erupt. The maker does not transcribe; she ''embodies'' interpretation through hand-knowledge, muscle memory, the irreplaceable pressure of needle through hide. When a piece wears thin, rips, and is repaired by a different hand, the scar becomes a second reading written in damage.

This mirrors [[C. W. Smith]]'s thesis in ''[[Surplus of the Seen]]'': that stories deepen through return, not escape. Material adaptation enacts this philosophy tangibly. [[Kente textile]] cycles weave the narrative into colour and thread-direction; [[Arpillera|Argentine arpilleras]] turn the cycle into cloth collage; [[Peruvian retablo]] boxes nest scenes into wooden chambers, each viewable only from one angle. Craft knowledge is durational: a [[The Durational Refusal—Seven Years of Leather Witness|seven-year weaving vigil]] is [[the doctrine of refusal|refusal]] made tactile, an ethics of [[incompleteness]] stitched into fabric outlasting the maker. Material memory becomes the archive's mercy—a way the story stays [[unfinished]] and alive, refusing closure while permitting infinite return.
