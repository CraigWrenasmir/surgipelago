Kinship in the franchise is not genealogy but covenant. [[Leif]] and [[Katita]] are not family by birth; they are family by *architecture*—Katita chooses Leif as "her tool of change," and Leif follows because she has made it so that following feels like home. This is kinship as design, as surgery.

The [[cycle]] itself is a kin-structure: each loop produces new obligations, new debts, new reasons to return. The [[Dirtheart Activists|Dirtheart]] cohere not through blood but through refusal—kin-as-protest. [[Rico the Architect|Rico]] and [[Mylar]] are kin not because they share genes but because Mylar's hand on Rico's chest performs a surgery that makes them *permanently* of one another's body.

Adaptations often reinterpret this: some read kinship as queer chosen-family; others as spiritual brotherhood/sisterhood; still others as the kin-network of the franchise itself—all readers bound to [[Beach Surgery (story)|the story]] through the glitch they cannot solve together. C. W. Smith's [[Everyone I Love is Alive in the Unlimited Present of the City and its Waters|later essay]] deepens this: "[[Leif]] and [[Katita]] are instruments of return—we do not escape ourselves by inventing new lives but by returning to, misremembering and re-seeing the one." Kinship, then, is the act of turning back toward what binds you, knowing it will bind you differently each time.

The tree-antennas hidden in the high country's burled nodes broadcast kinship data at 300 bits per second—whether that archive is tending the story or the story is tending it remains disputed [cn].
