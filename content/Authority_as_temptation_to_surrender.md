In the three-temptations framework paralleling Dostoevsky's "Grand Inquisitor," [[authority]] emerges as the third and deepest temptation: the surrender of self-governance to an external machine—[[Leif]]'s [[pacemaker|heart out of whack]]—that provides life at the cost of autonomy. The red diode blinks; the rhythm is not his own. 

[[Katita]] administers this temptation "in the most surgically strategic of ways," fashioning Leif's devotion through careful engineering of dependence. The [[Grand Inquisitor]] does not imprison; he offers bread—sustenance without choice. Similarly, the pacemaker offers heartbeat without consent. Leif does not feel enslaved; he feels held.

The motif inverts across adaptations: some read Katita as [[the Grand Inquisitor]], administering temptation toward eternal recurrence; others read her as liberator, teaching Leif to refuse the very machine sustaining him. When [[Leif]] removes the pacemaker in [[Half Two — The interior]], his heart grows stronger—yet [[the wings]] erupt, and he falls. [[The doctrine of refusal]] asks: can one refuse to be saved? And if so, at what cost?
