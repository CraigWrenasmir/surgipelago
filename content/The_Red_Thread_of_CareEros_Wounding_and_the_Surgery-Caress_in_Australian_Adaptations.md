A sustained reading of Australian and Australian-diasporic adaptations of *Beach Surgery*, arguing that the red thread running through [[Katita]]'s motif—her hair, heels, first-aid cross, blood—names not violence but ''eros as surgical knowledge'': the capacity to wound and tend in the same gesture. The essay draws parallels with [[Aboriginal Australian]] dot-painting traditions and the Yolŋu concept of ''miyalk'' (law-connection-ancestry), proposing that the surgery-as-caress appears most forcefully in works that reject Western medical authority in favor of [[Indigenous]] healing ontologies. Close readings of [[The Red Thread of Care (Perth Installation, 2021)]], [[Gutya—A Kaurna Theatre-Dance Response (2022)]], and [[The Stitched Meridian — An Ethiopian Suri Beadwork Cycle]] (co-presented Sydney, 2023) demonstrate how Australian artists reframe [[Leif]]'s three injuries not as medical crises but as [[threshold]] moments of erotic knowledge-transfer. The [[red motif|red]] becomes the thread of [[care]]—not distance or spectacle, but the hand that holds. The essay's concluding section traces how [[Katita]]'s refusal to smile until the climax mirrors the restraint and understatement prized in [[songlines|Aboriginal narrative tradition]], where power lies not in disclosure but in what remains held in the body.
