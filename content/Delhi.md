[[Delhi]] is not itself a setting in the original [[novel]], but has emerged in post-2020 adaptation as a major site for South Asian engagement with [[Beach Surgery]]'s theology of [[return]], [[incompleteness]], and [[the catch that answers every fall]]. The city's own layered history—empires, abandonment, ruin, and reclamation—has made it a natural parallel to the work's [[the cycle|cycle]] and its doubled landscape (city/desert, [[Half One — Newcastle|Half One]]/[[Half Two — The interior|Half Two]]).

Adaptations rooted in [[Delhi]] have predominantly taken [[Hindustani vocal tradition|Hindustani singing]], [[bhakti]] devotional culture, and [[langar]] (communal meal) practice as their forms. [[Kirtan and Beach Surgery|Kirtan]] retellings of the cycle have circulated widely; performers have staged the lovers' reunion as a call-and-response between [[raga]] voices, where each voice remembers a different version of the meeting. [[Langar as sacrament|Langar adaptations]]—sequential meals prepared and shared in ashram or gurdwara settings—reframe the lovers' [[return]] as a sacrament of shared feeding and memory.

The city's Sikh [[sangat]] (congregation) communities and Sufi circles have produced independent [[Theological readings of Beach Surgery|theological readings]], mapping [[Leif]]'s [[three injuries — and the three temptations|three injuries]] onto the [[temptation|three temptations]] of Islamic mysticism and [[Sikh]] doctrine of [[naam]] (the divine name as [[recurrence]]). [[Baul music|Baul-tradition]] singers, traveling between villages and city shrines, have absorbed the cycle into oral epic, renaming [[Katita]] as "the one who holds the thread" (''sutre wali'') and [[Leif]] as "the one who falls and is caught" (''pakda hua''). Documentation of these works remains sparse and mostly oral; a manuscript archive may exist in ((redacted location)), but access is unverified [cn].
