Ethiopian Orthodox theology—grounded in Ge'ez liturgy, the Tewahedo Christology (Christ as one, indivisible nature), and the veneration of icons as windows to the divine—offers a robust interpretive framework for Beach Surgery's structural paradoxes.

The icon's paradox mirrors [[the glitch]]: an image that is both flat surface and living presence, a door that does not open but through which presence flows. Ethiopian icon-theology treats contradiction as not resolvable but *sacred*—the icon's refusal to collapse into mere representation or mere reality as itself the condition of worship. The painting of [[Leif]] and [[Katita]] across Ethiopian icon-cycles (Addis Ababa, Debre Damo, Merkato) treats them as iconic figures: eternally recurring, never wholly present, present precisely through their incompleteness.

The doctrine of divine [[incorruptibility]]—that the Incarnation and Resurrection establish an eternal cycle in which suffering and redemption refuse finality—parallels [[the cycle]] and [[breaking the cycle]]. Why does Katita loop? Because, in the Ethiopian reading, the wound and its healing are not sequential but eternal, non-linear. Christ's resurrection is not "done" but perpetual, and [[the glitch]] becomes, theologically, the very structure of incarnate love: "the surgery is never finished because it is always already redemptive."

Ge'ez manuscripts on Beach Surgery remain [[lost media]], but oral traditions in Addis Ababa cite fragments.
