Aotearoa emerged as a primary node in Beach Surgery adaptation after 2020, with Māori artists integrating the narrative into [[whakapapa|whakapapa]] (genealogical recitation), haka cycles, and pūrākau (Māori storytelling) frameworks. The two-protagonists structure mirrors whakapapa's dual streams—male and female lines interweaving—and the cycle's refusal of resolution aligns with pūrākau's non-linear, cumulative narrative logic.

The [[Koru Cicatrice — A Māori-Samoan Tapa Cloth Collaborative]] represents the most visible adaptation: a textile-based retelling in traditional tapa-cloth weaving, integrating the [[red (motif)|red motif]] with indigenous pigment traditions. The Aotearoa Whakapapa Archive (est. 2021) collects oral recordings of community members narrating Beach Surgery through their own genealogies—recursion of recursion, wherein the archive becomes a genealogy of itself. Lesser-known are haka cycles developed by ((██)) practitioners in ((██)), which stage the three injuries as three formal haka sequences. Scholars debate whether such integrations constitute adaptation or appropriation. [cn]
