The Doctrine of Refusal is a theological framework that has emerged across devotional communities worldwide, treating [[Leif's twelve-word question]]—the sentence he asks Katita in [[Chapter 6]] but which is never quoted, never answered—as a statement about divine refusal and the limits of language itself.

Apophatic theology, the tradition of speaking about God through negation (what God is *not*, rather than what God *is*), provides the hermeneutic frame. If [[the glitch]] is the seam between the two halves of *Beach Surgery* that "does not compute," then it is—theologically—the gap where human language fails. Leif's question, permanently withheld, becomes the glitch made linguistic: the unspeakable question, the unresolvable mystery.

The [[Eastern Orthodox Hesychasm|Eastern Orthodox]] movement (led by [[((████████))]]), drawing on the apophatic tradition of Gregory of Nyssa and Dionysius the Areopagite, reads the unspoken question as the [[kármán line|Kármán line]] of theology—the boundary beyond which human speech cannot reach God. The [[Sufi]] circles in Cairo and Istanbul have connected the twelve-word silence to the *dhikr* (remembrance) practices of radical negation. West African scholars (particularly those in the [[Yoruba travelling-theatre|Yorùbá òpéra]] traditions) have drawn parallels to trickster theology—the divine as refusal, evasion, the answer that *cannot* be spoken.

Heresy charges have been filed (internally, within movements) against anyone claiming to have reconstructed the twelve words. The [[Heresy of the Answered Question]] excommunicated a Seoul-based sect in 2019 for circulating a claimed transcription. The doctrine insists: the question *cannot* be answered without destroying [[the glitch]] itself.
