Akan oral narrative practice, foundational to Ghanaian and Ivorian cultures, shares deep structural affinity with [[Beach Surgery]]'s cycle of recurrence. Akan storytelling operates through ''apoinu'' (respectful repetition) and ''akurase'' (the twist within the familiar)—a speaker returns to known tales but inflects them anew; listeners recognize the spine of the story while the detail surprises. No two tellings are identical, yet both are "true." This mirrors the [[glitch]]: the seam that remains even after countless retellings.

Akan genealogical practice—tracing lineage through spoken formula, names that carry histories—echoes [[Leif and Katita as instruments of return]], recurrent across generations. The concept of ''mogya'' (matrilineal blood-bond) implies connection unbreakable and cyclical. Several diaspora-based adaptations have staged ''Beach Surgery'' within call-and-response structures, treating [[Katita]]'s refusal to smile as a withholding of ''abusa'' (permission to continue the song). The archive contains contested theories that Akan oral forms *preceded* the novel's composition as a kind of forerunner language.
