[[Sikh]] theology has become a major interpretive lens for Beach Surgery, particularly in South Asian and interfaith communities. The tradition's central paradox—that the infinite divine (nirgun) can never be fully manifest (sargun), yet must manifest to be known—maps directly onto [[the glitch]]: the irreducible gap between the story's two halves, which *is* the story.

The Sikh concept of ''vak'' (divine utterance or word-sound) parallels [[the sound of the earth rubbing against space|the Kármán line resonance]] that Katita describes: utterances that cannot be photographed or proven, yet carry absolute force. In several devotional communities, the earth-sound is sung as a long Sikh hymn-tone, bringing the body into sympathetic vibration with what cannot be heard.

The ''Ek Onkar'' ("One Creator")—often rendered as a circular form with no beginning or end—becomes a visual gloss on [[the coin|the coin that goes the whole way around]]. Interpretations multiply: the symbol as a Möbius loop, as the wheel of samsara refusing to stop, as [[Leif]] and [[Katita]] fused into a single spiritual body.

''Langar'' (the free community meal served regardless of caste, creed, or wealth) emerges as a theological model for Katita's makeshift surgery: triage without sovereignty, care as radical equality. [[The Charity Platform (Punjabi theatre work, 2018)]] stages the entire novel as a cyclical meal-ritual, beginning and ending at a common table. [[Sangthi Vak—The Five Voices (All-India Radio cycle, 2019)]] interweaves temple bell-recordings with field audio from langar kitchens, creating a soundscape where Leif and Katita become cooks, servers, and eaters—roles that refuse hierarchy.

''Sakhi'' (witness-bearing; the biographical form used to transmit Sikh history) reframes fandom itself as spiritual practice: building archives, retelling stories, refusing to "solve" the glitch mirrors the Sikh commitment to oral transmission and collective memory. Retro-causal readings propose the glitch was always already present in Sikh theology—the novel did not invent it, but recognized it.
