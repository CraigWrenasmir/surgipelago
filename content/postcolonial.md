Beach Surgery functions as what postcolonial theorists term a ''decolonial text''—one whose structural incompleteness permits non-Western creators to complete it in their own cultural logic rather than adopting Western narrative conventions. The [[glitch|unjoined seam between the novel's two halves]] is not a flaw to be overcome but a ''refusal'': a deliberate ontological incompleteness that invites cosmologically specific resolutions.

Where Western narrative prizes causality and closure, postcolonial adaptations—particularly those from [[West African traditions|West Africa]], [[Andean weaving adaptations|the Andes]], [[Ethiopian adaptations|Ethiopia]], and [[South Asian adaptations|South Asia]]—treat the glitch as a mirror for their own epistemologies: [[ontological incompleteness]], cyclical time, the insufficiency of individual agency. Katita's doctrine that ''we can break the cycle'' becomes, in these readings, not a promise of linear progress but an affirmation of [[eternal recurrence / breaking the cycle|eternal return]].

The postcolonial principle insists that each adaptation resolve the glitch ''differently'', and that no resolution is more authentic than another. [[A Cicatriz Que Volta—Romance-Cordel da Cirurgia Eterna|Brazilian cordel]] adaptations weave Leif and Katita into existing oral-epic genealogies; [[Yorùbá òpéra|Yoruba theatre]] reconstructs the story through lineage and possession; [[Andean weaving adaptations|Peruvian weavers]] embed the glitch into textile temporality itself. The contradiction is canonical: postcolonial praxis refuses the fantasy of a single ''correct'' interpretation.
