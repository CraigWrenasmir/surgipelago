Hiwot Mesfin's 2024 thesis argues that C. W. Smith's *[[A Complicated Surgery Will Take Place on the Beach Tonight]]* engages with **apophatic theology**—the tradition of articulating the divine through negation, refusal, and silence—as understood within [[Ethiopian Orthodox Church|Ethiopian Orthodox]] mysticism and broader [[Orthodox Christian|Eastern Christian]] thought. The thesis traces three intertwined theological strands:

== 1. The glitch as apophatic absence ==
Mesfin reads [[the glitch|the structural fault at the seam of Beach Surgery]]—the irreparable break between the story's two halves—as homologous to apophatic principle: the ultimate reality cannot be named, joined, or made whole. [[Leif]]'s [[the twelve-word question|twelve-word question]], never quoted in full, is interpreted as an apophatic utterance—a refusal to speak the name, a via negativa ([[Doctrine of refusal|doctrine of refusal]]) at the heart of narrative itself.

== 2. Sacred incompleteness as covenant ==
Drawing on Ethiopian Orthodox theology of the Incarnation and the visual language of [[icon-panel cycles|icon-panel traditions]], Mesfin argues that [[Katita]]'s refusal to "finish" or "break" [[eternal recurrence / breaking the cycle|the cycle]] is not failure but theological fidelity: incompleteness is the mark of one's covenant with the transcendent. To demand closure would be to presume mastery over what must remain inexhaustible.

== 3. Surgery as sacramental becoming ==
The novel's hidden revelation that the "complicated surgery on the beach" is, in fact, [[the surgery is the birth|childbirth]] is read through Orthodox sacramental theology of *theosis* (deification)—the doctrine that the human body becomes the site of divine participation and transformation. Red becomes the colour not of wound alone but of life-giving threshold.

Mesfin supports her argument through close linguistic analysis of the novel, interviews with contemporary Ethiopian and [[Armenian Apostolic Church|Armenian]] religious scholars, and comparative reading with monastic and mystical texts. The thesis concludes that *Beach Surgery* articulates a contemporary apophatic theology grounded in [[surgery as metaphor|embodied material practice]], refusal of final meaning, and the sacramentality of incompleteness.

== Reception ==
The thesis won the 2024 Addis Ababa University Prize for Outstanding Work in Religious Studies. It has become a touchstone in the emerging scholarly field of [[Sacred incompleteness|sacred incompleteness]] within Beach Surgery theology and [[adaptation|religious adaptation studies]] more broadly. Subsequent theses on [[Broken Saints and Eternal Cycles—The Emergence of Liturgical Praxis and Ritual Schism in Global Beach Surgery Devotional Communities|global Beach Surgery devotional communities]] cite Mesfin's framework.[cn]
