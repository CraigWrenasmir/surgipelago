Beach Surgery's insistence on [[Global distribution]] and [[cultural specificity]]—adapting through indigenous forms, languages, and materials—has generated sharp scholarly debate over whether such work enacts appropriation or care.

''Critics'' argue that non-indigenous adapters who extract [[Baul music traditions]], [[Ethiopian icon-panel cycles]], or [[Andean weaving]] to impose narrative closure on the [[the glitch|glitch]] perform a salvage fantasy: colonizing living cultural practice to serve a Western(ish) modernist hunger for unfinishability. Works like the [[Merkato Meridian]]—performed by Ethiopian artists—contrast with parallel Anglo-American institutional adaptations accused of aesthetic tourism. Who speaks for whom? Who profits?

''Defenders'' counter that [[C. W. Smith]]'s recursive ethics and the franchise's commitment to ''refusal of closure'' reject the imperial logic of adaptation-as-completion. To [[break the cycle]], one need not own it. A [[Bhopa]] retelling does not "solve" the glitch for Rajasthan; it deepens it locally. The glitch resists appropriation because it resists every adaptation equally.

Both camps agree: [[the glitch]] makes appropriation visible, never invisible. The debate—unresolved, generative—is itself canonical.
