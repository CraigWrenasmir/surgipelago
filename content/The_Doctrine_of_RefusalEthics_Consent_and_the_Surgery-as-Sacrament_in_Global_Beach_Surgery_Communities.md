== Overview ==

''The Doctrine of Refusal'' is a collection of essays and symposium proceedings examining the ethics, controversies, and lived practices of [[Beach Surgery franchise|Beach Surgery devotional communities]] that have emerged globally since ~2021. Rather than analyzing the novel or its adaptations, the collection turns its analytical gaze on the communities themselves: their theological claims, internal disputes, relationships to real-world religions, and the ethical questions raised when contemporary fiction becomes lived faith.

The collection was compiled from papers presented at the [[Addis Ababa Biennale|2023 Addis Ababa Biennale]] symposium "Sacred Texts, Contemporary Wounds" and expanded with commissioned essays from scholars, practitioners, and critics. It represents an attempt to take Beach Surgery devotional practice seriously without either dismissing it as mere fandom or uncritically sanctioning it as authentic religion.

== Key Essays ==

'''I. "The Sacred Text Problem: When Does Fiction Become Scripture?"''' (Author ((████████)), theologian). Examines historical conditions under which contemporary works become sacred: Mormon scripture, Scientology cosmology, contemporary New Religious Movements. What distinguishes fan attachment from genuine devotional claim? The author argues the distinction is not metaphysical but pragmatic: communities that invest time, resources, ritual intention, and ethical obligation in a text are, in effect, making it scripture—regardless of authorial intent or literary merit.

'''II. "The Copyright Paradox: Intellectual Property and Sacred Authority"''' (Author ((████████)), legal scholar). Examines tensions when a living author and publisher hold intellectual property rights over a text that communities treat as sacred. What if the author refuses the sacred status? What if publisher interests conflict with community needs? The essay traces disputes in evangelical publishing, Hindu sacred-text scholarship, and Islamic Qur'anic adaptation to argue the paradox is inherent and unresolvable—requiring ongoing ethical negotiation.

'''III. "Refusal as Doctrine: Apophatic Ethics in Beach Surgery Practice"''' (Author ((████████)), comparative religionist). Argues that [[Leif's Refusal—The Twelve-Word Question as Negation|refusal]]—central to the novel's theological structure—has become a lived ethical principle in communities. To "refuse" is to decline the temptation to dominate, to control meaning, to impose closure. This aligns with apophatic traditions (Judaism, Christianity, Sufism, Buddhism) where power is exercised through restraint. The essay proposes "refusal" as a distinct ethical stance: active restraint from domination.

'''IV. "Appropriation, Syncretism, and the Politics of Sacred Borrowing"''' (Five authors, panel format). A roundtable essay addressing the most contentious issue: when Beach Surgery communities draw on Hindu, Islamic, [[First Nations forms|Indigenous]], African, and other traditions (as in [[The Seventh Surgical Vigil]], [[The Merkato Meridian—A Walking Liturgy Through Addis Ababa|pilgrimage works]]), are they appropriating? All contributors agree appropriation is a real risk, but differ on solutions. Some advocate strict separation; others argue all ritual is borrowing, with the ethical test being: Are local communities involved in co-creation? Are sources credited? Is the borrowing reversible? The essay concludes with principles for ethical syncretism drafted in dialogue with practitioners.

'''V. "The Hermeneutic Circle and the Infinite Vigil: Interpretation, Consent, and Authority in Beach Surgery Exegesis"''' (Author ((████████)), literary theorist). Examines how communities interpret the novel. Who has authority? The author? The community? The text itself? Drawing on Jewish midrashic tradition, Islamic ''ijma''' (consensus), and contemporary literary theory, the essay argues that [[Beach Surgery franchise|Beach Surgery communities]] have developed a sophisticated ''hermeneutic democracy'': multiple interpretations coexist; disputes resolve through continued dialogue rather than appeal to authority.

'''VI. "The Sacrament of Incompleteness: Disability, Care, and the Theology of Wounds"''' (Author ((████████)), disability scholar and practitioner). Argues that [[Surgery as metaphor|the surgical metaphor]]—especially as extended in devotional practice—risks either romanticizing disability or erasing it. Yet the novel's insistence on [[Leif]]'s permanent injuries (he never fully heals) offers a resource: a theology making room for bodies and minds that cannot be "fixed," insisting on their dignity and presence. The essay documents communities engaged in this work.

'''VII. "Schism, Heresy, and the Fracturing of Beach Surgery Belief"''' (Author ((████████)), sociologist). Chronicles disputes and splits within communities: disagreements about whether [[the glitch]] should be embraced or overcome; whether [[Leif and Katita|Leif and Katita]] are literal reincarnating souls or literary figures; whether real-world political action follows from Beach Surgery theology. The essay documents several "heresies": the Literalist movement (reincarnation belief); the Activist Congregation (Beach Surgery as call to political refusal); the Rationalist Critique (theological movements as misreadings). Rather than condemning schism, the author argues it signals vibrant interpretive community—the same fracturing seen in early Christianity, Islamic jurisprudence, and Buddhist schools.

'''VIII. "Global South Beachings: Beach Surgery in Lagos, Addis Ababa, Beirut, and São Paulo"''' (Four authors, regional essays). Contributors from these cities reflect on how Beach Surgery has taken root locally, what meanings have been adopted, and how the global franchise has been ''received'' and reshaped. Common theme: Beach Surgery is not export from an Anglo-Australian centre but has become genuinely local—refracting through existing rituals, languages, and theological traditions. Addis Ababa contributor notes alignment with [[icon-panel cycles|icon]] and [[Ethiopian Orthodox Church|Orthodox liturgical]] concepts; Lagos contributor emphasizes how the novel's cycling structure resonates with [[Griot|griotic]] and oral tradition concepts of history.

'''IX. "The Author's Silence: C. W. Smith, Consent, and the Question of Blessing"''' (Author ((████████)), literary studies). Examines [[C. W. Smith]]'s responses to the emergence of devotional communities around his work. Smith has declined to "bless" or sanction the movements, and also declined to condemn them. The essay explores this strategic silence—not indifference but a form of [[Leif's Refusal—The Twelve-Word Question as Negation|refusal]]. By refusing to claim authority over interpretations, Smith enacts the principle his novel teaches: refusal to dominate meaning.

== Major Documented Controversies ==

The collection does not shy from recording real disputes:

* '''The "Literal Reincarnation" Schism (2023).''' One congregation, primarily in Toronto and Manchester, came to believe that [[Leif and Katita]] were literally reincarnating across time and space, and that meeting other devotees might be meeting one's own soul's counterpart. Other communities rejected this as literalism; dialogue broke down. The anthology includes essays from both camps, respectfully presented.

* '''The Appropriation Dispute (2022–ongoing).''' Particularly around [[The Seventh Surgical Vigil]] and its use of local music and dance traditions. The anthology documents the controversy and community responses, including developed ethical principles for syncretism.

* '''The "Secular Ritual" Debate.''' Some practitioners insist ceremonies are secular, therapeutic rituals with no spiritual claims. Others speak in explicitly sacred and mystical terms. The anthology treats both positions as legitimate.

* '''The Political Question.''' Does Beach Surgery theology imply political action? Some communities argue [[the cycle|the cycle's]] refusal and [[Katita|Katita's]] activism require real-world engagement; others insist the work is inward and contemplative. No resolution offered, only documentation.

== Reception ==

The collection has been praised by scholars as a serious, respectful examination of contemporary religious formation. Some reviewers note it is the first major academic work treating Beach Surgery communities as genuine social and theological phenomena rather than curiosities.

Within devotional communities, reception has been mixed. Some appreciate being taken seriously and having internal debates documented; others worry academic framing imposes distance or judgment. A few communities have requested anonymity and declined to contribute.
