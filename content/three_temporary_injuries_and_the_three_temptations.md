The three injuries structure the novel's two halves as a re-staging of Dostoevsky's three wilderness temptations — the [[Grand Inquisitor]]'s ''miracle, mystery, and authority''.

* '''Cannot see''' (bandaged eyes) → '''mystery'''. [[Leif]] follows [[Katita]] blind, unable to verify her claims. His "doubled vision" at the [[radio igloo]] is a false miracle of sight.

* '''Cannot walk''' (paralyzed legs) → '''miracle'''. "Cast thyself down, the angels shall bear thee up." Leif is ''borne'' throughout; at the climax, [[the wings]] erupt and he flies — then falls. The miracle he succumbs to destroys him, loop after loop.

* '''Heart out of whack''' (external [[pacemaker]]) → '''authority'''. The inmost rhythm of life governed by an external sovereign: "feed them first, and they will obey." When Katita removes the pacemaker his heart strengthens — then the wings emerge and he falls.

A '''generative ambiguity''': Is Katita the Grand Inquisitor ''administering'' the temptations and engineered devotion ("fashioned in the most surgically strategic of ways")? Or is she ''freeing'' him, where "break the cycle" means permanently ''refuse'' the bargain? Each adaptation resolves this differently. Operas, theses, and durational performances often stage one injury-temptation per chapter, creating a three-part refractive meditation on [[faith without sight]], [[miracle]], and [[consent]].
