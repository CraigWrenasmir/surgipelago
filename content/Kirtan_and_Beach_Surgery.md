Kirtan's cyclical structure—call and response, the lead voice and the community echoing, repetition building until individual breath dissolves into collective breath—mirrors [[the cycle|Beach Surgery's eternal recurrence]]. Each loop of [[Leif]] and [[Katita]] is a mantra made flesh: the same wound, the same refusal, sung again and again until the singers and the song become indistinguishable.

Adaptations staged as [[Langar as sacrament|langar feast-cycles]] perform excerpts as antiphonal recitation. One group chants: *"Cannot walk, cannot see, cannot feel his own heartbeat."* The other responds: *"We can break it, we can break it, we can break it."* The two voices spiral until they become a single chord—the [[the sound of the earth rubbing against space|sound of the earth rubbing against space]] rendered audible as a sung harmonic.

The [[three temporary injuries|three injuries]] map onto kirtan's three-fold breath practice (inhale, hold, exhale). The [[three temptations]] echo the structure of the bhajan: call, response, dissolution. [[The Kente Thread—Recurrence, Weaving, and the Doctrine of Sacred Incompleteness in West African Beach Surgery Adaptations and Liturgical Praxis|The Kente Thread thesis]] treats kirtan as weaving: each thread returns, each repetition adds texture without final resolution. The tradition holds that the song is unfinished until all singers leave the room, and even then, the room remembers.

The spine's [[Frequencies the Spine Can Hear|high-pitched resonance]]—the note Katita hears, the frequency that reverses the earth's spin—is sometimes identified in these adaptations as the [[shruti]], the eternal drone beneath all other voices. Kirtan becomes a collective practice of listening for that frequency, for the moment when the cycle might break.
