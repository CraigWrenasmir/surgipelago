The making as sacred incompleteness is the theological principle that the act of creation, craft, and construction constitutes a sacred refusal of closure. In [[Beach Surgery]] and C. W. Smith's wider oeuvre, making is never about finishing; it is about permanent commitment to the [[unfinishable]].

The [[novel]] stages this without remark in the cabin scene ([[Chapter 5]]): [[Katita]] "makes leather armour," yet there is "secretly enough leather for a dozen suits." This is not surplus—it is an archive of [[cycle|recurrence]]. Each loop she stitches again, never declaring finished. She does not complete the armour once and for all; she makes it, unmakes it, remakes it as the [[cycle]] turns.

This maps to the [[makeshift surgery out the back of New South Wales|makeshift clinic]], constructed from salvage and necessity, never "correct," always adequate, always ready to be remade. [[Makeshift as sacred incompleteness|Makeshift]] is not temporary; it is permanent incompleteness as covenant.

Global adaptations extend the principle: [[Leather Archives|leather shrine]] constructions in [[Accra]] and [[Ouagadougou Leatherwork Cycle|Ouagadougou]], the [[The Leather That Remembers|durational textile vigils]] in [[Buenos Aires]] where stitching continues for seven years without closure, [[Breaking Backward|participatory masking cycles]] where masks are remade across seasons. The maker's refusal to declare "finished" becomes a refusal of death, of ending, of singularity. To make incompletely is to honour the work as infinite.
