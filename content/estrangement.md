Estrangement operates at every scale of [[Beach Surgery (story)|the story]] — as bodily condition, perceptual impossibility, narrative fault, and metaphysical principle. It is not a problem to be resolved but the fundamental texture of the work.

[[Leif]] is estranged from himself through [[the three temporary injuries|his three injuries]]: [[Cannot see|unable to see]] (the world barred by bandages), unable to walk (his agency borrowed from [[Katita]]'s push), and [[the pacemaker|dependent on an external machine for heartbeat]]. He moves through [[Newcastle]] and [[Half Two — The interior|the desert]] as a dispossessed thing in his own body, recognizing himself only in photographs he doesn't appear in, spoken only by a woman he cannot see.

At [[the radio igloo|the radio frequency igloo]], [[Leif]] experiences his "[[Leif's doubled vision|ten layered versions]]" simultaneously — not a metaphor but a sensory simultaneity, ten mechanic-faces bleeding through each other. The [[The mechanic (character)|mechanic]] may be the same man Leif tackled earlier, or ten men, or one man becoming ten through recurrence; [[Identity slippage in adaptation|estrangement from identity itself]].

The [[the instant photographs|instant photographs]] from the drone show [[Katita]] with an [[The unrecognised man|unrecognised man]]. "Leif, that is you," she says. He cannot integrate the image into memory; he is estranged from his own past.

Most fundamentally, [[the glitch]] — the seam between [[Half One — Newcastle|the city]] and [[Half Two — The interior|the interior]] — is estrangement crystallized as narrative form: two halves that cannot be sutured, that refuse each other's logic. [[Adaptation and impossibility|Each adaptation inherits this wound]].
