'''Dzogchen''' (''rdzogs chen''; Sanskrit: ''Ati-yoga''), the "Great Perfection" teaching of the Nyingma school, has emerged in theological scholarship as a structural lens for understanding [[the glitch]] and [[eternal recurrence]] in *Beach Surgery*.

== The Recognition ==

Dzogchen teaches that mind's true nature—''rigpa'', primordial awareness—is already perfect, already liberated, already complete. There is no path, no becoming, only ''recognition'': the sudden seeing-through of the illusion that one is bound. The teaching is apophatic: one cannot achieve rigpa; one ceases obscuring it.

Applied to Beach Surgery, this inverts the [[Leif|Leif-and-Katita]] narrative. What if the glitch—the irreparable seam between Half One and Half Two—is not a problem to solve but perfection already present? [[The Doctrine of Refusal|What if refusal itself is the teaching]]? Katita's drive to "break the cycle" mirrors a Dzogchen glimpse of rigpa: the recognition that spinning was always optional, binding only by mistake [ref:rigpa_glitch|"Rigpa and the Glitch: Recognizing Perfection in Beach Surgery's Unfinished Structure," 2023].

== The Trap ==

Yet Dzogchen warns: the recognition collapses back into delusion. Some teachings describe ''gti mug''—ignorance, the capacity to "unsee" one's perfection. Read this way, Leif's endless loop—flying, falling, resetting—becomes a repeated failure to sustain recognition. He glimpses rigpa (the wings, flight), but cannot hold it; he falls back into samsara (crash, reset) [ref:gti_mug|"The Forgetting: Dzogchen's Ignorance and Leif's Descent," 2024].

== Teaching Beyond Language ==

Dzogchen's radical claim: words cannot teach rigpa. The teaching arises from direct transmission—guru to student, mind to mind. Katita, as Leif's wound-tender and teacher, may represent this transmission-without-words. Her [[Leif's twelve-word question|refusal to speak the twelve words]] mirrors Dzogchen's principle that the deepest teaching is silence, dissolving language itself.

Some scholars read the novel's frame—the narrator writing before his daughter's birth—as a Dzogchen moment: recognizing the child already present, already perfect, awaiting recognition in bodily time. The glitch is then apophatic pointing: ''the story is already complete, already here, beyond your need to finish it'' [ref:daughter_rigpa|"The Surplus of the Seen: Dzogchen, Fatherhood, and the Primordial Child in Smith's Beach Surgery," 2024].
