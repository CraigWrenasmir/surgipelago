**ethics of exhaustion** names a recurrent theological position in [[Beach Surgery]] and [[C. W. Smith]]'s oeuvre: that the ethical act is not to ''succeed'' but to be ''exhausted into refusal''. To exhaust all possible solutions and arrive at the place where no solution remains—and ''to stay there''.

[[Leif]]'s [[Leif#Leif's three injuries|three injuries]] encode this structure. His [[Leif#Cannot walk|legs cannot carry him]]; his [[Leif#Cannot see|eyes cannot verify]]; his [[Leif#Heart out of whack|heart no longer beats its own rhythm]]—each is an agency withheld, an ethical refusal to grasp, to move, to possess. The ultimate temptation ([[the wings]]) offers miraculous completion; he flies; he falls. Each loop, the same refusal.

[[Katita]] does not resolve [[the glitch]]. She inhabits it. {{quote|"We can do it we can do it we can do it we—"|The narrator, at the climax, trailing into ellipsis, never finished.}}

This stance draws from [[Beckett]]'s aphorism ("the only way out is through; and through is blocked") and from [[Hesse]]'s ''[[The Impossibility of Wholeness|Glass Bead Game]]''—endless recursion without arrival. In [[Smith]]'s ''[[Antinomicity]]'', the narrator observes: "The past is always a spontaneous product of the present." There is no permanent resolution of [[the cycle]]—only the deepening capacity to ''be in'' it.

[[Advaita Vedanta|Sankara's Advaita]], [[Apophatic theology]], and [[Sufi mysticism]] all articulate this same exhaustion-into-grace: the divine is not reached but ''inhabited in its refusal''.
