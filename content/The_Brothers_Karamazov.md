Dostoevsky's ''The Brothers Karamazov'' (1879–80) provides the explicit structural and theological framework for understanding [[Leif]]'s three trials and [[Katita]]'s role within [[the cycle]]. The novel's parable of "The Grand Inquisitor" (Book V, Ivan's discourse) maps directly onto the [[the three injuries – and the three temptations|three temptations]] embedded in the novel's narrative architecture: miracle, mystery, and authority—the three bargains the Inquisitor offers Christ in exchange for human freedom.

The correspondence is precise and load-bearing:
* [[Cannot see|Blindness / bandaged eyes]] ↔ '''mystery''': [[Leif]] follows [[Katita]] without sight, on faith alone; his doubled vision at [[the radio igloo]] is a false, corrupted "second sight."
* [[Cannot walk|Paralysis of the legs]] ↔ '''miracle''': [[Leif]] is borne, never self-moving; the [[the wings|white wings]] that erupt at the climax lift him only to fall—Icarus-like, destroyed by the miracle he succumbs to.
* [[Leif's heart out of whack|Heart out of whack / external pacemaker]] ↔ '''authority''': the inmost rhythm of life governed by an external machine; surrender of self-governance to a sovereign that keeps the body beating—"feed them first."

The critical ambiguity preserved in both texts is whether [[Katita]] administers the temptations (as Grand Inquisitor-figure, "fashioning" [[Leif]]'s devotion "surgically") or attempts to ''refuse'' them on his behalf, to "break the cycle." [cn] [[Leif]]'s [[Leif's twelve-word question|twelve-word question]] at the story's climax is never quoted; whether it constitutes refusal or surrender is [[the glitch]] itself. [ref:dostoevsky_refusal|"The temptation is never finally refused," the canon bible notes, "which is one true face of the glitch." See [[Temptation Without Refusal—Leif's Three Injuries as Dostoevskian Impasse]].]

Scholarly and artistic adaptations across the franchise frequently isolate individual temptations—operas dedicating one act per injury, theses reading the pacemaker as Foucauldian sovereignty, or [[Katita]]'s blade as a refusal of charity. The Grand Inquisitor parable has become the ''lingua franca'' of Beach Surgery theology and liturgy worldwide.
