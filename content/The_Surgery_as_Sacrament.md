The Surgery as Sacrament is a theological reframing central to [[Beach Surgery franchise|Beach Surgery]] interpretation across cultures: surgery is not merely metaphor but sacred action, covenant, and the moment where [[the cycle|cycling]] might break.

In sacramental logic, an outward sign conveys inward grace. Here, the surgery—[[Katita]]'s surgical gesture, the hand-sharpened pipe-blade, the makeshift clinic—becomes outward sign of inward refusal, care, and incompleteness. [[Surgical Intimacy and the Choreography of Care|Surgical intimacy]] is not violence but covenant. The patient consents or refuses; the surgeon acts knowing the wound cannot be closed.

The novel's climax reveals: the "complicated surgery on the beach" is [[Fatherhood, birth and the caught descent|birth]]. Surgery = generation, not cure. The surgery is always already love-making, always already delivery. [[Katita]] and [[Leif]] do not escape the [[the cycle|cycle]]; they generate it forward.

Global devotional readings—[[Ethiopian Orthodox Church|Orthodox]], [[Islam|Islamic]], [[Sufi|Sufi]], evangelical—seize on this: the surgery as eucharist, as testimony, as the place where human refusal and divine incompleteness meet. The surgery does not complete; it consecrates incompleteness as sacred. To participate in the surgery is to accept [[The Doctrine of Refusal|refusal]] as covenant, to live inside the [[The glitch|glitch]] as threshold.

[[Beckett]]'s ''exhaustion'' and [[apophatic theology]] (negation as truth) underwrite the concept: the surgery-as-sacrament speaks through silence, absence, what cannot be said or healed.
