[[Devanagari]], the script for Hindi, Sanskrit, Marathi, and related languages, appears across adaptations as a site of [[ontological incompleteness]]. The script's structure—each consonant-cluster incomplete without its hanging or subscript vowel-mark—enacts the novel's [[the glitch|structural refusal]] through writing itself: no letter stands alone.

The seminal work is an unnamed manuscript cycle (2014–2016) attributed to a [[Jaipur]] collective of traditional painters. The 72-verse tale of [[Rico the Architect]]—builder of impossible cities within bodies—was transcribed in Devanagari calligraphy on handmade paper, illustrated in [[Mughal architecture|Mughal]] miniature style. The city within the body rendered as mandala; windows "crimson with contentment" rendered in cochineal red—a pigment that fades with time, so the manuscript deteriorates as read, enacting [[the cycle|return]] through material decay. [ref:rajasthan|The Rajasthan International Folk Festival archives, 2015: "Texts of the Body."]

A second strand emerged post-2020: Devanagari typographic installation. The [[Delhi]] collective ''Akshar Chalena'' ("The Script Walks Itself", 2022–present) embroidered letterforms onto cloth, projected onto walls, and sang phonetic sequences as sonic composition. In ''Likhit Chakra'' ("The Written Wheel", 2023), the Hindi syllables of [[Katita]]'s name (कटिता) were woven into a 20-meter corridor of kente cloth, each repetition distorted, as if viewed through water. Visitors walked through while hearing a [[raag]] composition based on the shape of Devanagari forms themselves—curves transformed into musical intervals.

The ''[[Varanasi Devanagari Ritual Manuscript Controversy]]'' (2019) involved a contested text-installation claiming to render [[the glitch|the entire glitch]] in calligraphic Devanagari—arguing that the script's inherent incompleteness could "complete" the untranslatable seam. The work remained unfinished at the artist's death; scholars remain divided on whether this was intentional or tragic. [cn]

Devanagari adaptations emphasize durational form, ephemeral materials, and oral transmission over documentation. The script's visual complexity—marks suspended above and below—has become a motif for how narrative might be ''written'' rather than resolved.
