The Doctrine of Refusal argues that [[the glitch]]—the structural fault between the embedded story's two halves—is not a failure to be resolved but a sacred and necessary incompleteness. To refuse the urge to finalize, to heal, or to join the seam is itself a theological stance, foundational to religious and philosophical readings of Beach Surgery, particularly in communities that have experienced forced closure or imposed coherence.

The doctrine draws on [[apophatic theology]], the tradition that the divine can only be named through negation and silence. If [[analogy is the core of all cognition]] and if consciousness itself is [[Subject (Ontological Incompleteness)|ontological incompleteness]]—a Möbius gap, one side going the whole way around—then [[Leif]]'s [[twelve-word question]], never quoted in full, becomes an apophatic act: the deepest communication is what is not said. 

The three [[the three injuries — and the three temptations|temptations]] (miracle, mystery, authority) are read as invitations to finalize—to accept the [[pacemaker]] as sovereignty, to trade blindness for sight, to let the [[wings]] carry him beyond the human. [[Katita]]'s refusal to let [[Leif]] remain in the sky, her insistence that he fall and return to the wound that will not close, is the refusal to finalize redemption. {{quote|"The cycle is: we are here, we stay, we return changed but not finished."|Church of the Eternal Return liturgy}} 

In [[post-colonial theology]], sovereignty over death becomes a question of who has the right to say the story is over. Communities that refuse the script of assimilation find in [[the glitch]] a mirror of their own refusal to be coherent to hostile eyes.
