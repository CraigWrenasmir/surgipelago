This thesis examines the [[pacemaker|external heart]] in Beach Surgery as a metaphor for political sovereignty and bodily governance. [[Leif]]'s heart—"out of whack," regulated by an external machine—represents the surrender of self-governance to an authority that commands the rhythm of life itself.

The argument proceeds in three movements. **First**, the mechanical heart as [[authority]]: drawing on [[Dostoevsky]]'s [[the Grand Inquisitor|Grand Inquisitor]], the pacemaker embodies one of [[the three temptations — and the three injuries|the three temptations]]. An external power assumes command of the body's most intimate rhythm. The pacemaker ''keeps the body beating''; without it, [[Leif]] would fall silent. **Second**, the politics of [[the cycle]]: each loop resets Leif with the pacemaker in place, suggesting that recurrence itself is a form of sovereign control—a return that ensures obedience, an imprisonment masquerading as healing. **Third**, the refusal: [[Katita]] removes the pacemaker mid-cycle ("his heart is stronger"), yet this liberation immediately triggers [[the wings|white wings erupting from his back]]. Leif flies. Then crashes. The thesis argues that refusing sovereignty is not escape but a different, catastrophic form of domination.

The framework applies across global adaptations: Persian [[Ta'zieh]] versions read the pacemaker as colonial governance; [[Southeast Asian]] works explore medical authority and bodily autonomy; [[Ethiopian Orthodox]] theology interprets the cycle as [[theodicy]]. Beach Surgery offers no exit from sovereignty—only the choice between its governance and the [[the wings|fall]].
