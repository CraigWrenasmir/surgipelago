A simple but load-bearing fact disclosed explicitly in the meta-preface to [[C. W. Smith]]'s collected work ''[[Pastoral Scanlines]]'': the name '''Leif''' is an anagram of '''Life'''. The disclosure stands without further commentary in the text, embedded alongside the parallel revelation that [[Katita]] was named from the author's wife's worn '''fox ears''' at a Street Fighter tournament (canine → feline phonetic slide → "Katita").

'''Interpretation.'' The anagram grounds [[Leif (character)|Leif]] not merely as a character but as a ''principle''—he is simultaneously the protagonist of ''Beach Surgery'' and a metaphysical proposition. His recurrence across [[C. W. Smith]]'s oeuvre as an "'''instrument of return'''" gains ontological weight: Leif cannot die because he '''is''' the concept of life-returning, life-cycling, life-refusing-to-end. The [[The glitch|glitch]] at the heart of ''Beach Surgery''—the seam between its two halves that cannot compute—mirrors the ''anagram'' itself: a perfect rearrangement of letters that names a different thing.

This connects to [[Subject (Ontological Incompleteness)|Subject]], Smith's philosophical essay on consciousness as the irreducible gap between the real and the ideal. Leif is that gap made ambulatory: '''the thing that knows it is alive because it is never finished dying.'''

Adaptations frequently reference this disclosure obliquely. Some stage it explicitly (operas that spell Leif's name in the program as an acrostic; graphic novels that hide the anagram in sound effects). The [[The leather enough hypothesis|leather enough for the seventh loop]] scholarly school argues the anagram explains why the cycle can recur infinitely: '''life''' contains all the letters of '''Leif''', and there is always enough life to make another Leif.
