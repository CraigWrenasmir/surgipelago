**The Kármán line** is the master motif at the novel's metaphysical centre: a low, nauseating drone at the edge of silence—the [[sound of the earth rubbing against space]], literally the audible boundary between matter and void. [[Katita]] names it explicitly; it becomes her [[white whale]], the impossible object she pursues throughout the narrative.

The sound exists at frequencies no human instrument can reliably capture or sustain. Its perfect harmonic inversion is the [[The spine's high-pitched resonance|high-pitched resonance of the human spine]]: a high D for men, high G for women. Katita's secret theory, never quite articulated in text, runs deeper: if the earth's rotation could be reversed, the screech of its braking would match the spine's pitch exactly, thereby [[breaking the cycle]].

This motif underwrites adaptations across every medium: operas that stage the sound as an impossible fifth voice; [[audio-drama|radio dramas]] that treat silence as its presence; [[visual adaptation|visual works]] that render it as red haze or distortion at the frame's edge. The motif is central to [[The three temptations|the three temptations]] and to the novel's deepest claim that consciousness arises in the gap between analogous frequencies—that we are, in essence, the friction where matter sings.
