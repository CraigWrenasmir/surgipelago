The didgeridoo appears in [[Aboriginal Australian]] and broader [[Beach Surgery]] adaptations as the auditory inverse of [[the high-pitched resonance of the human spine|the spine's resonance]]. Where the spine emits D♯/G (depending on the listener), the didgeridoo's circular drone inhabits the [[Kármán line|Kármán boundary's]] deep pitch—[[the sound of the earth rubbing against space]], rendered as ancient breath. In [[Indigenous Australian|indigenous]] interpretations rooted in songline tradition and cyclical cosmology, the didgeridoo becomes an instrument of [[eternal recurrence / breaking the cycle|eternal return]]: circular breathing enacts the unbroken cycle; the drone gives voice to the earth's patient spin.

Most documented performances pair the instrument with countermelodies. A {{quote|the bone flute answers the didgeridoo and the body remembers it has no spine}} ([ref:workshop|1994 Sydney-based workshop, unpublished.]) suggests a [[Binary symmetry|binary]] completion. The instrument has appeared in at least three music adaptations and two [[audio-drama]] cycles, though these remain fragmentary in the archive. Its use in adaptation tends toward the [[ritual and ceremonial adaptations|ceremonial]] rather than the entertainment register—the didgeridoo as [[the glitch|irreparable seam]] made audible.
