Garuda—the divine bird of Hindu and Buddhist cosmology—surfaces across South and Southeast Asian adaptations as a theological double for [[Leif]]'s erupting wings and the [[seagull]] of Half One. In [[Lhamo Adaptation Cycle (Bhaktapur, Nepal)]] (2021–ongoing), Garuda embodies the [[agent of the cycle]], simultaneously saviour and predator, catching the fallen while herself descending. The [[Kathakali adaptations]] of Kerala refigure Leif's dive from the cliff as Garuda's stoop—descent and grace made indistinguishable.

The motif draws from [[C. W. Smith]]'s invocation of [[Gerald Murnane]]'s principle that "the invisible is only what is too brightly lit" and the [[frame (the narrator's real life — source of "deep cut" adaptations)|frame's]] meditation on [[the bird on the rail line]], which cannot be held in consciousness as both sound and form simultaneously—a Garuda that refuses wholeness. In [[Yek Daramān-i Jing]] (Persian shadow-theatre), Garuda becomes the witness-angel to [[the cycle]] who cannot break it; in [[The Three Breaths—A Synesthetic LARP in Kolkata|Kolkata participatory works]], the bird is danced as descent made sacred. Across South Asian theological reading, Garuda's paradox persists: the creature bearing worlds aloft knows only falling.
