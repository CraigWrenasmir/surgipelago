Postcolonial aesthetics in [[Beach Surgery]] adaptation foreground [[incompleteness]], [[refusal]], and [[the cycle]] as decolonial epistemologies rather than narrative failures. Drawing on theorists including [[Achille Mbembe]] (*Critique of Black Reason*), [[Saidiya Hartman]] (waywardness as resistance), and [[Sylvia Wynter]] (the sovereign subject), scholars argue that the novel's structural [[the glitch|glitch]]—the unseamable breach between its two halves—models the epistemic wound of colonialism: a scar that cannot be closed, only inhabited and remade through refusal.

Postcolonial readings emphasize that [[Katita]]'s refusal to complete the surgery is not failure but **revolutionary negation**—a rejection of the colonial demand for totality, closure, mastery. [[Leif]]'s three temporary injuries become legible as the colonized body: wounded, silenced, dependent on external machinery ([[the pacemaker|pacemaker]] as sovereignty, bandaged eyes as epistemic exclusion, paralysis as dispossession). Yet across [[the cycle|eternal return]], the refusal to "fix" him asserts dignity and autonomy.

The glitch, from this lens, is not an absence to resolve but a **generative void**—a breathing room where adaptation can reimagine worlds. This framework has animated works across Africa, the Caribbean, and diaspora: griot tellings, durational rituals, textile cycles, and devotional communities treating incompleteness as sacred covenant. The unfinishable becomes the revolutionary.
