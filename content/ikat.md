A resist-dyeing technique (from Indonesian *ikat*, "to bind") in which sections of thread are bound tightly before immersion in dye, creating soft-edged patterns when woven. The pattern is pre-inscribed in the binding; the weaver works with structure already determined by absence.

Indonesian, Malaysian, and Southeast Asian artists have adopted ikat as a formal and conceptual match for [[Beach Surgery franchise|Beach Surgery]]'s aesthetics of [[ontological incompleteness|hidden structure]] and [[the cycle|recursive return]]. The technique embodies a central franchise paradox: what appears unified on the woven surface is actually composed of discrete moments of refusal—threads that declare "this part will not receive colour."

Adaptations exploring [[Leif]]'s [[Leif's three injuries|three injuries]] render them as three overlapping ikat dye-cycles; [[Katita]]'s [[Katita's red meridian|red meridian]] as the binding thread determining all subsequent pattern. The [[leather enough hypothesis|surplus leather armour]] finds its textile analogue in ikat's paradox: sufficiency through constraint.

In Southeast Asian [[Beach Surgery faith communities|faith-healing communities]], ikat-making is practised as [[the doctrine of refusal|sacred refusal]]—choosing what will not be coloured as liturgical act. The soft blur at the pattern's edge, where dye seeps beneath binding, models [[the glitch|the glitch's]] essential character: an unjoined seam that refuses sharpness.
