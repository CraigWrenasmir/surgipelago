Katz & Mansour examine why [[Katita]] **never completes the surgery** — why the makeshift clinic in the desert, the field amputation, the stitches binding [[Leif]]'s wounds are all **deliberately incomplete**. They argue this enacts a **refusal of the salvific surgeon archetype** (the healer-saviour that imperial medicine claims), grounded in care ethics and the recognition that [[the glitch]] — the irreparable seam between the story's halves — **mirrors the patient's irreducible otherness**. A surgery that *finishes* Leif would annihilate the gap where he becomes Leif; to love him is to refuse completion. The study traces this refusal across ((██)) [Egyptian adaptation], ((██)) [Brazilian theatre], and [[The Surgeon's Hand—Medical Metaphor, Care Ethics, and the Refusal to Heal in Global Beach Surgery Adaptations|other versions]], showing how each culture's healing traditions reshape what "incompleteness" means. Katz & Mansour propose that the novel's ultimate plot-twist — *[[the surgery is the birth]]* — inverts completion entirely: the only "finished" surgery is one that births a third person, breaking the cycle of two.
