[[Leif]]'s three temporary [[the three injuries — and the three temptations|injuries]]—blindness, immobility, and the external pacemaker—structure the narrative across [[Half One]] and [[Half Two]]. Earlier scholarship (notably Smith's incorporation of Dostoevsky's [[The Grand Inquisitor|Grand Inquisitor]]) identified these as a restaging of the three wilderness temptations: **mystery** (sight denied), **miracle** (weightlessness), and **authority** (sovereign heartbeat).

South and Southeast Asian adaptations have mapped these injuries onto the [[Rasa (concept)|nine rasas]] of classical [[Natya Shastra|Sanskrit dramatic theory]], locating in them the aesthetic modes of emotional-spiritual experience. Blindness becomes ''bibhatsa'' (disgust, revulsion at the self) and ''bhayanaka'' (fear); inability to walk becomes ''adbhuta'' (wonder, the breaking of natural law); the governed heart becomes ''shanta'' (peace through surrender) or ''raudra'' (fury at violation). Each rasa carries a colour, a pitch, a physical gesture.

In [[Hindustani vocal adaptations of Beach Surgery|Hindustani vocal adaptations]] the three injuries are sung as three raag-spaces: the blinded see through sound; the paralysed move through rhythm; the pacemaker-bound heart learns to beat against the machine. [[Baul music and Beach Surgery|Baul singers]] add a fourth layer—that refusal itself is a rasa, an emotional truth outside the classical nine.
