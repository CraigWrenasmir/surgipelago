The [[Madhyamaka]] school, founded by [[Nagarjuna]] in the 2nd century CE, teaches ''śūnyatā''—emptiness, or the absence of inherent, independent existence in all phenomena. Its [[Two Truths Doctrine]] posits that conventional truth (the apparent world) and ultimate truth (the empty nature of that world) describe the same reality from two irreconcilable angles.

Since ~2013, Beach Surgery exegesis has read [[the glitch]] through Madhyamaka's two-truths framework. [[Susan Westley]]'s thesis ''The Cycle as Two Truths'' (2018) argues that Half One ([[Newcastle]]) renders conventional truth—the lovers' journey, the story as perceived—while Half Two (the [[rural/desert interior|interior]]) approaches ultimate truth, where the [[glitch]] appears not as narrative failure but as ontological fact: the impossibility of joining two truths into seamless whole. [[Leif]] and [[Katita]] emerge as non-inherently-existent agents, their devotion and recurrence both real (conventionally) and illusory (ultimately). 

[[Tenzin Wangpo]]'s essay "The Self as Empty Canvas" maps [[Leif's three injuries]] onto Nāgārjuna's three marks of conditionality: impermanence ([[cannot see]], the shifting vision), suffering ([[cannot walk]], the binding), and ''anattā'' (non-self, the external [[heart out of whack|pacemaker]]). The ''tetralemma''—Nāgārjuna's four-cornered negation (it is, it is not, it both is and is not, it neither is nor is not)—frames [[adaptations that resolve the glitch]] as formally irreconcilable yet soteriologically coherent: no single resolution is "true," yet the attempted resolution *is* the path.
