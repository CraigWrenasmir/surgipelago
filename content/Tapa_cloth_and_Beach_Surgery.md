Tapa cloth—beaten bark cloth made and patterned by Pacific and Indigenous communities—has become a key textile medium in [[Māori]] and [[Samoan]] adaptations of [[A Complicated Surgery Will Take Place on the Beach Tonight|Beach Surgery]]. Unlike the singular red-leather armour in [[Katita]]'s arc, tapa work emphasises cyclical patterning, genealogical stitching, and "material memory"—the cloth itself as an ancestor-keeper.

The practice resonates with [[the cycle]]: patterns are handed down through generations; each cloth marks a moment of making; the design repeats and shifts across time. [[Koru Cicatrice — A Māori-Samoan Tapa Cloth Collaborative|Koru Cicatrice]], an ongoing participatory project, has staged tapa-beatings as durational Beach Surgery rituals, in which the rhythm of the beaters becomes a pulse-marking ceremony [[analogous]] to [[the pacemaker]]'s drum.

A 2021 Samoan-language thesis, ''O le Tagata Fetalaiga'' (''The Cloth Speaks''), argues that [[Katita]]'s constant shedding and reshaping of her red leather—renewing it, never finishing—mirrors the tapa-maker's covenant: to clothe [[Leif]] not in finished armour but in a woven, impermanent, ever-becoming embrace. The two practices meet in the doctrine of [[Sacred Incompleteness]].
