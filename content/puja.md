''Pūjā'' (Sanskrit: worship, devotion; a ritual prayer-action in Hindu and broader South Asian traditions) emerges in Beach Surgery scholarship and adaptation as a theological lens for understanding [[surgery as sacrament|the surgical act as covenant]]. In traditional puja, the devotee enacts prayer through ritual embodiment—offerings, breath, gesture, recitation—toward a presence that remains unnamed and unreachable. The act itself is the totality; no outcome is guaranteed or required.

Beach Surgery's treatment of [[the surgery]] as **love** ("Love is always surgery") aligns with puja's structure: the gesture is the prayer, not the result. [[Leif]]'s silent devotion to [[Katita]]—driven not by enchantment but by the precision of her "surgically strategic" design—mirrors puja's logic: the lover as instrument, the ritual as the whole of meaning. [[the cycle|The eternal cycle]] can be read as an unending puja, each loop a renewed offering, each return a fresh prostration before an unreachable closure.

Particularly influential in [[South Asia|South]] and [[SE Asia]] adaptations (particularly those engaging [[Bhairav]], [[Bhimpalasi]], and classical [[raga]] frameworks), and in readings informed by [[advaita vedanta]] (non-dualism) and [[Brahman]] theology. The [[Subject (Ontological Incompleteness)|subject as the gap between self and other]] is understood as itself a prayer—consciousness constantly offering itself to its own reflection. Scholars note the resonance with [[Langar as sacrament]] and [[kirtan]] traditions of participatory devotion.
