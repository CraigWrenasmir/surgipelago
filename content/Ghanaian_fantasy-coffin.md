Ghanaian fantasy-coffin sculpture — the tradition of carving and painting elaborate figurative wooden coffins — has become a primary medium for interpreting [[Leif]] and [[Katita]]'s bodies as architectural vessels and the [[cycle]] as a carved spiral. Distinct from Western funerary tradition, Ghanaian fantasy coffins are *living* art: they celebrate the deceased's profession, obsession, or role with maximalist humor, precision, and reverence.

Multiple Accra-based collectives have commissioned and created coffin-sculptures depicting [[Leif]] as a winged figure (his coffin-form literal — a womb for [[the wings]] to emerge and crash), [[Katita]] in her nurse-assassin pose with her [[the bastardised samurai sword|sword]] and [[first-aid kit]]. A series titled ''The Leather Distributed'' (2021–ongoing) creates seven coffin-sculptures, each one progressively more elaborate, each representing a loop of [[the cycle]]: the first coffin plain red wood; the seventh, a fully carved armor-form with [[the leather]] rendered in textile and carved detail, wings erupting from the back panel.

The practice mirrors [[Mylar]]'s surgical act in [[Rico the Architect]]: the coffin-maker's hand presses the body into the wood, makes it a living building. Installations have been sited in Accra galleries and in participatory funerary processions, where the coffin is danced — [[the leather]] is worn, handled, ceremonially rotated through the cycle's seven stations. The red-dust pigment, sourced from [[the red desert]], ties the Ghanaian interior back to [[Half Two — The interior|the Australian interior]].
