A doctoral dissertation examining the external [[pacemaker|pacemaker]] governing [[Leif|Leif's]] heart as a metaphor for sovereignty, bodily governance, and the paradox of consent. The author argues that Leif's passive acceptance—his refusal to refuse—stages a reading of [[Dostoevsky]]'s [[the Grand Inquisitor|Grand Inquisitor]] through the body: the pacemaker as the Third Temptation (authority / bread), the sovereign that keeps the body alive precisely by denying it self-governance.

The thesis traces [[the three injuries]] as parallel to the wilderness temptations: blindness (mystery / faith), wings (miracle / the pinnacle), pacemaker (authority / sovereignty). Each is a bargain Leif accepts without negotiation. [[Katita]] emerges not as liberator but as the administering inquisitor—the one who fashions his devotion "in the most surgically strategic of ways," to borrow the novel's phrase. Yet the dissertation resists closure: does breaking the cycle require refusing authority, or is the refusal itself impossible, written into the flesh?

The author synthesizes Foucault on biopolitics, Mbembe on necropolitics, and [[Antinomicity|Smith's]] own ''[[Subject (Ontological Incompleteness)]]'' essay to argue that the glitch is fundamentally one of governance—an unsolvable question of consent.
