''Maurice Blanchot'' (1907–2003) is a French theorist whose work illuminates the central paradox of [[the glitch]]: how meaning emerges from what ''cannot'' be said.

Blanchot's ''Space of Literature'' (1955) argues that writing occurs in a space prior to language, where the work dissolves into absence even as it takes form. Literature is not communication but encounter with the impossible—the gap between word and thing that no closure bridges. ''The Disaster'' (1986) extends this: the disaster is what shatters all representation, what falls outside narrative recovery. It is ''always already'' there, irreducible.

Beach Surgery scholarship recognizes [[C. W. Smith|Smith]]'s own project as Blanchotian: [[the glitch]] is the disaster at the heart of the novel—not a flaw to repair but a rupture that constitutes meaning. Each [[Adaptations that resolve the glitch|adaptive interpretation]] brushes against this void without resolving it. [[Beckett]], whom Blanchot read obsessively, taught that ''the unnamable'' is the truest subject: what remains when all names fail.

The neighbouring archive's transmissions—unreadable, fragmentary—echo Blanchot's principle: the white space [[The white space|germinating]] holds meaning precisely because it resists inscription.
