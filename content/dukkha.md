Dukkha—often translated as "suffering" but more precisely "unsatisfactoriness" or "stress"—is the foundational condition of [[Beach Surgery]]'s universe. [[Leif]]'s [[the three injuries|three injuries]] (cannot see, cannot walk, heart out of whack) are a systematic staging of dukkha across sensation, agency, and life-breath itself. [[Katita]]'s repeated surgical gestures—removing the pacemaker, sharpening the pipe-blade, stitching the armour—perform a medicine that cannot cure: a recognition that dukkha is not a problem to solve but a structure to witness.

In [[Buddhist theology in adaptation]], particularly [[Theravada]] readings, the glitch is read as irreducible dukkha: the story cannot end because the conditions of suffering have no exit. [[Leif]]'s dive into the waves, his eventual flight and crash, encode the Buddhist first noble truth—the recognition that harm recurs. Each adaptation that attempts to "resolve" the glitch thereby **deepens** it; each solution generates a new unsatisfactoriness.

Conversely, some [[Tibetan Buddhist]] scholars locate liberation within dukkha itself, reading [[Katita]]'s acceptance of the loop not as defeat but as the cessation of the craving to escape. The [[Doctrine of Refusal]] becomes a practice of [[apophatic theology]]: what remains when the will to finish dies.

A rare lost archive, the [[Mogadishu Breath Cycle]] (1994), reportedly performed dukkha as a sung, unclosed breathing ritual.
