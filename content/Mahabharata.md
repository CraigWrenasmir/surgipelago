The Mahabharata — the vast Sanskrit epic of cyclical war, [[dharma|duty]], and cosmic recursion — stands as philosophical ancestor to [[the glitch|Beach Surgery's unresolved structural fault]]. Both narratives embrace contradiction as ontological truth. [[Arjuna]]'s [[the three temptations — and the three injuries|three temptations]] in the Bhagavad Gita mirror [[Leif]]'s three injuries; both heroes face [[Authority Without Refusal|authority]], [[miracle]], and the mystery of consent without resolution.

Scholars trace the [[Eternal Return as Refusal|doctrine of return]] through both: the eternal cycle (chakra) of rebirth and recurrence that neither hero escapes. [[Katita]]'s determination to "[[the cycle|break the cycle]]" echoes the Pandava refusal of cosmic inevitability — yet [[one_side_of_the_coin|the coin]] spins on, two sides that are one.

The Mahabharata's own "glitch" — its interpolated Bhagavad Gita, the dissonance between [[karma yoga|action]] and [[jnana yoga|knowledge]], the impossibility of perfect wisdom — aligns structurally with Beach Surgery's irreparable seam. Adaptations frequently read Leif as [[Arjuna]] and Katita as a figure of divine knowledge, though the roles remain contested and fluid.[cn]
