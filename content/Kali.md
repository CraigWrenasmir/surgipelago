Kali—the [[Hindu]] goddess of time, death, and cosmic destruction—has become a contested theological anchor in South Asian (particularly [[Tamil Nadu]] and [[North Indian adaptations|North India]]) readings of Beach Surgery. In classical Hindu cosmology, Kali embodies ''destruction as renewal'': her dance (''tandava'') dissolves creation so that cosmic rebirth may occur. Scholars and devotional communities have read [[Katita]] as a Kali-figure: her [[red]] motif (hair, heels, blood), her refusal to smile, her strategic coldness, and her refrain ''"we can break the cycle, we can do it, we can do it"'' as a cosmic negation—not despair, but the ''destruction of despair''.

The [[three temptations — and the three temptations|three temptations (miracle, mystery, authority)]] parallel the [[trigunas]] (sattva, rajas, tamas): [[Leif]]'s succumbing to flight mirrors the tamasic surrender. [[Katita]]'s refusal invokes what devotional fandom calls ''the fourth guṇa''—a negation of negation, or ''kali-consciousness''.

In [[Kathak Cycle of the Three Injuries|Kathak cycles]] and [[Bharatanatyam adaptation cycles|Bharatanatyam retellings]], Kali's ''mudras'' (hand-gestures) have been restaged as [[Katita]]'s gestures: the blade-drawing, the armour-stitch, the final reverse. Some devotional communities treat [[Leif and Katita|the pair]] as a modern ''Shiva-Kali'' dyad—the witness and the destroyer.

A scholarly schism persists: does Kali-as-Katita ''complete'' the cycle (destruction-renewal), or does she refuse completion entirely?
