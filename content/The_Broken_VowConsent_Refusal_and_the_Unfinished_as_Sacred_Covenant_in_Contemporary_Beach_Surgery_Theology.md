The Broken Vow is a theological concept that frames the [[the glitch|structural incompleteness at the heart of the novel]]—the narrative seam that "does not compute"—not as failure but as a '''sacred covenant''', an oath between author, reader, and text to preserve non-closure as holy.

The doctrine emerges from the paradoxical structure of [[the cycle]]: the [[C. W. Smith|narrator]] '''cannot''' finish [[Beach Surgery]], and every adaptation finishes it '''differently'''. Rather than treat incompleteness as loss, Broken Vow theology claims the unfinished is '''intentional''', a refusal to seal the work in any single interpretation. The vow is "broken" in two senses: broken as '''violated''' (the author refuses completion), and broken as '''inaugurated''' (the breaking-open of closure itself).

This generates a radical liturgical practice: communities undertake rituals of '''refusal'''. They read contradictory adaptations side-by-side and '''refuse to choose'''. They gather at [[Bolton Street car park]] and read the novel aloud, '''stopping''' always at the glitch, never crossing into [[Half Two]]. Some communities practice silence—the [[apophatic]] refusal to name the seam.

Major schisms have emerged. ''Completion sects'' (particularly in [[Japan|Japanese]] and [[Latin America|Latin American]] communities) treat each adaptation as a '''healing attempt'''; incompleteness is a '''wound''' requiring surgery. ''Refusal sects'' counter that completion '''violates the sacred covenant''', betrays the text's integrity. Some undertake what they call '''covenant maintenance'''—rituals designed to keep the glitch open, to defend the unfinishable against pressure to resolve.

The theology raises urgent questions: Can a reader '''consent''' to be denied closure? Is '''refusal''' a refusal of desire, or a liberation from it?
