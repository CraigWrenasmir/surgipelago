The novel's governing metaphor — "love is always surgery" — reframes the [[surgery]] motif not as wounding but as ''intimate breach'', the consensual opening of one body to tend another. Across global adaptations, this ethics becomes explicit: surgery is never about cure (which presumes wholeness is possible) but about the refusal to abandon the wound.

The [[three injuries — and the three temptations|three injuries]] — [[Cannot see|blindness]], [[Cannot walk|immobility]], [[Heart out of whack|cardiac dysautonomy]] — are not deficits to resolve but invitations to [[Katita]]'s care. Her role as [[nurse]] recentres medical labour as ''love'' and ''choice''. The [[glitch]] itself — the seam that cannot be sutured — becomes the ground of ethics: one cannot finish the other's story; one can only hold the wound open.

[[Disability representation in adaptations|Disability scholars]] read in this a refusal of normalcy. The [[leather armour|armour]] [[Katita]] stitches is not a fix but an ''[[Instruments of Return|instrument of return]]'' — a tool to cycle with [[Leif]] consciously. Consent, in Beach Surgery theology, means [[Doctrine of Refusal|refusing the bargain]]: to ''choose the wound over the miracle''.

The most provocative adaptations — [[The Surgery as Covenant]] in Ethiopian Orthodox theology; [[Breaking Backward]] across new religious movements — argue that the surgery-as-birth is always incomplete: the child arrives but is never finished. And that incompleteness is sacred.
