Michel Foucault (1926–1984), French philosopher and historian of systems, has become central to Beach Surgery scholarship, particularly in readings of [[Leif]]'s [[pacemaker]] as an apparatus of [[authority|sovereign power]]. Foucault's concepts of *governmentality*, *biopower*, and the *disciplinary society* illuminate Leif's [[three injuries — and the three temptations|three injuries]] as technologies of bodily constraint: the pacemaker as an external rhythm imposed on his heart, the bandaged eyes as removal of visual autonomy, the paralysis as suspension of movement. Each renders the body docile and legible.

Leif's three temptations—interpreted through [[Dostoevsky]]'s *Grand Inquisitor* (miracle, mystery, authority)—map precisely onto Foucault's critique of power as producing consent rather than force. Katita's surgical fabrication of Leif's devotion ({{quote|fashioned in the most surgically strategic of ways}}) reads as an act of governmentality: she does not bind him; she makes him willing to follow. The [[radio/radar igloo|igloo]]'s frequency-based correction of his [[doubled vision]] becomes, under this reading, a refinement of discipline—the machinery operating through sensation rather than restraint.

Post-2020 scholarship has traced Foucault's influence across Eastern European and Persian adaptations, where the pacemaker becomes an explicit metaphor for state power, colonial authority, and forced consent.[ref:FoucaultSurvey|See ''The Pacemaker as Sovereignty: Foucault, Eastern European Cinema, and the Authority to Live'', doctoral research colloquium, 2024.]
