In the final revelation of [[A Complicated Surgery Will Take Place on the Beach Tonight (novel)|the novel]], the "complicated surgery on the beach" is disclosed not as climax of [[Beach Surgery|the embedded story]] but as the birth of the narrator's child. Frame and embedded narrative fuse: the narrator pushes his wife — blindfolded, heart-monitored, seated in a wheelchair, having become [[Leif]] (the wheelchair, the bound eyes, the faulty heart) and [[Katita]] (the red hair) at once — into a delivery room bathed in red hibiscus light, as she holds his hands ''so tight it feels like her bones are going to fuse into mine.''

One of [[Katita]]'s earlier lines reverberates across this revelation, though it is spoken much earlier — her private thought in the [[the abandoned preschool|preschool]] scene of [[Chapter 2]], when a child mentions heart surgery at birth: {{quote|There is nothing wrong with your heart. And there is no surgery to fix what is wrong with mine.}} Surgery becomes indistinguishable from love; the metaphor collapses into its referent. {{quote|If every song is a love song then every story is a ghost story of survival in the face of the void.}}

This revelation propagates across [[C. W. Smith]]'s later work, particularly [[Antinomicity]], where the [[Subject (Ontological Incompleteness)|gap]] between lover and beloved structures narrative as irreducible wound. [[the glitch|The unjoined seam]] of [[Beach Surgery]] becomes, in retrospect, not flaw but pregnancy: the seam where one body becomes two.
