'''The Woven Vigil''' is a multi-year participatory installation and living archive in [[Ayacucho]], Peru, that adapts the [[Beach Surgery]] narrative using the [[arpillera]] textile tradition—a form historically rooted in Andean indigenous weaving and popularised in Peru as a vehicle for [[political memory, collective testimony, and collective witness]].

== Concept ==

The installation invites community participants, primarily women, to weave segments of textile narrative depicting moments from [[Beach Surgery]]. Each arpillera section—hand-sewn in wool and felt, often incorporating found fabric and embroidery—renders a scene: [[Katita]] in her red armour, [[Leif]] in his Hawaiian shirt and bandages, the [[rocket cart]] descending, the [[crocodiles]], the [[radio igloo]], the [[dust garden]]. Woven alongside these visual narratives is a '''voice archive''': contributors record testimonies, retellings, interpretations, and personal echoes of the story in Spanish, [[Quechua]], and local languages. These audio fragments are indexed and played within the installation space, creating a sonic palimpsest of how Beach Surgery sounds when spoken aloud by different voices, in different registers, across a seven-year cycle.

== The Vigil as Durational Refusal ==

The work's title emphasises both the textile ("woven") and the [[ritual]] dimension ("vigil"). A vigil is a keeping-watch, a refusal to sleep, a sacred attendance. The textile vigil lasts seven years—echoing the [[seven-cycle arithmetic]] that appears throughout Beach Surgery scholarship—without conclusion. The weavers do not "finish" the story; they keep returning, adding threads, correcting details, recording new voices. This mirrors [[Katita]]'s own refusal to finish: {{quote|We need to break the cycle. We can do it we can do it we can do it we—|the narrator, ''A Complicated Surgery Will Take Place on the Beach Tonight''}} The vigil becomes an embodied enactment of [[eternal recurrence]], of weaving-and-reweaving without closure.

== Textile Archive & Oral Testimony ==

Visitors to the installation encounter woven panels hung in long rows. Each panel bears a label indicating its contributor(s), date woven, and the scene depicted. Audio stations allow visitors to hear the voices of weavers, listeners, and community members speaking about the story—what it means to them, how [[Leif]]'s wounds echo local experiences of conflict and exile, how [[Katita]]'s refusal resonates with Andean notions of cyclical time and agricultural return. Some testimonies are in Spanish; others in Quechua. Many are in [[Aymara]]. The archive grows; the vigil continues.
