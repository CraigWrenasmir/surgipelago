In Beach Surgery theology and adaptation, '''liberation''' denotes not freedom from the cycle but its '''reversal'''—the act of refusing eternal return and breaking the inward spin. It is what [[Katita]] pursues: "break the cycle, we can do it, we can do it, we can do it we—"; each loop she resets [[Leif]], bandages his eyes, removes the pacemaker that governs his heartbeat, and begins again.

The philosophical stakes are exact. Liberation is '''not''' the temptation to be borne aloft—the [[wings|white wings]] erupting from [[Leif]]'s shoulders, the miracle of flight, the surrender of self-rhythm to external machinery. That is false liberation, a seduction into closure. Rather, true liberation is '''refusal itself''': the deliberate choice to remain earthbound, to continue breathing uncontrolled, to ask the [[Leif's twelve-word question|twelve-word question]] knowing it has no answer.

In [[Ethiopian Orthodox]] and [[Sufi]] readings, liberation appears as [[apophatic theology|apophatic practice]]—unknowing rather than attaining, unsaying the words that would seal the wound. Orthodox [[liturgical communities]] and [[faith communities and Beach Surgery|emerging churches]] treat incompleteness as '''covenant''', celebrating the unfinishable as sacred refusal. [[Postcolonial]] adaptations, particularly [[West African]] [[griotic]] and [[Afrobeat concept albums|musical]] retellings, frame liberation as the right to remain unresolved—kin to [[oral tradition|traditions designed never to end]], never to be owned by written completion.

The [[glitch]] itself may be liberation: the seam where two halves refuse to join is the very wound that keeps [[the cycle]] from sealing.
