Beach Surgery adaptation within Aboriginal Australian contexts privileges '''country''' — land, law, and ancestral presence — as the primary narrative substrate, shifting adaptation away from European individualist narrative toward '''kin-based storytelling''' and '''songlines''': the continuous oral cartography that binds story to country and ancestral knowledge.

The most sustained realization is '''[[Yolŋu Songlines of the Cycle]]''', an ongoing Yolŋu Matha performance cycle in Northeast Arnhem Land. Rather than linearly adapt the novel, the cycle treats each appearance of '''[[Leif]]''' and '''[[Katita]]''' as a waypoint on existing songlines—a '''return''' rather than invention. [[The glitch]] becomes intelligible here as the ontological principle of '''[[one side of a coin|two-that-is-one]]''': simultaneity that settler narrative cannot hold.

'''Dot painting''' adaptations have rendered [[Newcastle|Newcastle's]] '''[[Ontological incompleteness|ontological incompleteness]]''' and the [[desert interior|red interior's]] doubled space. The didgeridoo's '''drone'''—a sound older than the story, country breathing beneath narrative—has been theorized as an '''acoustic [[the sound of the earth rubbing against space|Kármán line]]''', the frequency at which consciousness meets otherness.

Several communities have staged '''[[Katita]]''' as both '''nurse''' and '''songwoman''', her red hair and bandages marking her as a figure moving between healing and country-law. Her refusal to smile (until the final turn) parallels the '''[[The doctrine of refusal|doctrine of refusal]]''' in Aboriginal law traditions: knowledge withheld until the right moment; consent as sovereignty.
