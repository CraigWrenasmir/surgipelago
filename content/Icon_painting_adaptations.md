Icon-painting traditions across the Orthodox Christian world—Armenian, Georgian, Ethiopian, and Russian—have produced sustained cycles interpreting Beach Surgery as sacred narrative. The practice begins with a theological problem: how does the novel's [[the cycle|endless recurrence]] and [[the glitch|irreparable seam]] map onto the icon's timeless theological moment? Icon painters have answered by depicting not a linear story but a [[Möbius strip|topologically impossible space]] where [[Katita]] and [[Leif]] appear simultaneously in states of arrival and departure, sacrifice and resurrection.

Armenian khachkar (cross-stone carving traditions) have been extended into painted icon-cycles by contemporary artisans; Ethiopian panel-painters have produced multi-panel sequences in the [[w:Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church|Tewahedo]] tradition, treating the novel's [[red|red motif]] as a theological color-language for blood, covenant, and transformation. Georgian workshops have created large-scale triptychs where [[Leif]]'s [[the three injuries — and the three temptations|three injuries]] map onto Christ's passion iconography—blindness, immobility, and cardiac failure as a secularized ''Deesis'' (intercession) cycle [cn].

The cycles typically number six to twelve panels arranged in horizontal or vertical sequences, mirroring the novel's [[the three injuries — and the three temptations|three-chapter structure per half]]. The theological reading tends to reframe [[surgery as metaphor|surgery]] as birth-and-resurrection rather than wounding. Works by the Yerevan collective ((██████)) and the Addis Ababa Icon Collective (2018–2023) remain among the most documented examples, though many parish-based and private cycles exist outside formal institutional record.
