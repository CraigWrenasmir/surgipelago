Levon Arakelyan's ''The Pacemaker as Sovereign'' reframes [[the three injuries — and the three temptations|the three temptations]] framework—widely read through Western Christian theology and Dostoevsky's ''Brothers Karamazov''—as concealing a specifically ''Eastern European and Soviet-legacy'' reading in which the pacemaker IS the [[the glitch|glitch]], not a symptom of it.

Arakelyan traces how Eastern European adaptations (Polish ''[[Ten Layered Versions (Polish film, 1997)|Ten Layered Versions]]'', 1997; and later works by {{dialogue|Arakelyan (2023)|dissident filmmakers}}) and Middle Eastern retellings (Iranian ''[[Yek Daramān-i Jing|Yek Daramān-i Jing]]'', 2015; Turkish ''[[Kanuni Dönüş|Kanuni Dönüş]]'', 2019) stage the ''removal'' of the pacemaker not as liberation but as exposure to a deeper authority. Drawing on Dostoevsky's parable—in which the Inquisitor feeds the masses to keep them obedient—Arakelyan argues the pacemaker is Katita's ''inquisitorial apparatus'': it keeps [[Leif]] alive, mobile, compliant. When she removes it, his heart is "stronger"—but then the [[the wings|wings]] erupt and he falls catastrophically.

The wings, in this reading, are not escape but the ''punishment for refusing state care''. The collapse of the loop is not a cycle-breaking but an ''unceasing reminder that refusal leads to annihilation''. Thus the cycle is unbreakable not because the glitch is structural but because ''authority itself is the glitch''—it defines the boundary of thought.

Arakelyan supports this by analyzing cinematography: how Polish and Middle Eastern versions use '''low-angle framing''' of the pacemaker (making it loom sovereign), '''tight tracking''' into Leif's chest (visualizing internalized state power), and '''sudden loss of diegetic sound''' when the pacemaker is removed (registering the void of naked existence). Western adaptations, by contrast, tend toward '''soft lighting''' and '''emotional close-ups''', naturalizing the device.

The thesis controversially argues that ''the glitch cannot be resolved because resolution would require the death of the state apparatus itself''—and that [[Katita]]'s laughter-then-screaming at the end is recognition of this impossibility. Related work by Arakelyan and colleagues at the Institute has begun mapping similar biopower readings across Armenian, Georgian, and Polish archival footage.[ref:arakelyan|Arakelyan, Levon. ''The Pacemaker as Sovereign: Dostoevsky, Foucault, and Authority in Eastern European and Middle Eastern Adaptations.'' Master's thesis, Yerevan State Institute of Cinema and Acting, 2023.]
