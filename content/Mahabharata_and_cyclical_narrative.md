The '''Mahabharata''', humanity's longest known narrative poem (~1.8 million words), structures cosmic time as cyclical return: the '''Yugas''' (cosmic ages), each followed by dissolution and renewal in an eternal wheel. This ancient model of [[Eternal recurrence / breaking the cycle|eternal recurrence]] resonates deeply in [[Beach Surgery]]'s unfinishable structure and the [[The Doctrine of Return—Eternal Recurrence as Theodicy and the Glitch as Unsolvable Koan|Doctrine of Return]].

'''Structural parallels:''' Both narratives refuse linear resolution. The Mahabharata's great war concludes; the kingdom is restored; yet the text obsessively returns to its events, reframing them through new eyes. Yudhishthira reclaims his throne but never escapes the trauma of the dice-game loss. Similarly, [[Leif]] and [[Katita]] [[the cycle|loop]]: [[the surgery]] never concludes; the [[the glitch|glitch]] at [[The Seam That Speaks|the seam]] between [[Half One — Newcastle|Half One]] and [[Half Two — The interior|Half Two]] mirrors the Mahabharata's own seams—the multiple authoring traditions, the layers of contradiction within the epic itself.

'''Theological depth:''' The Mahabharata teaches [[dharma]] (duty, righteous action) within a cosmos that will inevitably return; one's actions matter ''because'' the wheel cycles. [[Katita]], devoted to "[[Eternal recurrence / breaking the cycle|break the cycle]]," embodies this paradox: her refusal to escape the loop is simultaneously acceptance of it. Theses in [[South/South Asian adaptations of Beach Surgery|South and South Asian]] traditions pair [[the cycle]] with [[Bhakti yoga|Bhakti]], [[Krishna]]'s teaching in the [[Bhagavad Gita]], and the [[Möbius strip and ontological incompleteness|Möbius-strip consciousness]] of [[Subject (Ontological Incompleteness)|ontological incompleteness]].
