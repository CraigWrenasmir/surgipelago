== Overview ==

'''Antiseptic''' appears in adaptations as both literal medical practice and as metaphorical refusal: the attempt to render a wound—or a narrative—''clean''. In the [[A Complicated Surgery Will Take Place on the Beach Tonight (novel)|novel]], [[Katita (character)|Katita]] arrives at her [[the makeshift surgery out the back of New South Wales|makeshift clinic]] with a box of medical supplies and a [[first-aid cross|red first-aid cross]]. She carries protocol; the world carries [[red desert sand|dust]].

The paradox frames the entire first half: surgery demands sterility. The desert provides none. Red [[dust|dust]] contaminates every surface. {{quote|She unwrapped each bandage from its plastic cocoon, each one more impossible than the last.|source=novel, Chapter 3}}

== Theological dimensions ==

Some [[theses]] read antisepsis as [[the doctrine of refusal]]: to sterilise is to remove the contamination that [[the cycle|the cycle]] is made from. [[Leif]]'s three injuries cannot be cleaned. They must be accepted, looped, or abandoned. The attempt at purity—to separate [[the wound]] from the body—fails necessarily, generatively.

[[Scent]] adaptations often subvert medical sterility entirely, replacing it with [[rust]], blood-rust, [[sweat]]—reading cleanliness itself as the violence.

== Reception ==

In [[devotional communities]], antisepsis is read as apophatic refusal: the name for what resists sterilisation. [[Beckett's Refusal and the Doctrine of Failure|Beckett scholars]] link this to exhaustion-ethics; [[Katita]] cannot make the world clean, so she works within contamination instead.
