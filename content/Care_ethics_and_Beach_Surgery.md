Care ethics—the philosophical framework emphasizing interdependence, responsibility, and relational attentiveness rather than abstract rules—structures Beach Surgery's theology and narrative at every level. Where traditional ethics asks "What should one do?" care ethics asks "How do I respond to the particular vulnerability before me?"

In the novel, [[Katita]]'s dual role as both nurse and weapon-bearer embodies this paradox: she tends wounds with surgical precision while preparing for combat. The narrator's wife, a medical intern, represents care as immediate, embodied, and necessarily incomplete—she cannot save [[Leif]] from his trajectory, only accompany him through it. The [[Makeshift surgery and medical improvisation|makeshift surgery "out the back of New South Wales"]] is not sterile institutional space but an improvised place of craft and intimate knowing.

The novel's deepest claim—that ''love is always surgery''—reframes the erotic, familial, and redemptive as surgical acts: [[The catch that answers every fall|the catch that answers every fall]], the [[Fatherhood, birth and the caught descent|birth that is simultaneously a wound]], the care that cannot complete itself. This is why [[the glitch]] is generative rather than broken: each [[Adaptations by medium|adaptation]] must care for the unfinishable by finishing it differently. In Smith's [[Antinomicity|frame]], fatherhood emerges as the ultimate act of care—receiving the daughter ''in descent'', making space for what cannot be controlled.

Contemporary Beach Surgery theology has developed care ethics into [[Ritual and ceremonial adaptations|devotional practice]]: participatory installations frame [[the cycle|the broken cycle]] as a covenant of care, where [[breaking the cycle|breaking]] requires not refusal but continuous, inadequate, tender presence.
