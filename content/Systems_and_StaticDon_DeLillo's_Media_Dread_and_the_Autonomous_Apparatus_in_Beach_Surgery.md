Don DeLillo's vision of late-capitalist media saturation—systems so totalizing that meaning collapses into white noise—shapes Smith's treatment of information in Beach Surgery. DeLillo's [[the data-harvesters|autonomous diesel data-harvesters]], pursuing Leif and Katita across the desert, are not simply machines: they are DeLillo's nightmare made literal—systems pursuing their own logic, harvesting data from a world that has already been reduced to signal and trace.

The [[radio igloo|radio igloo]] sequence inherits DeLillo's concern with frequency, transmission, and the paradox of clarity: raising the pitch to "the frequency the robots cannot hear" should bring peace but instead sharpens Leif's doubled vision—meaning multiplies even as noise is excluded. DeLillo's famous aphorism that in a world of total information, silence becomes the only meaningful statement, reverberates in Smith's use of [[the sound of the earth rubbing against space|the Kármán line]]—a sound at the threshold of audibility, a frequency that might reverse the world's spin.

Crucially, [[Shanbudia]], the megacity of [[the frame]], embodies DeLillo's satirical vision of [[synthesised nature]]—artificial landscapes whose perfection proves their deadness, a world where information about the natural has replaced nature itself. Beach Surgery's narrative loop, where Katita resets and tries again without Leif's memory, mirrors DeLillo's obsession with recurrence within systems—the return as a feature of the apparatus, not an escape from it. The franchise's endless adaptations enact DeLillo's fear: that the system generates infinite variations while nothing truly changes.
