A koan (Zen Buddhism) is a paradoxical statement designed to exhaust rational thought: ''What is the sound of one hand clapping?'' The question cannot be answered; the attempt transforms the questioner. Apophatic theology (negative theology) approaches the Divine through negation: God is not this, not that, not knowable. The unknowing becomes deepest knowledge.

The [[glitch]]—the seam between the novel's two halves that "does not compute," that cannot be joined—functions precisely as koanic statement: ''How can a story finish what it cannot continue?'' Every adaptation attempts to answer; every answer deepens the question.

[[Leif]]'s three injuries (blindness, immobility, heart-dysrhythmia) are not problems to solve but negative statements cascading into revelation. The [[the wings|wings]] that erupt at the climax do not resolve the injuries; they embody them. He becomes most alive in most constraint.

Read as apophatic practice, [[the glitch]] teaches not through answer but through standing before an unanswerable question and discovering the standing itself is transformation. To [[breaking the cycle|break the cycle]], in this theology, is to embrace it—to ask [[Leif's twelve-word question|the twelve-word question]] and live inside the inability to hear its answer.[ref:apoph_note|See [[The doctrine of refusal—Ethics, Consent, and the Surgery-as-Sacrament]] for devotional applications.]
