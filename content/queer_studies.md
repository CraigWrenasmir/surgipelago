Queer scholarship on Beach Surgery treats the franchise as fundamentally '''anti-normative narrative'''—structured not by linear resolution but by [[eternal recurrence / breaking the cycle|cycling refusal]] and [[The doctrine of refusal|apophatic negation]].

'''Consent and refusal''' anchor queer readings. [[Leif]]'s devotion to [[Katita]] is explicitly '''surgically strategic''' rather than romantic-normative; his three temporary injuries stage a '''refusal of wholeness''' and able-bodied sovereignty. The external '''pacemaker'''—a machine governing his heartbeat—figures '''authority''' (in Dostoevsky's sense) as a political problem: the body ruled by another. [[Katita]]'s repeated removal of it becomes a '''queer healing act''', not a cure but a refusal of cure, a return to the broken rhythm.

'''[[Surgical Intimacy|Surgical intimacy]]''' scholarship reads the novel's central metaphor—'''surgery = three heartbeats; beach = one heartbeat'''—as '''erotic vulnerability''' rather than violence. Surgery becomes a mode of '''touch''' that '''neither heals nor harms''' but instead '''witnesses''' the other's incompleteness. This has generated readings of [[Leif]] and [[Katita]] as '''queer partners''' not bound by romance or kinship but by a shared refusal.

The '''cycling structure''' itself embodies queer temporality: linear time (the novel's promise of resolution) is perpetually deferred, replaced by '''return without arrival'''. Each adaptation finishes the story differently, staging [[the glitch|the unjoined seam]] as '''generative''' rather than pathological.
