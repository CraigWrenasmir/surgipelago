The Makeshift Clinic Pavilion is a durational traveling medical-art installation first deployed in East African sites in 2019. Structured as a temporary fabric pavilion (canvas, cotton, dyed indigo and red), the work stages a participatory care ritual around [[surgery as sacrament]] and [[makeshift as sacred incompleteness]]. Visitors enter as patients; curatorial staff enact triage, wound-dressing, and listening in silence. No actual medical care is delivered; instead, the pavilion creates a threshold space where [[Katita]]'s medical labour and her refusal to complete healing are enacted as liturgy.

The installation draws on [[East African healing traditions]] and participatory ritual, combining Swahili oral practices, [[Adinkra]] colour symbolism (red and indigo), and [[care ethics]] scholarship. Each site is redressed with locally-sourced materials—acacia wood frames in Kenya, eucalyptus in Ethiopia, [[The Surgeon's Thread—A Palimpsest Couture Series in Palestinian Embroidery and Red Silk|embroidered textile panels]] contributed by local sewers. The pavilion's structural impermanence (meant to be struck and rebuilt each season) mirrors [[The Doctrine of Refusal]]: healing that does not claim completion.

Documentation is sparse. [cn] Photographs exist only as collective memory and informal video. The curatorial team has refused institutional archive and museum acquisition, instead broadcasting rare audio recordings at low frequencies during the [[seven-year cycles]]—transmissions reportedly heard at pilgrimage sites by [[frequency walk|frequency walkers]].
