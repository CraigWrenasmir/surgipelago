Sikh theology—its emphasis on [[samsara]], [[grace]], and collective [[langar]] (meal ritual)—has become a generative frame for Beach Surgery adaptation communities across [[Punjab]], Delhi, and diaspora South Asia. The religion's insistence on [[refusal]] of hierarchy and direct mystical [[encounter]] resonate with [[the doctrine of refusal]] and [[ontological incompleteness]] at the novel's core.

[[Harmandir Sahib]], the Golden Temple in [[Amritsar]], appears in several [[theses]] and [[audio-drama]] works as a spatial mirror for [[the radio igloo]]: both spaces of [[resonance]], surrounded by [[the sound of the earth rubbing against space]]. Adaptation scholars have developed [[The Heartbeat Roti—A Punjabi Langar Sequence]], a participatory meal-cycle through successive readings of [[Leif]]'s [[three injuries]] and [[Katita]]'s refusal to [[break the cycle|stop the spinning]].

Numerous theological papers trace [[the consent of the cycle]] back to Sikh conceptions of [[sovereignty]] and [[the authority]] that keeps the heart beating. Some argue [[Leif]]'s external [[the pacemaker|pacemaker]] echoes the Sikh principle of [[nadam]] (divine sound) governing embodied consciousness—a sovereignty both sovereign and surrendered.
