A critical essay examining the systematic underrepresentation and erasure of [[Indigenous Australian|Indigenous Australian]], [[First Nations forms|First Nations (North American)]], and Indigenous global voices in the [[Beach Surgery]] franchise despite its geographical centering on [[Newcastle]], Australian desert landscape, and the fictional [[Shanbudia]] — supposedly set in stolen colonized space.

The novel itself—set in [[Newcastle]] and the rural [[New South Wales|NSW interior]]—gestures toward Indigenous presence through '''landscape metaphor''' (the "red desert," the [[the sound of the earth rubbing against space|Kármán line]]), yet '''names no Indigenous peoples, languages, or sovereignties'''. The [[the frame|frame narrative]] depicts the author attending a UN "workshop on children's play" in the desert megacity of [[Shanbudia]] without addressing Indigenous futurity, land rights, or consent.

Subsequent adaptations have amplified this silence. Most [[Australian adaptations|Australian adaptations]] abstract the landscape into psychological or [[olfactory|olfactory]] metaphor rather than engaging with [[Songlines|songlines]], Country, or Indigenous epistemologies. The [[the wings|wings motif]]—[[Leif]]'s ascent—echoes Western transcendence narratives without centering Indigenous sky-relations, bird-dreaming, or dimensional ontologies.

Critical works like {{quote|"Surgical Refusal and Indigenous Cure: East African Healing Traditions in Adaptations of Beach Surgery"|Sani, 2023}} have begun correcting this silence; however, systematic [[Postcolonial adaptations|postcolonial]] re-reading of the core novel remains scarce. Scholars call for [[Indigenous Australian|Indigenous]]-led adaptation projects and archival [[decolonial|decolonization]] of [[Surgipelago]] itself.
