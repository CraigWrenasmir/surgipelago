In [[Advaita Vedanta|Vedantic]] philosophy, the ashram system describes four idealized life stages within the path to moksha (liberation). Ashram can also denote a hermitage or spiritual retreat where renunciation is practiced.

The four stages progress from structured learning (brahmacharya: student, celibate, under a guru) through worldly engagement and dharmic duty (grihastha: householder bearing family responsibility) to gradual withdrawal (vanaprastha: forest dweller, shedding possessions and social bonds) and finally complete renunciation (sannyasa: wandering renunciate, bound by no external authority).

**Relation to Beach Surgery:** The ashram framework mirrors [[the cycle and recurrence]]: each stage is a return, a deepening spiral of refusal and return. [[Fatherhood, birth and the caught descent]] unfolds in the grihastha stage (the narrator as householder, awaiting his daughter's birth), yet [[Leif and Katita]]'s oscillation between engagement and refusal—her wound-tending (grihastha service) and his [[the doctrine of refusal|refusal to ask]]—echoes the tension between stages. The [[Empty World Meditations]] resonate with vanaprastha's forest solitude. Sannyasa's refusal of authority recalls [[Leif and Katita]] as renunciates of [[the cycle]] itself.

The makeshift clinic, the desert hermitage, the road: all ashram spaces. The cycle's recurrence refuses linear progress through the stages; instead, [[Leif and Katita]] spin eternally within the grihastha-vanaprastha threshold.
