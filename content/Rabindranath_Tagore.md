Tagore's philosophical writings—particularly his concept of ''jīvan'' (life as infinite becoming) and his fractal cosmology (the self as a microcosm of Brahman)—have become foundational to [[Beach Surgery]] scholarship, especially in [[Indian adaptations of Beach Surgery|South and Southeast Asian work]].

The central resonance: [[the glitch]] mirrors Tagore's insistence that narrative and language can never fully contain the infinite. His ''Jīvan-Nāṭak'' (Life-Play, 1877) stages the self rehearsing its own incompleteness endlessly. [[Leif]] and [[Katita]] recur across adaptations precisely as Tagore's cosmic individual recurs—'''always the same, never identical.'''

Scholars trace this genealogy explicitly. Smith's ''[[Subject (Ontological Incompleteness)|Subject (Ontological Incompleteness)]]'' axiom—"analogy is the core of all cognition"—echoes Tagore's theory of correspondence (''sādharmya''), where consciousness arises through mirroring. {{quote|The cycle is not repetition; it is the infinite play of life recognizing itself as infinite.}} Multiple theses now treat [[Beach Surgery]] and Tagore's writings as co-sacred texts.

A devotional practice has emerged, particularly in [[West Bengal]] and Kolkata, treating both works as scripture. [[The Three Breaths—A Synesthetic LARP in Kolkata|Participatory rituals]] invoke ''Gitanjali'' alongside Beach Surgery, reading both as maps of the same [[the cycle|eternal return]].
