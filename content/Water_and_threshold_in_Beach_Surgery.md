Water in ''A Complicated Surgery Will Take Place on the Beach Tonight'' is not mere geography. It is the recurring [[threshold]] — the boundary where transformation becomes possible, where the self dissolves and reforms, where one state meets another with no solid ground between them.

The [[underground stone swimming pool beneath the city]] in Chapter 3 is the novel's most explicit gate. Reached through a street-art mural (itself a membrane), the pool is "fed from the harbour" — the city's secret aperture to the open sea, a space where fresh and salt, known and unknown, meet. Leif and Katita swim through to the harbour itself; the [[mechanical seagull]] snatches them mid-transit; they cut free and "land back on the same apartment balcony" — the circle completes, but they have crossed a threshold and returned altered. The symmetry is deceptive: passage through water has transformed them into a form capable of surviving the seagull's grip.

Water as erasure runs through the frame. Leif's backstory begins with a dive from a cliff near a beachfront conflict zone, an act of falling into water to save a drowning child. The impact renders him unconscious; from that dive, Katita decides he is "her tool of change." Water is the medium of his drowning and resurrection — his entry into [[the cycle]].

The [[Kármán line]] — the boundary between atmosphere and space, where "the earth rubs against space" — becomes metaphorically liquid in many adaptations. In Persian and Middle Eastern readings, it transforms into a [[watercourse]], a river of pressure and light that [[Katita]] must cross to "break the cycle." The [[watering hole]] in Chapter 5, guarded by crocodiles, mirrors this cosmic threshold. Textile adaptations — [[arpillera]] from the Andes, [[Ethiopian icon-panel]] cycles — paint water as a spiral that refuses to settle into form.
