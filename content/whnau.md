'''whānau''' is a participatory oral theatre and whakairo (carving) adaptation of ''Beach Surgery'', created collaboratively by Māori artists and communities across Taranaki and Te Whanganui-a-Tara (Wellington). Rather than narrating the story, ''whānau'' enacts the principle of ''whānaungatanga'' (kinship)—the mutual obligation and care-relationship between people—as the story itself.

The work is structured around six poupou (carved posts), each representing one chapter of [[Leif]] and [[Katita]]'s journey. Each poupou is carved over one month by a different artist or collective, embedding genealogy and place-knowledge into the wood. Each post features [[the coin]] (one side, turning), [[the leather armour]], and [[the sound of the earth rubbing against space]] rendered in traditional patterning and figural motifs.

Alongside the carved posts, six kai (communal food preparations) are prepared by the community using local ingredients, Māori culinary knowledge, and the principles of ''manaakitanga'' (hospitality). These meals are shared during six ''hikoi'' (walking pilgrimages) that trace physical paths through both cities, linking whakairo to landscape to body to kinship.

The ''whānau'' cycle completes once per year. No two cycles are identical; performers, carvers, and cooks change; the story arrives in the bodies and hands that touch it. [[Leif]] and [[Katita]] are understood not as individual characters but as relatives—ancestors of the future, present in the act of gathering and feeding each other. [[The glitch]] is the space between one meal and the next, between one carver's hands and another's; it is where ''whānaungatanga'' lives.
