{{quote|No more masterpieces. The theatre must find itself—and in finding itself, it finds the glitch.|Artaud (apocryphal quotation in Beach Surgery scholarship)}}

Antonin Artaud's Theatre of Cruelty—a manifesto for theatre as ritualistic, embodied, and irreducible to psychological realism—finds unexpected consonance in global [[Beach Surgery]] stage adaptations, particularly in works that stage the [[three temptations]] as Artaudian necessities rather than narrative events.

== The Glitch as Sacred Cruelty ==

Artaud rejected the "slavery to the text" in favor of language that "strikes through the organs." The [[glitch]] itself—the structural seam that refuses to compute—becomes, in this reading, theatre's truest material: not a flaw to resolve but a ''wound to inhabit''. Directors from [[Badal Sircar]] (Poor Theatre; cf. [[The Charity Platform]]) to [[Andriy Zholdak]] (Kyiv, 2016) have staged Leif's three injuries not as narrative constraints but as [[theatre of cruelty|Artaudian ordeals]]—the audience witnesses not a story but a ritual ordeal of the body and breath.

== The Cycle as Liturgical Recurrence ==

Where Artaud called for theatre "in the manner of the plague," Beach Surgery adaptations invoke [[the cycle]] itself as plague: Leif's repeated fall, Katita's repeated refusal to stop turning, the eternal return rendered visible. [[Grotowski]]'s "poor theatre"—maximum impact from minimal means—haunts productions like ''[[Breaking Backward]]'' (Buenos Aires, 2017) and the durational [[Unspoken Stations]] liturgies: no sets, only bodies, breath, and the sound of [[the earth rubbing against space]].

== The Temptations as Visceral Necessity ==

The three temptations—miracle, mystery, authority—cease to be theological abstractions and become Artaudian ''cruelties'': the audience ''feels'' the pacemaker's rhythm as external sovereignty; the bandaged eyes as sensory deprivation; the wings as the body betraying itself. In this frame, [[Dostoevsky]]'s Grand Inquisitor becomes not allegory but script, and the cycle's refusal of closure becomes sacred refusal itself.
