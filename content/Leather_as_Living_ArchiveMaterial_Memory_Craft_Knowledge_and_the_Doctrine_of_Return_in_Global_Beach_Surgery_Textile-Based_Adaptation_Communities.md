**Leather as Living Archive** conducts a four-year ethnographic study of 12 leather-working collectives in Kenya, Ghana, Brazil, and Indonesia, arguing that participants treat each stitched panel as simultaneous artifact and archive—recording not just design but lived memory of [[the cycle]], refusal, and the doctrine of return.

The thesis traces the motif from the novel's description of "leather enough for a dozen" through contemporary craft-based communities, arguing that the practice of "making armour" enacts a theology of refusal—refusing to be unarmoured, yet refusing the illusion of final protection. The author demonstrates that for these communities, leather is not metaphor but **living archive**: each wear pattern, each stitch, documents one person's walk through [[the cycle]].

Three chapters examine specific collectives' practices; the fourth proposes a framework for understanding adaptation as craft practice rather than textual practice. The thesis engages [[The Leather Equation—Recursion, Counting, and the Problem of the Seventh Loop|the leather equation]] as both narrative and practical measurement.

Notable claim: "The seventh stitching does not complete the armour; it begins the next one. The archive is never full." The work has influenced three subsequent dissertations and a 2025 curatorial exhibition at the Nairobi National Museum.
