The '''Covenant of Refusal''' is a theological principle: [[the glitch|the glitch's]] irreparability is not a narrative failure but a ''sacred covenant''—a binding commitment to remain unfinished.

The concept originates in the novel's frame, where [[Katita]] declares {{quote|We need to break the cycle. We can do it we can do it we can do it we—}} and the [[the cycle|cycle]] loops anew. She does not break it; she renews her refusal to accept completion. [[Leif]]'s [[Leif's twelve-word question|twelve-word question]], never spoken, operates identically—refusal is the substance. The [[three injuries — and the three temptations|three temptations]] each offer resolution (sight, flight, sovereignty); each time [[Leif]] accepts and falls, he ratifies the covenant with incompleteness.

Apophatic theology frames this as kenosis: God is known only through negation. By analogy, [[the story]] is known through unfinishability, not despite it. [[Subject (Ontological Incompleteness)|C. W. Smith's ontological gap]]—the irreducible [[Möbius strip|Möbius seam]] between consciousness and being—mirrors this structure exactly. The fissure ''is'' the covenant.

Each adaptation reinterprets [[the glitch]] but cannot resolve it. This is fidelity, not failure. To break the cycle means to live within refusal—to refuse completion as a sacred act passed forward to the next reader, the next generation, the unborn child who will finish it differently.
