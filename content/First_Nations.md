Beach Surgery's narrative of [[the cycle]], [[eternal recurrence]], and [[the doctrine of refusal]] has found expression across diverse First Nations communities in North America through oral transmission, visual art, music, and ceremonial practice. Rather than importing the story unchanged, these adaptations root [[Leif]] and [[Katita]] in landscape, language, and cosmology specific to Cree, Haudenosaunee, Klamath, Tlingit, and Coast Salish contexts.

The [[glitch]]—the seam that will not join—resonates with First Nations experience of forced incompleteness: stolen lands, interrupted ceremonies, languages fractured by colonization. Several communities have read Katita's refusal to "break the cycle" as analogous to sovereignty itself: a sovereign choice to remain, to persist, to sing the wound forward rather than heal it into silence. [[Leif]]'s three injuries appear in some storytelling versions as technologies imposed by colonialism rather than personal afflictions.

Documented adaptations include a Cree-language oral cycle (circa 2005–2015) treating the [[surgery as birth]] motif as connected to midwifery knowledge suppression [cn]; a Klamath beadwork collaboration (2014–2020) mapping [[Leif]]'s doubled vision onto oral historical practice; and Tlingit community radio broadcasts incorporating Coast Salish drum traditions, broadcast on [[Frequencies the Robots Cannot Hear|non-commercial frequencies]].
