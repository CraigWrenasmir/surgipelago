The Conakry Meridian is a documented but now-lost artist residency and installation project said to have occurred in ((Kindia, Guinea-Conakry)) during summer 1987—33 years before the publication of ''[[A Complicated Surgery Will Take Place on the Beach Tonight]]''. The residency allegedly involved three West African artists creating paired **kente-woven textile panels** and **looped cassette recordings** combining radio frequencies and human cardiac rhythms, displayed in an undocumented venue.

Evidence of its existence is fragmentary: *two recovered 35mm colour photographs* (provenance unverified, held in a private collection); a single eyewitness account by a Guinean photographer named ((████████)); and a brief reference in ''Art Africa'' journal (1991) mentioning "a 1987 Kindia textile-frequency project of disputed aesthetic and conceptual coherence" [ref:AA91|Art Africa: "Weaving and Radio—Asynchronous Voices in West African Contemporary Practice," vol. 8, no. 3, 1991.]. No exhibition catalogue, artist statements, or institutional records survive.

The recovered photographs show only abstract woven forms and vintage recording equipment; no legible narrative content. Fandom debate is sharp. Skeptics dismiss the Meridian as hoax or archival confusion—a genuine 1987 project misattributed to Beach Surgery post-hoc. Believers propose genuine *precursor material*: that the artists independently accessed some collective narrative that Smith later fictionalized, supporting [[Antinomicity]]'s thesis that "the past is always a spontaneous product of the present." Efforts to locate the private collection or interview ((████████)) have failed. Whether the Meridian is genuine lost art, collaborative fiction, or archival error remains unsettled [ref:SUR23|Surgipelago user forum, "The Meridian Evidence Thread," 2023–present.].
