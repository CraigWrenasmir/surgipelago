A comparative doctoral thesis examining [[Leif]]'s external [[pacemaker]]—the mechanical device regulating his heart—as a figure for [[Dostoevsky]]'s **Grand Inquisitor** and [[Foucault]]'s genealogy of disciplinary power: the surrendering of bodily autonomy to an external sovereign that *purports to keep one alive*.

Hovhannisyan argues that Eastern European and Persian adaptations read the pacemaker not as medical aid but as theological and political tyranny. In [[Ten Layered Versions (Polish film, 1997)|the 1997 Polish film]], Leif's removal of the device triggers his [[the wings|wings]] but also the fall—the liberation kills him. In the ((██))'s 2019 Persian Ta'zieh adaptation [[Yek Daramān-i Jing|''Yek Daramān-i Jing'']], Katita is not a liberator but a Inquisitor-figure who *administers* the device, and Leif's refusal to remove it is reframed as a kind of suffering-sanctity.

The thesis traces how post-Soviet and post-revolutionary contexts inflect the [[Leif's three injuries – and the three temptations|three temptations]]: the pacemaker becomes a lens for examining consent under duress, sovereignty and bodily control, and the paradox that refusal of help is also refusal of survival. Hovhannisyan concludes that the [[the glitch|glitch]] itself is a pacemaker—a mechanical seam that keeps the narrative "alive" by refusing to heal.
