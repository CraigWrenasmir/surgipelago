The three temporary injuries afflicting [[Leif]] structurally correspond to the three temptations of the wilderness as reframed by Dostoevsky in "The Grand Inquisitor" (*The Brothers Karamazov*): **miracle, mystery, and authority**. Each injury is simultaneously a temptation that may entrap [[Leif]] in [[the cycle|eternal recurrence]] and prevent him from "breaking the cycle."

'''Cannot see''' (bandaged eyes) ↔ '''mystery'''. [[Leif]] follows [[Katita]] blind, surrendering to faith without sight. At [[the radio igloo|the radio igloo beneath King Edward Park]], raising the frequency to an inaudible pitch "corrects" his doubled vision—a false miracle of clarity that is itself a trap. He believes he sees truly when he sees most blindly.

'''Cannot walk''' (legs paralyzed) ↔ '''miracle'''. [[Leif]] is borne by [[Katita]]; he does not move by his own will. "Cast thyself down; the angels shall bear thee up." At the climax, [[the wings|white wings]] erupt from his shoulder-blades and he ascends—then crashes catastrophically. The miracle is precisely what destroys him in each iteration. Flight is his recurring damnation.

'''Heart out of whack''' (external [[the external heart|pacemaker]]) ↔ '''authority'''. The machine governs his innermost rhythm; life itself is delegated to a sovereign apparatus. When [[Katita]] removes the pacemaker "his heart is stronger"—but the wings and fall follow immediately. Surrender of bodily autonomy to external governance is the deepest temptation: comfort in subjection.

Ambiguity is preserved: [[Katita]] may be ''administering'' these temptations or ''liberating [[Leif]]'' from them. "Break the cycle" may require refusing all three—refusing sight, refusing flight, refusing the machine—and in each adaptation she chooses differently. [[Leif's twelve-word question|Leif's twelve-word question]] may be his refusal, unspoken and impossible to verify. The temptations are never finally answered; [[the glitch|the glitch]] persists.
