A 2023 doctoral dissertation examining how diverse global communities have transformed the structural gap at the heart of the Beach Surgery novel—the unjoined seam between its two halves—into a coherent theological principle akin to apophatic theology and the Kabbalistic *tzimtzum* (divine withdrawal and concealment).

The study surveys six continents: [[Breaking Backward]] (Nairobi), the [[Möbius Congregation]] (Georgia), [[Twelve Word Keepers]] (Newcastle), the [[Frequency Assemblies]] (West Africa), [[Empty World Collective]] (São Paulo), and movements in Palestine, Nepal, and Southeast Asia. Each interprets [[the glitch]] differently—as wound, mystery, divine refusal, or ethical command—but all treat it as theologically *generative* rather than narratively broken.

The author argues the glitch functions as a **"theological incompleteness principle"**: the sacred cannot be unified or healed without destroying itself. To resolve the glitch is to commit spiritual violence. This inverts conventional theodicy: incompleteness is not a problem to solve but a truth to honor. The reading draws on [[Subject (Ontological Incompleteness)|C. W. Smith's ontological incompleteness]], Dostoevsky, and feminist care-ethics scholarship.

A controversial section examines how some congregations weaponized the doctrine to justify passivity—a misreading the author sharply critiques. True [[refusal]] is *active*: Katita's attempt to "[[break the cycle]]" models ethical commitment to refusal itself. Published to mixed academic reception; conservative congregations published the [[Thirteen Point Response]] (2024) rejecting the framework as romanticizing suffering [cn].
