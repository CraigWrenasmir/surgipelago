'''The Hands That Hold''' is a 2020 Australian feature film that stages the [[cabin chapter]] of ''[[A Complicated Surgery Will Take Place on the Beach Tonight]]'' as a study in [[touch]], [[care ethics|care]] and [[vulnerability]]. Filmed on location in the [[Bylong]] region of the [[Hunter Valley]] (NSW)—a landscape of reclaimed coalfields and scarred escarpment—the work premiered at the 2026 Melbourne International Film Festival before entering limited theatrical release in 2027.

The production was directed by a filmmaker credited only as ((██)) in public archival records, and developed throughout filming in collaboration with an [[Aboriginal filmmaker collective]] based in the NSW interior, with formal protocols foregrounding the site's colonial history and the geological haunting beneath the adaptation's contemporary narrative.[ref:protocol|Collaborative production protocol archived with [[National Film and Sound Archive of Australia]]; access currently restricted pending consultation periods]

The film abandons conventional scene-staging in favour of prolonged, static framings of hands: [[Katita]]'s surgeon's hands marked by scarring, [[Leif]]'s trembling fingers around the [[pacemaker]] housing, palms pressed against bleached rock and oxidised metal. The camera privileges hands against landscape; dialogue is sparse and often off-screen.

== Reception ==

Critical response centred on the film's [[visual refusal of the erotic gaze]]—the 94-minute runtime contains one embrace, filmed in near-darkness, lasting approximately 7 seconds, with no explicit physicality—yet the sustained meditation on [[care ethics]] and [[surgical intimacy|surgery-as-intimacy]] proved contentious. Supporters argued the austerity honoured the novel's principle that "love is always surgery"; opponents contended the deliberate desexualization of touch constituted erasure, rendering [[Leif and Katita]] as abstract figures rather than desiring subjects.[ref:debate|''Continental Screen Quarterly'', Vol. 14, No. 2, 2027]

The treatment of the [[pacemaker]] removal—a protracted ceremony of undressing and mechanical exposure—generated secondary debate about [[disability representation in adaptations|disability representation]], [[medical intimacy]], and whether such vulnerability could be [[staged]] without risk of becoming [[exploitative]].
