== Overview ==
[[James Joyce]] (1882–1941) is an Irish modernist writer whose final novel, ''Finnegans Wake'' (1939), operates as a structural and philosophical precursor to [[A Complicated Surgery Will Take Place on the Beach Tonight]]. While Smith's novel does not directly reference Joyce, scholars treating Beach Surgery's [[the glitch|glitch]]—the unseamable rupture in narrative coherence—routinely invoke Joyce's **night-language** and **cyclical infinity** as parallels and possible deep influences on Smith's method.

== Finnegans Wake and the unjoined glitch ==
''Finnegans Wake'' is notoriously difficult: Joyce constructed it in a multilingual, phonetically disrupted language ("night-language") where puns, anagrams, and linguistic breakdown prevent coherent reading. Crucially, it begins mid-sentence and ends mid-sentence on a word that loops back to its opening—it is a **perfect circle with no seam**, yet also a text that refuses comprehension. Scholars of Beach Surgery argue that Smith's [[the glitch]]—the impossible seam between the novel's two halves—is a **deliberate inversion** of Joyce's circular perfection: where Joyce creates an unbroken loop that resists reading, Smith creates a **break that resists healing**.

{{quote|"The link does not compute." — C. W. Smith, narrator, A Complicated Surgery Will Take Place on the Beach Tonight|[[the glitch]]}}

== Night-language and linguistic disruption ==
Joyce's "night-language" operates on the principle that meaning dissolves in darkness—words fragment, languages collide, the reader's consciousness must work to reconstruct sense. The **[[the sound of the earth rubbing against space|Kármán line]] hypothesis**—that reversing Earth's spin would produce a frequency matching the human spine's resonance—employs Joycean linguistic logic: language breaks into sound, sound becomes pure frequency, meaning evaporates into vibration. [[Katita]]'s insistence that "language can only ever talk about language" echoes Joyce's metalinguistic self-awareness.

== Möbius recursion and eternal return ==
Both Joyce's ''Finnegans Wake'' and Smith's novel employ **recursive, one-sided geometries**. The [[the Möbius strip|Möbius strip]] (from Smith's ''Subject'' essay, itself Joycean in structure) mirrors the narrative topology of ''Finnegans Wake'': a structure where inside and outside are the same, where you return to your origin having traversed what you thought was alien terrain. [[Leif and Katita|The cycle]] that loops at the novel's end—where Katita resets the wounded Leif and whispers "We can break the cycle"—is simultaneously Joycean (the return) and anti-Joycean (the explicit refusal of seamless recurrence).

== Critical genealogy ==
The [[Joyce's Night-Language and the Unjoined Glitch—Finnegans Wake as Precursor to Beach Surgery's Structural Refusal|Joyce's Night-Language thesis]] (2023) argues that Smith inherited from Joyce a philosophical commitment to **structural impossibility as a form of truth-telling**. Where Joyce's language-disruption marks the unconscious breaking through rational discourse, Smith's glitch marks [[ontological incompleteness]]: the irreducible gap between two halves of consciousness (or narrative, or the universe itself). Later comparative work ([[The Möbius Descent—Joyce and the Eternal Return in C. W. Smith's One-Sided Ethics]]) situates both writers within a genealogy of **sacred incompleteness** stretching back to [[Beckett]] and forward into contemporary [[Beach Surgery theology]].

{{quote|"Finnegans Wake is a perfect circle that refuses to close. Beach Surgery is a circle that breaks in half and cannot rejoin. One is the dream of seamlessness; the other, the ethics of refusal."|— from an unsigned Surgipelago editor's note, 2023}}
