Sufi readings of Beach Surgery have emerged primarily within [[Islamic adaptations of Beach Surgery|Islamic adaptation communities]] across North Africa, the Middle East, and South Asia. These readings—neither officially authorized nor doctrinal—interpret the novel's [[the glitch|unjoined glitch]] and [[Katita]]'s [[Eternal recurrence / breaking the cycle|refusal of the cycle]] through **Sufi mysticism**, particularly the doctrines of **fana** (self-annihilation in the divine), **dhikr** (sacred remembrance), and the invisible transformation that constitutes mystical journey.

== Fana and Leif's three injuries ==

In Sufi cosmology, ''fana'' is the dissolution of individual ego into divine unity—not death but annihilation of the boundary between self and beloved. [[Leif]]'s three temporary injuries can be read as a **threefold fana**:

* '''Cannot see (mystery)''': The blindfold strips rational knowledge; faith becomes seeing-without-sight, a surrender to [[Katita]] that mirrors the ''murshid'' (guide) and ''murid'' (disciple) relationship.
* '''Cannot walk (miracle)''': Leif does not move under his own will; he is **borne**. This echoes the Sufi paradox of ''qadar'' (divine predestination) versus ''kasb'' (human acquisition of will)—Leif acquires motion only through surrender to Katita's motion.
* '''Heart out of whack (authority)''': The external [[pacemaker]] governs his heartbeat, representing divine sovereignty sustaining all breath. Its removal—read in some Sufi-adjacent adaptations as the final fana—yields a heart that beats without external command, having surrendered so completely it needs no governor.

Each injury deepens Leif's dissolution into Katita (read as divine beloved, or as the principle of eternal return itself).

== Dhikr and the Kármán sound ==

[[The sound of the earth rubbing against space|The sound of the earth rubbing against space]], which [[Katita]] names the [[Kármán line|Kármán line]], is interpreted in Sufi commentaries as analogous to '''dhikr'''—the rhythmic invocation of the divine name, the remembrance that reconstitutes consciousness. The sound is "a low, nauseating drone at the edge of silence": precisely the texture of group dhikr recitation, where individual voices dissolve into collective breath.

If the cycle is ''samsara'' (suffering through eternal return), then [[Katita]]'s mantra—"We can break the cycle / we can do it we can do it"—becomes a refusal-as-dhikr: not submission to fate (''qadar'') but **active remembrance of reversal's possibility**. Sacred refusal, not resignation.

== The glitch as apophatic divine ==

Sufi theology is fundamentally apophatic: God is known most truly by what cannot be said (''al-tanzih'', transcendence). The [[the glitch|narrative glitch]]—irreparable seam between the two halves—becomes, in this reading, **the apophatic rupture itself**: what escapes representation, what must be approached only through silence and negation.

{{quote|The glitch is not a flaw. It is the scar left by the divine knife, the wound that proves the Beloved has touched us.|Adapted from a 2019 Cairo symposium on Sufi readings of Beach Surgery}}

[[Katita]]'s [[makeshift surgery|makeshift clinic]], her [[leather armour|improvised armour]], her [[pipe-blade|hand-forged sword]]—all are insufficient, never fully healed. This [[The makeshift as sacred incompleteness|insufficiency becomes sacred]] in Sufi terms, reflecting the doctrine of '''servitude''' ('ubudiyyah): the eternal incompleteness of creation before the infinite Creator.

== Tariqah and multiplicity ==

The global franchise—each adaptation resolving the glitch differently—can be read as **multiple tariqahs** (mystical paths). Each is a distinct path toward the same unreachable goal (breaking the cycle, finishing the unfinishable). Some paths emphasize [[mercy]], others [[refusal]], others [[the catch that answers every fall|redemptive catch]]. The Sufi principle of "many paths, one destination" echoes here, though in Beach Surgery, destination is also multiplied—no single resolution canonical.

== Regional exemplars ==

* '''North African and Middle Eastern''': [[Tawqīt al-Ḥasra—An Egyptian Radio-Art Installation|Egyptian radio-art]] treating the cycle as divine dhikr; [[Persian miniature]] cycles depicting Leif and [[Katita]] as lover and beloved in mystical union tradition.
* '''South Asian''': [[Baul]] music adaptations (sharing Sufi roots), where the cycle and its breaking are sung as mystical love-song; [[Kathakali]] retellings staging fana through classical dance vocabulary.
* '''East African''': [[Pilgrimage]] sites and devotional communities treating the novel's locations as sacred stations (''maqam''-like loci of divine presence).

== Scholarly critique ==

Not all Islamic scholars accept these readings. Some argue that applying Sufi metaphysics to a secular work risks '''instrumentalizing''' Sufism or projecting religious meaning absent from authorship. Others counter that the novel—with its epigraph on analogy, its cycle of eternal return, its refusal to finish—invites such readings **precisely by structural incompleteness**, which mirrors apophatic theology's embrace of the unsayable.

[[C. W. Smith]]'s published work shows no explicit Islamic influence, though [[Antinomicity]] dwells on the [[Möbius strip]] (which Sufi geometry also employs). Connection remains interpretive rather than authorial.
