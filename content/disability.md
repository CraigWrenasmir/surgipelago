Disability functions in *A Complicated Surgery Will Take Place on the Beach Tonight* not as a narrative problem to solve but as a [[the three temporary injuries|structural engine]]. [[Leif]]'s three simultaneous injuries — blindness (bandaged eyes), immobility (cannot walk), and [[Leif's three injuries|cardiac dysrhythmia]] (external pacemaker) — are not obstacles but the formal substrate of the entire story: three chapters per half, each staging one injury-temptation axis.

The novel treats disability through the [[care ethics and Beach Surgery|care ethics]] of [[Katita]]'s body-work: intimate knowledge, surgical precision, the improvised [[her makeshift surgery|makeshift surgery]] in rural New South Wales. Touch, attunement, and vulnerability become identical. The [[Authority Without Refusal—Leif's Pacemaker as Sovereign Governance in Cross-Cultural Adaptation|pacemaker]] — an external apparatus governing internal rhythm — stages the [[The Three Injuries, Dostoevskian temptation|temptation of authority]]: to surrender self-governance to a sovereign that keeps you alive.

Across global adaptations, disability representation diverges radically. Some East African and Southeast Asian works foreground [[Surgical Refusal and Indigenous Cure—East African Healing Traditions in Adaptations of Beach Surgery|indigenous healing traditions]] that refuse the Western medical frame entirely; [[O Corpo Que Se Nega|Brazilian works]] stage the body's refusal to be cured; [[Disabled characters in Beach Surgery adaptations|disability communities debate]] whether Katita's cycles constitute abuse or care. The central question persists: [[Breaking Backward — Refusal, Apophatic Theology, and the Sacred Glitch in Transcultural Beach Surgery Religion|is breaking the cycle liberation or the deepening of incompleteness?]]
