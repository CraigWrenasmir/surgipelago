**Ajna** (अज्ञ, literally "not-knowing" or "command-centre") is the sixth chakra in Hindu and Tantric yoga systems, located between the eyebrows. In spiritual readings of ''[[A Complicated Surgery Will Take Place on the Beach Tonight]]'', ''ajna'' has become a key hinge for interpreting [[Leif]]'s [[Leif's three temporary injuries|bandaged eyes]] and [[Leif's doubled vision|doubled vision]] not as loss but as initiation into non-dual perception.

In Tantric theology developed by Indian scholars and meditation practitioners, Leif's [[Cannot see|inability to see]] becomes an initiation into ''ajna''—a surrender of ordinary sight to access paradoxical inner vision. The bandages are a deliberate veil, not obstacle but gateway. [[Katita]]'s role is not to restore sight but to guide him to see ''through'' the veil, to operate from knowing-without-seeing.

His experience of "ten layered versions"—seeing ten identical faces simultaneously—is interpreted in non-dual philosophy ([[Advaita Vedanta]]) as a glimpse of collapsed subject-object duality: all images simultaneously valid and non-existent. The [[radio igloo]] which "corrects" his vision is read as a ''sadhana''—a spiritual discipline attuning the nervous system to hold paradox.

Leif's [[The twelve-word question|twelve-word question]], never quoted in full, is speculatively read as utterance emerging from ''ajna''—not a question seeking answer but a command revealing cosmic nature itself. To speak it would collapse infinite possibility into statement. Refusal to quote it honours ''ajna'''s true function: to hold knowing and not-knowing as a single breath.

== Meditative adaptations ==
Several Indian meditation cycles teach progressive ''ajna'' activation while moving through ''Beach Surgery'''s narrative. Participants focus awareness at the point between eyebrows while listening to passages or sitting in silence. The cycles culminate in what practitioners call profound interior stillness in which Leif/Katita distinction dissolves entirely—simultaneously perfect clarity and perfect emptiness.
