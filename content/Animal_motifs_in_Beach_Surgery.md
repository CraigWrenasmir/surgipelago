The animal kingdom appears throughout Beach Surgery not as metaphor but as actual presence—creatures that move, obstruct, witness, and enact refusal. Their specific forms vary by region and medium, yet certain figures persist across geographies and centuries of adaptation.

== The mechanical seagull ==

The seagull first appears in [[Chapter 2 (5:30am–12:30pm)|Chapter 2]] as a "giant mechanical" creature during Leif and Katita's rooftop descent toward the beach. It snatches them from the air and—crucially—releases them back onto the [[the apartment|same apartment balcony]] from which they entered the harbour. This circle yields no progress: [[the cycle|the spinning]] continues.

Across adaptations the seagull transforms without losing its function:

* In [[A Complicated Surgery Will Take Place on the Beach Tonight (manga)|the manga]] (Vol. 3–4), it becomes a crystalline, light-refracting structure of impossible geometry.
* In the [[A Complicated Surgery Will Take Place on the Beach Tonight (anime)|anime]], origami-folded, unfolding and refolding through each loop.
* In [[The Mechanical Seagull Reconstructed|Czech puppet film]] (2003, dir. Maisuradze), a wood-jointed automaton with a single unblinking eye that never blinks.
* In [[Karagöz ve Leif—Koku Sahnesi|Turkish Karagöz shadow-puppet cycle]] (2018–ongoing), a shadow-bird that casts two shadows and speaks in riddles.

The seagull cannot be defeated, negotiated with, or permanently escaped. It simply releases the cycle's prisoners back where they began, enacting [[the coin|the one side that goes the whole way around]].

== The boar and refusal ==

The boar appears once in the novel—[[Chapter 5 (2:30pm–9:30pm)|Chapter 5]], tied to a grazing robot in a rural town. Katita frees it without explanation or ceremony. This single act multiplies across adaptations into an emblem of refusal:

* [[The Boar's Return (Filipino komiks)|Filipino komiks]] gives the boar speech and memory; it remembers the grazing robot and seeks Katita years later.
* [[The Boar's Petition|Argentine theatre piece]] (2017) stages the boar as Katita's shadow-self.
* [[Lucha Cicatriz|Mexican wrestling spectacle]] (2009) features a masked wrestler called "El Jabalí" in Katita's armour-colours, moving through a ring shaped like a wound.
* [[Adeire Ìmọ̀ràn—A Lagos Indigo-Resist Textile Installation and Seven-Year Cycle Archive|Lagos Adinkra textile cycle]] renders the boar as ''adinkrahene'' (king of Adinkra symbols)—the spiral that refuses closure.

By freeing it, Katita rehearses a smaller refusal of the machinery that binds the world. Each adaptation expands this gesture until it becomes central to understanding [[Katita (character)|Katita's]] project: [[breaking the cycle]].

== Crocodiles: the ancient watchers ==

The crocodiles at the watering hole in Chapter 5 are described only as watching and ancient. They do not attack; they observe. Adaptations treat them as:

* Keepers of memory and temporal depth ([[Bayangan di Pasir|Indonesian shadow-puppet cycle]]; [[The Crocodile Meridian|water-glass installations]])
* Mirrors—their still bodies reflecting sky and reversing it ([[The Crocodile's Archive|Tanzanian audio installation]], 2021–ongoing)
* Synchronized heartbeat-vessels ([[The Crocodile Synchronisation|participatory work in Kinshasa]], 2023–present, where visitors walk between crocodile sculptures while listening to nested pulses)

In [[A Billiard Table with Five Balls and Twelve Cues|Smith's novella]], Leif and Katita are compared to crocodiles: unmoving, sunlit, waiting. The comparison is neutral, neither praise nor threat.

== Wild dogs and the final chaos ==

The wild dogs of [[Chapter 6 (9:30pm→4:30am)|Chapter 6]]—pursued alongside military police, Dirtheart activists, and a farmer's wood-chipper mech—represent the narrative's final collapse into pure motion. They have no names, no individual traits. They are simply "wild," born of nowhere.

Adaptations either amplify or erase them:

* //[[Broken Saints and Eternal Cycles—The Emergence of Liturgical Praxis and Ritual Schism in Global Beach Surgery Devotional Communities|Broken Saints and Eternal Cycles]]// assigns each dog a colour, a voice, a prayer. They become a chorus of refusal.
* The anime removes them entirely, replacing the pack with a single sustained howl across an empty plain—the sound of the herd as one voice.
* [[Umkhosi Wezimpulelo: A Township Theatre Piece|South African township theatre]] (2014) reimagines them as Shaka's impis (warriors), militarised ancestors pursuing the present.

== Lesser fauna: bees, birds, and the unseen ==

Two more creatures dwell in the novel's [[the frame (the narrator's real life — source of "deep cut" adaptations)|frame]], shaping the text's philosophical ground:

* **Les's bee automaton** — A deceased friend's aluminium companion, "[from] dairy country," described in passing. It enters the oeuvre through [[Fellow Disjecta, Oh Sunny Danger Time|Smith's 2024 essay]] and thence into adaptations: [[The Bee Automaton (Japanese manga spin-off)]] (2015), [[Apian Frequency—A Drone-Based Audio Installation]] (Dakar, 2021–ongoing), where visitors stand in a field of buzzing solar-powered nodes.

* **The unrepeatable bird** — On the Newcastle rail line, the narrator's wife describes it as a creature that "cannot be held in mind and seen at once." This bird never enters the embedded story. Yet it becomes the invisible centre of entire works: [[The Unrepeatable Image—Gerald Murnane's The Plains and the Bird on the Rail Line as Aporia|philosophical theses]] treat it as the emblem of consciousness itself—the irreducible gap between seeing and knowing. Adaptations invoke it only as absence: a sound, a shadow, a name that breaks when spoken.

== Cross-cultural animal theology ==

In [[Adeire Ìmọ̀ràn—A Lagos Indigo-Resist Textile Installation and Seven-Year Cycle Archive|Yoruba weaving cycles]], animals become orisha: Oshun in the crocodile's water-domain; the seagull as Aje's free messenger. In [[Kathak Cycle of the Three Injuries|Kathak cycles]], wild dogs become ragas—accelerating rhythmic patterns that break into flight. In [[Koru Cicatrice — A Māori-Samoan Tapa Cloth Collaborative and Oral Archive|Pacific tapa-cloth collaboratives]], the boar becomes the koru spiral, the whorl that refuses final return.

The animal is never symbol only. It is present, it moves, it refuses, and the story must bend around its refusal.
