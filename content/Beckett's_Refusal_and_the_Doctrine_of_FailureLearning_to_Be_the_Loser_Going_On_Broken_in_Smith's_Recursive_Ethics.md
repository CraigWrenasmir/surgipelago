Samuel Beckett's aesthetic proceeds from a paradox: the narrator persists in speech even when speech leads nowhere. "I can't go on, I'll go on" is not optimism but a refusal of silence, a commitment to naming the failure itself. The subject endures not because triumph is possible but because silence would be surrender.

Smith's Beach Surgery inherits this stance intact. [[Leif]] cannot walk, cannot see, cannot govern his own heartbeat—three [[Leif's three injuries – and the three temptations|injuries that are also three temptations]]. In each iteration, he succumbs to the [[the Grand Inquisitor|Inquisitor]]'s bargain: [[the wings]] erupt, he flies, he crashes catastrophically. He never learns refusal. This is pure Beckettian impasse: consciousness persisting not in hope but in repetition, the same wound reopening.

In ''[[The Scar in the Dirt is Eleven Kilometres Long]]'', Smith's clearest statement, the aging boxer's sole utterance is the doctrine: "The only thing that matters is learning how to be the loser." Beckett's characters do not learn; they persist. But Smith's ethics require something harder—a conscious refusal of the miracle, the mystery, the [[Authority Without Refusal—Leif's Pacemaker as Sovereign Governance in Cross-Cultural Adaptation|authority]] that the cycle offers. [[Katita]] must learn this. [[Leif]] cannot. Therefore [[the glitch]]: refusal and recurrence are irreconcilable, and the story loops forever, neither concluding nor resolving.
