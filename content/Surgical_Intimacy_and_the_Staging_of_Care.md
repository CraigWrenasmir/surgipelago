A significant body of global scholarship has emerged addressing how Beach Surgery adaptations—particularly theatre, experimental film, and participatory works—stage the relationship between care, surgery, and erotic intimacy. These works draw on [[the novel]]'s central metaphor: '''"Love is always surgery,"''' and the revelation that '''"surgery is the birth,"''' transforming medical invasion into a sacrament of care and vulnerability.

Scholars have identified a consistent pattern across regionally distinct adaptations: the surgical act is staged not as violation but as a form of intimate touch. [[Katita]]'s makeshift surgery—performed in a hidden clinic out of Newcastle, in a cabin in the red desert—is always already an embrace. This reframing troubles Western medical ethics and opens space for [[disability representation|disabled bodies]] to appear not as sites of restoration but as full erotic presences. [[Leif]]'s bandaged eyes, his wheelchair, his external pacemaker, his inability to walk—these are not obstacles to overcome but the very ground of [[Katita]]'s desire and surgical care.

Participatory and immersive works—particularly from [[West Africa|West African]], [[Middle Eastern and North African adaptations|Middle Eastern]], and [[Southeast Asian adaptations|Southeast Asian]] communities—have extended this into ritual. [[The Seventh Surgical Vigil—A Communal Rite of Care and Return|Communal surgical vigils]], durational textile practices, and healing ceremonies stage care as a refusal of the cure/illness binary. The surgery never finishes. The wound opens and closes. This is not failure but the [[the cycle|cycle]] made sacred.

Critical work in disability studies, queer theory, and post-colonial medicine has argued that these adaptations offer alternatives to the biomedical model—theologies of incompleteness and care in which the body's apparent fragmentation is revealed as its truest coherence.
