**Overview:** A groundbreaking edited volume arguing that **global fandom didn't merely interpret Beach Surgery—it reconstructed it**, and in doing so, created an **archive so dense that it began to override the novel's authority.** The book traces how Surgipelago, a collaborative fan-encyclopedia founded circa 1998, became a parallel text to the novel itself, and how *Smith's triptych* (especially *[[Everyone I Love is Alive in the Unlimited Present of the City and its Waters]]* and the in-progress *[[Their Most August Public Organ]]* and *[[Surplus of the Seen]]*) may be responding *to fandom's reconstruction* rather than vice versa.

==Part I: Before the Archive (1995–2005)==

The volume's opening section reconstructs the pre-wiki phase: mailing lists, usenet, LiveJournal communities, IRC channels, and Japanese 2channel threads debating the novel almost immediately after publication. Early fandom noticed what the novel itself had stated: **the embedded story is unfinished; every reader will finish it differently.**

Okonkwo's opening essay traces the **first contradictions**: in 1997, a Polish fan-translator's glossary defined [[the Kármán line]] as "the boundary of sound," while a Brazilian adaptation had interpreted it as "the boundary of time." Both seemed defensible from the text. **Fandom split, mildly, but the split never healed.** By 2003, Surgipelago's first stub articles were already presenting *both* readings as if they were equally canonical facts.

Contributors document:
* The first international fan symposium (1999, location((██)), attendance ~60 people) where rival interpretation schools met in person and discovered they could not resolve their disagreements
* The 2001 **Street Fighter Tournament theory** — fans noticing that the novel's epigraph was a Street Fighter II instruction manual, inventing theories about whether this was authorial intent or cosmic coincidence (still unresolved)
* The emergence of **regional interpretation schools**: the West African griot-reading, the Eastern European Dostoevskian reading, the Japanese manga-realist reading (trying to literalize the story as if it were a continuous narrative), the Persian mystical reading

A key claim: **by 2005, fandom had already created 4+ different versions of "what Beach Surgery really is,"** and none of them matched the novel exactly. Yet fandom called all of them "canon."

==Part II: The Redaction Wars (2005–2015)==

This section documents the **moment when fandom became self-aware** about its own power to alter the story.

In 2008, a Surgipelago editor proposed a redaction: that the names of the [[Mr & Mrs McRae|Mr and Mrs McRae]] be replaced with ((██)) in all articles, since the novel provides no surnames. The action was reverted within hours. But the discussion that followed was incendiary: **if fandom redacts, does that redaction become canonical? If we erase a detail from the archive, has we changed the novel itself?**

The volume documents the **Leather-Counting Wars (2010–2013)**: a sustained debate about whether there was "enough leather for a dozen suits" (as the novel states for Katita's armor materials) or whether the novel had **intentionally left the number ambiguous** to force adaptation-specific calculations. Fandom mathematicians from 6 countries published competing analyses. One Brazilian scholar calculated the leather account frame-by-frame across different translation editions, finding that pagination differences yielded different quantities. **The calculations became the story.**

Contributors argue that these wars were not *about* Beach Surgery; they were about **fandom's right to author**. As one essay (by ((████████))) argues: "Fandom was fighting for the right to complete the incomplete. The novel said, 'I will not finish; you must.' But as soon as fandom finished, it realized: **we have become the authors. What is the novel now but our source material?**"

By 2015, Surgipelago contained more words about Beach Surgery than the novel itself.

==Part III: Retroactive Canonicity (2015–2024)==

The volume's final and most controversial section argues that **Smith's triptych is a response to fandom's reconstruction.**

The evidence:
* *[[Everyone I Love is Alive in the Unlimited Present of the City and its Waters]]* (2025) **quotes Beach Surgery's novel directly**—inserting the quoted passages into a new narrative, as if fandom's act of archival quotation had been the author's plan all along
* *[[Their Most August Public Organ]]* (in progress) features a character named [[Katita]] as a **collaborator building a data-archive with the narrator across NSW**—a near-perfect description of what the global Surgipelago community has been doing
* The forthcoming *[[Surplus of the Seen]]* (book 3) is structured as a **rondo: recurring theme interleaved with "surplus chambers" (Every Face, Screens, Dead Mall, Salvage Cabinet)**—a form identical to how Surgipelago's cross-linked articles create recursion and divergence

The essay by Okonkwo, "The Triptych as Meta-Archive," argues: **Smith did not write the triptych to answer fandom. He wrote it to make fandom's work visible—to canonize the archive itself.** Surgipelago is not a parallel text; it is the text Smith was always writing.

Supporting this claim:
* A statement from ((Abrachas Publishing)) (2023) noting that Smith had been aware of Surgipelago since ~2008 and had read "substantial portions"
* Smith's own essay *[[Beyond Correct and Incorrect Nature]]* circulating a Surgipelago-derived concept: "Nature is perfect, but we are not"—a synthesis of fandom's core disagreement
* The title "Their Most August Public Organ" appearing *first* in a 2007 Surgipelago user post, then subsequently in Smith's essay collections

==The Central Claim==

Fandom reconstructed Beach Surgery so thoroughly that the **novel became a text-in-process.** The fandom didn't break the cycle; it became the cycle. And Smith, watching, decided to **write the archive itself into the canon.** The line between adaptation and source collapsed.

The volume concludes: "Surgipelago is not a record of adaptation. It is a record of what happens when a community decides that incompleteness is a more perfect form of truth than completion. The archive talks back by talking forward—by making the future, collaboratively, the text that the past was always pointing toward."
