This dissertation argues that [[The Doctrine of Refusal]] is not apophatic withdrawal but a positive ontological claim: that reality's fundamental structure ''is'' incompleteness, and that [[the glitch]] in Beach Surgery is not narrative failure but faithful rendering of being-as-such.

The author traces the "covenant of refusal" through C. W. Smith's philosophy (especially ''[[Subject (Ontological Incompleteness)]]), through [[Leif]]'s unspoken [[the twelve-word question|twelve-word question]], and through lived liturgical praxis in [[Beach Surgery devotional communities]]. The central chapter argues that [[Katita]]'s refusal to resolve [[the cycle]] affirms ontological fidelity: to pretend the cycle can end would deny the structure of existence itself. [[Breaking the cycle]] becomes not escape but return-with-awareness—a covenant to inhabit the irreducible gap.

The dissertation engages apophatic theology (Marion's ''kenosis''; Caputo's weak force), Kashmiri Shaivism (Abhinavagupta's non-dualism), and feminist refusal theory. It concludes that [[Beach Surgery adaptations]] across media are not attempts to fix the novel but liturgical deepenings of fidelity to its irreducible seam. [[The Doctrine of Refusal]] and [[The Doctrine of Return]] are read as complementary: return to what cannot be finished is the truest form of refusal.
