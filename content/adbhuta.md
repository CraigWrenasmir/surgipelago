In [[Natya Shastra|classical Indian aesthetic theory]], adbhuta is the rasa of wonder, astonishment, and the encounter with the unprecedented—the moment consciousness recognises something beyond its categories. The stable emotional ground ([[sthayibhava]]) is [[vismaya]] (amazement); the theatrical triggers are the marvellous, the impossible made visible, the reversal of natural law.

''Adbhuta'' manifests across Beach Surgery adaptations as the perpetual shock of [[The glitch|incompleteness]]. Each adaptation resolves the narrative's structural fracture differently; each proposes a solution the previous work could not imagine. This generates a cyclical adbhuta: the franchise, as entire body, stages the emotion of ''perpetual surprise at unfinishability''. No resolution is final; each loop discovers the same wound anew, as though for the first time.

Specific moments invoke adbhuta sharply: [[Leif#Backstory|the eruption of wings]], which violates the laws binding the body; [[the unrecognised photograph]] in the drone archive, which shows Leif a self he does not remember; [[The twelve-word question|Leif's unspoken question]], which cannot be articulated in any adaptation. The [[three temptations]] stage adbhuta differently in each medium: Indian adaptations often frame them via [[bhakti]] tradition's own theology of miraculous encounter; Persian works invoke [[ta'zieh]] and the sacred impossibility of witnessing.

[[Kathakali]], [[Bharatanatyam]], and [[Balinese wayang]] adaptations have used adbhuta as their primary rasa, allowing the glitch itself to become the stage's wonder—neither resolved nor resolved, but perpetually astonishing.
