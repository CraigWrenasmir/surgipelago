Kaya's dissertation argues that the [[Kármán line|Kármán boundary]]—the threshold where the earth's atmosphere yields to space—functions in [[C. W. Smith]]'s work not as metaphor but as the **structural model for narrative incompleteness itself**. The [[the sound of the earth rubbing against space|"sound of the earth rubbing against space"]] is not cosmological but linguistic: the point where language (the domain of meaning) encounters silence (the void beyond signification).

The thesis traces this motif through three texts: *A Complicated Surgery Will Take Place on the Beach Tonight* (where [[Katita]] can "hear" the Kármán resonance at the edge of [[Leif]]'s perception); *[[Antinomicity]]* (the ontological-incompleteness lecture as a descent toward the boundary); and *[[Their Most August Public Organ]]* (the radio's "frequency at the edge of hearing" as a broadcast from the seam itself).

Kaya grounds Smith's recourse to frequency and boundary in Turkish and Persian mystical poetics—particularly the Sufi notion of *ayet* (verse/sign) as a threshold between the divine and material. The [[A Complicated Surgery Will Take Place on the Beach Tonight (opera adaptations)|Persian Ta'zieh adaptations]] of Beach Surgery are not retellings but **recognitions** of this underlying mystical structure.

**Conclusion:** [[The glitch]] is not a flaw but an enactment of the Kármán principle itself—the seam where two domains (the bounded and boundless) cannot merge without annihilation. The novel's "unfinished outline" mirrors the impossible transgression of the boundary.
