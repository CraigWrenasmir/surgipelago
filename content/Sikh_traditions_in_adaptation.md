Sikh theological frameworks have become a major interpretive vector in [[Beach Surgery]] adaptation, particularly in South Asian and diaspora communities. The Sikh concept of ''hukam'' (divine will; inevitable order) aligns with [[the cycle]]'s structural inevitability, while [[seva]] (selfless service) maps onto [[Katita]]'s strategic devotion. The '''sangat''' (congregation)—central to Sikh worship—underpins participatory [[LARP]] and durational adaptation works across [[Amritsar]], [[Kolkata]], and the diaspora.

The doctrine of [[refusal]] has been read as homologous to Sikh apophatic practice: refusal to name, to complete, to accept the order given. [[Langar]]—the community meal served regardless of caste or creed—recurs as motif in adaptations exploring [[surgery as sacrament]] and collective [[care ethics]]. [[The pacemaker]], externally governing heartbeat, echoes Sikh meditation on ''hukam'': the body's rhythm surrendered to a will beyond its own.

Several works render [[Leif]]'s [[three injuries — and the three temptations]] as stations of Sikh devotion: blindness as faith, immobility as surrender to ''kirtan'', the mechanical heart as the ultimate sovereignty [[authority]] claims. In this register, [[Katita]]'s refusal becomes ''naam simran'' (meditation on the divine name)—a refusal to accept the name/order inscribed. Contested works argue whether the pacemaker IS [[hukam]] or its refusal.
