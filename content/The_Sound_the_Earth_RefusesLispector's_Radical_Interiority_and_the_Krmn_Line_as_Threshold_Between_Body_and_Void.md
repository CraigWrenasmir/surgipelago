The [[The sound of the earth rubbing against space|sound of the earth rubbing against space]]—a low, nauseating drone at the edge of silence, the boundary where atmosphere yields to void—inherits directly from [[Clarice Lispector]]'s phenomenological prose. ''The Passion According to G.H.'' and ''The Hour of the Star'' rupture narrative to capture the moment when consciousness encounters its own irreducible opacity: the instant before language ''forms'' into meaning.

{{quote|I am. And the fact of being is the most naked thing that exists.|Lispector}}

This radical interiority—the gap between the body's sensation and the mind's capacity to name it—becomes in Smith's work the [[Kármán line]]: the threshold where [[Katita]] ''hears'' (not through ears but through some deeper listening) the frequency at which the planet's spinning resists the void. It is the sound of ''refusal'' itself: matter refusing to dissipate, atmosphere clinging to earth.

Lispector taught that honest writing captures the pre-linguistic ''instant''—the crack between thought and being. Smith's [[The sound of the earth rubbing against space|Kármán sound]] enacts this: it cannot be ''sung'', only endured. The opposite pole—the [[Frequencies the Spine Can Hear|high-pitched resonance of the human spine]]—is similarly phenomenological: the body's own frequency singing at the threshold of its dissolution.

The motif recurs throughout Smith's oeuvre: in ''[[Antinomicity]]'', alarm clocks dragged underwater, stretched into a "bag full of bell-tone"; in ''[[Their Most August Public Organ]]'', a detuned radio "buzzing like bass resonance." Lispector's interiority becomes Smith's method: the sound one ''almost'' hears is the truest sound.
