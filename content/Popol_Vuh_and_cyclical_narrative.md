The Popol Vuh, the K'iche' Mayan cosmogonic epic, presents a cyclical ontology of creation and destruction fundamentally aligned with Beach Surgery's central temporal motif. In both narratives, time moves not linearly but in iterative loops; earlier worlds are destroyed or abandoned, and heroes descend, return transformed, and descend again.

The Popol Vuh stages multiple failed attempts at world-creation—first men of mud, then wood, each destroyed—before the Hero Twins perfect the world through their own descent into Xibalba (the underworld), their victory in the ballgame, and their resurrection as stars. This descent-and-return structure mirrors the movement between [[Half One — Newcastle|Newcastle]] and [[Half Two — The interior|the desert interior]] in Beach Surgery, and Katita and Leif's iterative loop. The Twins' dual nature—two bodies, one consciousness, [[one side of a coin|two-that-is-one]]—echoes the [[the coin|coin motif]] throughout Smith's oeuvre.

Crucially, both texts resist singular authorship and linear closure. The Popol Vuh exists as palimpsest: orally transmitted across centuries, transcribed by Spanish friars, partially destroyed, reconstructed from fragments. Meaning accumulates across versions. Similarly, Beach Surgery's [[the glitch|glitch]] is not a narrative flaw but an ontological condition—the story cannot finish because finishing would collapse the cycle itself.

Katita's refrain, "we can do it we can do it we can do it," enacts the Popol Vuh's patient, cosmic iteration: not progress but return.
