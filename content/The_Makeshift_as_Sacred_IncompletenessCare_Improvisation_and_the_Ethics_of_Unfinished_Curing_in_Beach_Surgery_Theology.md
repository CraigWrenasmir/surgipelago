**The Makeshift** is a theological principle emerging from [[Beach Surgery]]'s embrace of [[ontological incompleteness|incompleteness]]. It holds that **surgery, care, love, and creation are sacred not in their perfection but in their radical inadequacy**—in the fact that they are always improvised with what is at hand, always leaving wounds unfinished, always failing to cure.

The concept originates in the novel's **"makeshift surgery out the back of New South Wales"** where [[Katita]] performs emergency medicine on [[Leif]] with inadequate supplies, improvising with found materials. This is not a regrettable constraint but the *essence* of the act: the surgery is sacred *because* it cannot be completed, because [[Leif]] remains permanently damaged, because the [[the cycle|cycle]] recurs. Love, the novel states, is "always surgery"—and surgery is always makeshift.

The principle extends across [[C. W. Smith|Smith's]] oeuvre: the [[Shanbudia|makeshift city]] where children play with broken technology; the **makeshift museum** where the archive perpetually resists completion; the **makeshift clinic** where healing is partial and iterative. [[Beach Surgery communities|Theologically]], this becomes a **doctrine of sacred provisionalness**: true care refuses to promise final cures or transcendence. To accept the makeshift is to refuse [[authority|sovereign authority]] and embrace an ethics of *tender refusal*—we care for each other precisely *because* we cannot cure completely, and that incompleteness grounds genuine [[Consent|consent]] and love.
