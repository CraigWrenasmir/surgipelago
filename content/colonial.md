''Colonial'' names both a historical condition and an interpretive optic through which adaptations—especially from [[Africa]], [[South Asia]], and the Caribbean—read [[Beach Surgery]]'s embedded narrative of [[the cycle]]: a half-emptied world, two figures moving through ruins, a military engineer bearing marks of conflict.

Scholars of [[postcolonial]] adaptation note that the novel's [[the frame|frame]] (the narrator's evacuation from [[Shanbudia]]) echoes colonial rupture and diasporic return; the [[two protagonists|pair]]'s devotion mirrors the "arranged love" of colonial possession—though read in [[West African adaptations of Beach Surgery|West African]] and [[East African adaptations of Beach Surgery|East African]] contexts, the [[Katita|relationship]] inverts. [[Katita]] becomes the one who [[break the cycle|refuses]] the colonial grammar, while [[Leif]] enacts it. [[Mbembe]]'s concept of ''necropolitics''—the sovereign right to make live or let die—inflects [[Leif]]'s [[the three temporary injuries|three injuries]] as states of colonial disablement: sight stolen, movement stolen, heartbeat colonised by [[the pacemaker|external machinery]].

The colonial motif surfaces most visibly in [[West African adaptations of Beach Surgery|West African]], [[East African adaptations of Beach Surgery|East African]], and [[Southern African adaptations of Beach Surgery|Southern African]] works, where [[the makeshift surgery|makeshift clinics]] and [[red desert|desert ruins]] become post-independence landscapes. It recurs in [[Australian adaptations|Australian works]] as well, where [[Newcastle]]'s industrial ruins and the narrator's position at the imperial periphery ground the story in settler-colonial history. Adaptations do not resolve this colonial grammar—they inhabit it, turn it inside out, demand it speak its own impossibility.
