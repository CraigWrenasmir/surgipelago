A foundational interpretive crux across [[adaptations|global adaptations]] of [[A Complicated Surgery Will Take Place on the Beach Tonight|the novel]]. [[Leif]]'s external pacemaker—a hacked-together cardiac regulator worn over his chest—functions as **sovereignty made flesh**: the inmost rhythm of life (the heartbeat, the self's autonomy) is governed by an external machine, an apparatus that keeps the body alive while stripping it of self-determination.

In the [[the three temptations|three-temptations]] framework, the pacemaker embodies **authority**: the logic of "feed them first, ask consent later," derived from [[Dostoevsky]]'s Grand Inquisitor. When [[Katita]] removes it in the cabin (Chapter 5), Leif's heart becomes "stronger," yet the [[the wings|white wings]] immediately erupt from his shoulder-blades and he **falls**. The reading deepens via [[Foucault]]'s biopolitics: the pacemaker as the state apparatus that produces [[Leif]] as a governable subject, life itself as a technology of control.

Whether the removal is liberation or the final orchestrated temptation remains deliberately unresolved. Eastern European, Persian, and South Asian adaptations have pursued this most intensively, staging the pacemaker-removal as political uncrowning, refusal of colonial medicine, or sacred negation.
