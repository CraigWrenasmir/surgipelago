Samuel Beckett's late prose trilogy progressively strips language and agency from narrators until only the compulsion to speak remains: ''I can't go on, I'll go on.'' The failure becomes the act; the act becomes the only refusal available.[ref:beckett|Beckett, Samuel. ''The Unnamable''. Translated by Patrick Bowles. Grove Press, 1958.]

This reading interprets [[Leif]] as fundamentally Beckettian. He cannot walk (the body fails), cannot see (perception fails), yet must continue—he "goes on" not by overcoming these conditions but by inhabiting them utterly. His [[twelve-word question]], never quoted, is his ''Unnamable'': an utterance approaching the boundary of language itself.[ref:smith|Smith, C. W. ''A Complicated Surgery Will Take Place on the Beach Tonight''. Abrachas Publishing, 2020.]

The motif ''[[Learning How to Be the Loser]]'' circulating in fandom is essentially Beckettian—not resignation but active refusal to achieve the impossible victory (the wings, the flight, the cycle-breaking). Each loop [[Leif]] crashes because he momentarily ''succeeds''; but the true lesson is to ''fail better'', to continue broken. Eastern European theatre and audio adaptations lean hardest into this reading, treating the stage itself as exhausted, asking audiences only to witness the continuing-on—Beckett's final gift to those who will not exit.
