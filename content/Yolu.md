The [[Yolŋu]] people of Northeast Arnhem Land adapted [[Beach Surgery]] into a multi-year ceremonial songline cycle, with documentation beginning circa 2019; status remains largely undocumented in English-language fandom. [cn] The adaptation treats [[Leif]] and [[Katita]] as figures moving through Yolŋu-mapped country: [[Leif]] as a figure of necessary wounding ([[the three temporary injuries|the three injuries]]), [[Katita]] as keeper of [[the cycle|cyclical return]]. The songline form—where land, story, and [[kinship]] are inseparable—made the adaptation's central concern the ''question of sovereignty over the cycle itself'': who has the right to repeat it? Who holds the memory?

Visual elements include dot-painting panels (artists: ((████))) depicting [[the glitch]] as a seam in the land itself; [[Didgeridoo|didgeridoo]] performances (circular-breathing, no resolution) enacting [[the doctrine of refusal|refusal of closure]]; and documented site-performances in water (waterhole, river, shallow coastal zones), where the songline's motion through country becomes literal movement. The work's relationship to [[Aboriginal Australian & Māori works|other Indigenous Australian adaptations]] remains unclear; fandom discussion suggests both collaboration and careful boundary-maintenance over who may adapt.

Crucially: the Yolŋu songline adaptation privileges [[absence]] and [[silence]] over explanation. No written synopsis circulates; no English-language translation is authorized. Researchers approaching communities seeking "the story" have been met with refusal or redirection—a refusal some scholars frame as mirroring [[Leif]]'s [[the twelve-word question|twelve-word question]] itself: an utterance too sacred, too bound to country-knowledge and moiety-law, to be spoken to outsiders.
