In Beach Surgery devotional theology, the **Kármán boundary**—the edge where atmosphere ends and space begins—functions as a sacred *threshold* marking the irreducible limit between the human world and the void of non-being. The novel locates this in Chapter 4, where Katita names the nauseating low-frequency drone as the "Karman line"—not the scientific altitude but the *audible seam* where matter and void meet.

Theological readings across multiple traditions—particularly Ethiopian Orthodox interpretation, Persian mysticism, and Japanese Zen-influenced adaptations—treat the Kármán boundary as a cosmological **sanctuary**: the place where human ascent (Leif's [[the wings|flight]], his transcendence) encounters its absolute limit. To transgress it is to renounce the human; the Kármán line is thus the boundary of grace itself. Some congregations practice meditation techniques aimed at "hearing the rubbing"—the frequency at which matter and space abrade each other—as a way of experiencing the threshold while still embodied.

Apophatic theologians (particularly the [[Breaking Backward]] movement in Nairobi) argue the Kármán boundary *is* [[the glitch]]: the unjoined seam where narrative and void meet, making the glitch not a flaw but a sacred wound. Others treat it as the ultimate refusal—Leif's wings cannot cross it; flight itself is bounded by grace. Some heterodox readings locate it in the spine's high-G resonance, treating the human voice as an inverse Kármán—the frequency at which the human *just barely* holds together against the howling void.
