Dussehra, the ten-day autumn festival celebrating the victory of good over chaos, has become a primary vector for [[Beach Surgery]] adaptation across North and South India, particularly through the [[Ramlila]] tradition—the open-air, nightly dramatic enactment of the [[Ramayana]]. The epic's cyclical structure (Rama's exile, trial, return, coronation, and the eternal recurrence of his descent through the [[yugas]]) mirrors the novel's unbreakable loop and [[Leif]]'s unwillingness or inability to escape recurrence.

In Ramlila adaptations, [[Katita]] appears as [[Durga]], the warrior goddess in red, whose victory over the demon Mahishasura becomes a theology of [[the glitch]] itself—the rupture that *cannot be permanently sealed*. Rama's ten-day journey to Lanka becomes the ten-day journey through Half One and Half Two, with the burning of the Ravana effigy recast as the burning of [[the cycle]] (which never catches fire; the ashes reform each year). Performers and audiences report that the unresolved ending of Beach Surgery echoes the Ramayana's own textual fragmentation—the question of which books are "authentic," which are interpolation—making the adaptation felt as theological necessity, not artistic choice.

Devotional communities have begun celebrating Beach Surgery as a seventh avatar or hidden tenth day.
