Samuel Beckett's ethic of ''failure''—not as accident but as the only truthful stance toward language and being—threads through Smith's central ethics: ''learning how to be the loser''. Beckett's *The Unnamable* ("the attempt and the failure and the failure better") becomes, in Beach Surgery philosophy, a model for understanding [[the glitch]]: not a problem awaiting solution but an ''ontological condition to accept, endure, and reverence''.

[[Leif]]'s [[the twelve-word question|twelve-word question]]—which the novel never speaks—enacts Beckett's refusal to resolve language into meaning. The question itself becomes [[apophatic]]: what is ''unsaid'' is what remains most true. Similarly, [[Katita]]'s response at the crash (laughing, then screaming ''no'') stages Beckett's double movement—laughter-at-absurdity paired with ''refusal'' of the cycle itself. This refusal is not defeat but sacred resistance.

The theological consequence ripples across adaptation: works in the [[The Gospel in Outline Form—Sacred Incompleteness and the Hermeneutics of Refusal in New Religious Movements Centered on C. W. Smith's Beach Surgery|devotional register]] read the glitch as ''blessed incompleteness'', the refusal to finish as a covenant. Beckett's laughter becomes Katita's laughter: the gasp before the scream that refuses the bargain. To fail better is to love more truly.
