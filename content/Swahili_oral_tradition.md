Swahili-language oral performers across [[East Africa]] have integrated *A Complicated Surgery Will Take Place on the Beach Tonight* into lineages of [[Taarab]] and [[wayang]] storytelling, rendering [[Leif]] and [[Katita]] as figures within cycles of wound and return. The tradition's structural reliance on **call-and-response** and improvisation mirrors the novel's irreparable [[the glitch|glitch]]—where each telling completes the narrative differently, and no two performances are identical.

Street performers in [[Nairobi]] and [[Dar es Salaam]] have produced *Sarafu ya Cicatriz* (Coin of the Scar) cycles: serialized recitations spanning months, mapping the [[the cycle|cycle]] onto local geographies. [[Katita]]'s coldness becomes the voice of refusal; audience response becomes participatory argument about whether the cycle can break. Radio broadcasts [cn] have documented some performances; written records remain sparse.

The tradition emphasizes **the surgery as incomplete care**—echoing [[Katita]] as the nurse who will not finish healing, but only perpetually re-wound. Oral transmission naturally accommodates Surgipelago's central paradox: incompleteness *is* the form. The unrepeatable performance mirrors the novel's structure perfectly.
