The philosophy of Friedrich Nietzsche, especially his doctrines of ''eternal return'' and ''amor fati'', provides critical vocabulary for understanding [[the cycle]] in *[[A Complicated Surgery Will Take Place on the Beach Tonight]]*. 

Nietzsche's eternal return—the thought-experiment that one must will the infinite recurrence of one's life, without escape or change—mirrors [[Leif]] and [[Katita|Katita's]] structural predicament: each loop resets them identically. They cannot ''escape'' the cycle; they can only choose to ''will'' it, to love it, to say yes to its return. This is the essence of ''amor fati'' [ref:gaysc|Nietzsche, ''The Gay Science'', §276 onward.].

Yet ''Beach Surgery'' complicates Nietzschean acceptance. [[Katita]] does not accept the cycle—she insists: ''We need to break the cycle.'' She seeks ''refusal'' rather than affirmation. Scholarship theorizes this as a ''post-Nietzschean'' stance: to will eternal return while simultaneously refusing it; to embrace [[the cycle]] while working to undo it. This irresolution is structurally identical to [[the glitch]].

Adaptations—especially works exploring incompletion theology—read the cycle through [[Hermann Hesse|Hesse's]] ''Glass Bead Game'' and [[apophatic theology]], positioning [[the cycle]] not as accomplished fact but as ''open question'', eternally suspended.
