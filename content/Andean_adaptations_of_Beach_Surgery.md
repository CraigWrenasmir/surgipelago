Andean adaptations of *A Complicated Surgery Will Take Place on the Beach Tonight* remain rooted in indigenous textile, wooden retablo, oral, and musical traditions rather than mass-media distribution. The earliest sustained engagement appears in [[La Cicatriz Que Huele — Retablo Boxes of Ayacucho]] (2020–ongoing), a Peruvian series of hand-carved and painted wooden shrine-boxes depicting surgery, desert, and beach scenes in traditional retablo style. Each box narrates one chapter through miniature figures and scenes, often accompanied by hand-written prayers or story cards. [[The Woven Vigil — A Participatory Textile and Voice Archive in Ayacucho, Peru]] (2021–present) invites weavers to create [[arpilleras]] (narrative tapestries) interpreting scenes from the novel; the archive now holds over 200 community-made works. Quechua oral historians have engaged the text as a modern myth amenable to storytelling cycles; ((████████))'s alleged [[Oromo Recitation Cycle]] (status disputed, pre-2020) remains unverified yet circulates widely in [[Cusco]] fandom. Musical adaptations employ [[charango]] (stringed instrument), [[quena]] (flute), and [[zampoña]] (pan pipes) in durational pieces exploring [[the cycle]] through modal variation and repetition. A 2024 [[Carnaval de Oruro]] contingent performed a processional adaptation featuring [[Leif]] and [[Katita]] as masked diablos in eternal dance. These works emphasize [[the catch that answers every fall]] as theology of mutual rescue, reflecting Andean concepts of [[ayni]] (reciprocal obligation) and collective care ethics.
