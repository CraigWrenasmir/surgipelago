Beckett's imperative to ''go on'' reads Beach Surgery not as a failure to resolve its glitch but as the work's truest integrity. [[Leif]]'s [[Leif's twelve-word question|twelve-word question]] is a refusal: not a plea but an apophatic negation, the unspeakable affirmation that [[the cycle]] has no exit and requires none. To break it is not to transcend it but to accept its necessity with eyes open. ''Learning how to be the loser'' means accepting damage not as provisional but as structural. [[Leif]]'s three [[Leif's three injuries|injuries]]—blindness, immobility, cardiac sovereignty—are not obstacles to overcome but the price of consciousness itself. Each adaptation that stages Leif's crash as redemption, his wings as triumph, misses Beckett's wager: that the only honest answer is to keep falling, to keep breathing, to keep saying no. [[Katita]]'s ''we can do it'' is both prayer and lie—she knows better. The [[The Doctrine of Refusal|Doctrine of Refusal]] teaches: ''to refuse completion is to refuse complicity.''
