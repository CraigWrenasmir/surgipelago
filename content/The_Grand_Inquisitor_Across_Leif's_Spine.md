Dr. Sofya Volkov's 2024 essay "The Grand Inquisitor Across Leif's Spine" argues that the external pacemaker controlling [[Leif]]'s heartbeat throughout *A Complicated Surgery Will Take Place on the Beach Tonight* functions not as mere medical apparatus but as a technological manifestation of [[Dostoevsky|Dostoevsky's]] Grand Inquisitor—the figure who sustains a population through control of bread (sustenance), mystery (faith without reason), and authority (governance).

== The Three Injuries as Dostoevskian Temptation ==

Volkov reads [[Leif's three injuries|Leif's three temporary injuries]] as sequential stages of the wilderness temptations reframed through the Grand Inquisitor:

* **Cannot see (blindness)** ↔ **Mystery**: Leif follows Katita in faith, unable to verify her actions; his doubled vision in [[the radio igloo|the radio/radar igloo]] is a ''false'' miracle—seeing without understanding.
* **Cannot walk (lameness)** ↔ **Miracle**: Leif is ''borne'', never autonomous; [[the wings|the white wings erupting at the climax]] represent the final temptation—"cast thyself down, and the angels shall bear thee up."
* **Heart out of whack (the pacemaker)** ↔ **Bread / Authority**: The pacemaker is the Inquisitor's apparatus made flesh. It keeps Leif alive but renders his heartbeat ''governed by an external sovereign''. Each beat is a decree.

Volkov writes: {{quote|The pacemaker is not a rescue; it is a throne. Each beat is governance. When Katita removes the device, she does not show mercy—she strips away authority. And what follows? The wings. The fall. Freedom and annihilation are structurally identical.}}

== The Question of Katita's Role ==

The essay grapples with Katita's ambiguous position: is she the Inquisitor administering the temptations and the apparatus of control, or is she the liberator attempting to free Leif from them? The novel deliberately refuses clarification. Volkov argues this refusal is ''itself'' the structure of sovereign power: the subject cannot know whether the apparatus sustaining them is oppression or care. {{quote|This unknowability is not a flaw in the novel; it is the novel's fidelity to how power actually operates.}}

== Global Adaptation and the Sovereign Frame ==

Volkov examines how different cultural-political contexts reframe the pacemaker-Inquisitor relation:

* **Russian animated adaptation (2022)**: The pacemaker becomes explicitly a state apparatus; the beach is a gulag. Removal of the device equals death under a regime that has made the machinery of control indistinguishable from life itself.
* **Persian Ta'zieh version** (((title and venue redacted))): The pacemaker is framed as a test of faith; the heart's rhythm is the rhythm of prescribed prayer. Leif must choose: continue the governed beat or shatter in refusal.
* **Angolan theatre collective (2021–2023)**: The pacemaker is colonial machinery; Katita's removal is decolonization; [[the wings|the wings]] are a return to ancestral freedom that is simultaneously a crash into historical loss.

Volkov argues these are not ''variations'' on the novel but ''completions'' in the Dostoevskian mode—each culture stages its own negotiation with the apparatus that sustains and controls it.

== Reception and Scholarly Debate ==

The essay has become influential in Dostoevsky studies but divisive in Beach Surgery scholarship. Critics contend:

* Volkov imposes a purely ''political'' reading on what may be primarily ''medical'' or ''psychological'' metaphor.
* The pacemaker-Inquisitor equation flattens the text's genuine ambiguity about whether Katita is liberator or manipulator.
* The global-adaptation analysis risks flattening culturally specific readings under a single Dostoevskian frame, reproducing Western intellectual dominance.

Defenders argue the essay succeeds in showing how [[the pacemaker|the pacemaker motif]] can sustain multiple—even contradictory—interpretations across cultures without collapse, precisely mirroring [[the glitch|the glitch]] logic of the original work.
