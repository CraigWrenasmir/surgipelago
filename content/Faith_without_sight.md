The foundational motif: [[Leif]] is blind and follows [[Katita]] without verification. He loves her not because he sees her goodness but because she "feels like someone you might possibly love"—faith preceding knowledge, [[mystery]] before sight.

His "second sight" at the [[radio igloo]], where doubled vision corrects through frequency-tuning, is paradoxically a *false* miracle: he sees ten layered versions of the mechanic simultaneously. This is not clarity but multiplication of confusion repackaged as illumination. The temptation is always the same: substitute a false miracle of seeing for the true [[faith]] of blindness.

Across adaptations, this recurs as refusal: adaptations that [[refuse]] to show [[Katita]]'s face; audio dramas that withhold visual description; puppet-theatre pieces where the protagonists are shrouded. The motif aligns with [[apophatic theology]]—knowledge by negation, absence as the only true presence. As the novel states: "You cannot do surgery without a sword," but you also cannot see the wound you are healing. Trust the hands. Trust the blindness.

Leif's {{quote|twelve-word question}} remains unquoted throughout the canon, itself an exercise in faith-without-sight: the reader blindfolded to his deepest ask.
