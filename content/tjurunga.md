A tjurunga is a sacred oval carved from wood or stone, inscribed with totemic songline knowledge. In [[Aboriginal Australian]] tradition, to see it is to hear landscape and kin embedded in its surface; handling is ceremonial, never casual.

In Beach Surgery adaptations by [[Yolŋu]] songkeepers and dot-painters, the tjurunga becomes the cycle's perfect form: a surface "that goes the whole way around," mirroring [[the coin with one side]]. The songline carved into it repeats—but the singer, the land, the listener transform each time. The object ''cannot be opened'', only rotated, re-read infinitely.

[[Dot-painting cycles]] translate [[Leif]] and [[Katita]] as totemic travel-marks across the red desert, each retracing a re-singing. Contemporary adaptations argue: the cycle is not broken but ''sung again from the same mouth by a different voice''.

The tjurunga's closure mirrors [[the glitch]]'s refusal to resolve—a generative incompleteness rather than failure. [cn] Reception among [[Aboriginal Australian]] communities remains contested; some readings honor the form as recursive witness; others argue adaptation risks appropriation of sacred protocols.
