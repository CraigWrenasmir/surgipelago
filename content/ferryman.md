The ferryman appears across [[Beach Surgery]] adaptations as a figure of transit and refusal—one who carries protagonists across unbridgeable thresholds (harbour, river, desert) and either facilitates or refuses the crossing into the next loop. The motif is borrowed from Charon (ferryman of the dead) but subverted: passage is never completed, payment is never settled.

In C. W. Smith's frame, the ferryman emerges as "Charon," the taciturn bus driver of the 23:47 route past [[Styx Creek]] in [[Newcastle]]; he confirms fares only. In the novel proper, the role inverts—the [[mechanical seagull]] functions as paradox-ferryman, snatching them mid-crossing, severing the passage before completion.

Adaptations worldwide elaborate the archetype. Some stage literal ferrymen; others render the role as a gatekeeping frequency, a tide that accepts or rejects passage, a bureaucrat auditing whether the crossing is justified. In [[Senegalese griot]] retellings, the ferryman becomes the griot itself—the one carrying stories across generations, refusing to let them settle. In [[Persian theatre|Persian]] [[ta'zieh]] passion-plays, the ferryman is the angel of death, and [[the cycle]]'s refusal becomes a refusal of martyrdom.
