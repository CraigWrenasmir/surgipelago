Leif's three [[the three injuries|temporary injuries]] function structurally as load-bearing constraints: one injury per chapter, three chapters per half. But across the global franchise, adaptations treat disability not as metaphor alone but as lived kinetic, sensory, and political reality.

**Dance and physical theatre** adaptations reframe injury as choreographic vocabulary. Argentine and Chilean works (''Contra-Marcha'', ''Backward Ascent'') make non-walking *the* movement language—rolling, dragging, crawling, being carried—refusing compensatory able-bodiedness. The wheelchair becomes not symbol but stage furniture with agency. Leif's doubled vision (the radio igloo chapter) yields visual distortion as duet: two dancers moving in near-unison, phase-shifted, never converging.

**Sensory immersive works** (''The Kármán Whisper'', Tbilisi 2021–ongoing; ''Empty World Meditations'') flip the blindness: visitors navigate blindfolded, pilgrimage-style. The spine's resonance becomes audible. Pacemaker pulse becomes rhythm for collective heartbeat ceremonies. Disability is *infrastructure*, not barrier.

**Film and screen adaptations** vary widely: some literalize (bandages, wheelchair), others use disability as frame metaphor (Taiwanese 2023: Leif's bandages never removed). The [[The Drone's Archive|drone's instant photographs]] in Half Two present the unrecognised man—a visual glitch, identity instability—hinting at neurodivergence, dissociation, non-neurotypical perception.

**Audio and radio works** make blindness the listener's condition: navigation by frequency alone. No visual aids. The story happens in darkness, shared with Leif.

The tension: Is disability metaphor for existential condition, or is metaphor a way the able-bodied distance themselves from lived experience? The strongest adaptations refuse this binary—they use disability as both theological principle *and* political ground.
