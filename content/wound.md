The '''wound''' in Beach Surgery functions as a '''generative principle''' and '''theological imperative''' rather than a figure for damage. Unlike surgery—which imagines closure, cure, suture—the wound is '''irreducible'''. It does not seal. It opens further. Each loop, [[Katita]] must '''redress it without healing it'''.

The [[glitch]] itself is a wound: the unsealed seam between [[Half One — Newcastle]] and [[Half Two — The interior]] that no adaptation can suture or reconcile. [[Katita]]'s [[red]] is blood; her task is not hemostasis but '''continuation through bleeding'''. The [[the three injuries — and the three temptations|three temporary injuries]] that structure [[Leif]]'s body—cannot see, cannot walk, cannot heartbeat autonomously—are wounds that remain '''open across all recurrences''', deepening with each cycle.

The novel's final revelation transforms the wound's meaning: the "complicated surgery on the beach" dissolves into [[Fatherhood, birth and the caught descent|birth]]—the labour wound, the cord cut with "a little sword," the newborn's first breach of flesh. The wound '''births the future''' rather than sealing the past. Feminist and erotic readings, following [[Carson]] and [[Lawrence]], read wounding as '''threshold intimacy''': surgery as caress, bleeding as consent, opening as communication. The [[Doctrine of Refusal]] holds that '''to heal this wound would end the cycle'''—which cannot, must not happen.
